🧲 Magnets and Magnetic Field

Magnets have been known for thousands of years — but their deep connection to electricity was only discovered in 1819. This lesson introduces magnets, magnetic poles, permanent magnets vs electromagnets, and the Earth's magnetic field.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Electromagnetism is a fundamental force combining electricity and magnetism. It was discovered accidentally in 1819 by Hans Christian Oersted, who found that an electric current deflected a nearby compass needle. This discovery changed the world — without it, we'd have no radios, TVs, computers, or electric motors.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define magnets and magnetic poles
  • Explain the two types of magnets
  • Describe the Earth's magnetic field
  • Compare electric and magnetic fields
  • Understand magnetic field intensity (Tesla unit)
📖
Explanation
Complete theory from the textbook

1.1 What is a Magnet?

A magnet generates a magnetic field — the region around it where magnetic force acts. Every magnet has two poles.

📌 Magnetic Poles

• North pole (N) and South pole (S)
• Like poles repel (N–N or S–S)
• Opposite poles attract (N–S)

Key fact: Magnetic poles are ALWAYS found in pairs. No matter how many times you cut a magnet, each piece will have both a north and a south pole. There is no such thing as an isolated magnetic pole (monopole).

1.2 Permanent Magnets vs Electromagnets

Permanent Magnet Electromagnet
Magnetic field from internal structure of the material itself Magnetic field created by electric current through a coil
Retains magnetism for a long time Loses magnetism when current is off
Examples: bar magnets, fridge magnets Examples: doorbells, cranes, motors, MRI
Cannot be turned off Can be turned on/off and adjusted
Made of materials like iron, nickel, cobalt alloys Usually a coil of wire wound around an iron core
📝 Iron core

An electromagnet's iron core increases the strength of the magnetic field created by the coil.

1.3 Earth's Magnetic Field

📌 The Earth as a Giant Magnet

The Earth behaves like a giant bar magnet inside it:
• The magnetic north pole corresponds to the South geographic pole
• The magnetic south pole corresponds to the North geographic pole

A compass needle aligns itself in a north–south direction because it lines up with Earth's magnetic field.

1.4 Differences Between Electric and Magnetic Fields

Property Electric Field Magnetic Field
SI unitN/C (Newton per Coulomb)Tesla (T)
SourceElectric chargeMagnetic dipole (N-S pole)
Field lines start/endStart on + charge, end on − chargeNo start or end — form closed loops
Isolated pole?Yes — single charges existNo — poles always in pairs
Field loops?Do not form loopsForm closed loops

1.5 Magnetic Field Intensity (B)

📌 Magnetic Field B

The magnetic field is a vector — it has both magnitude and direction.

SI unit: Tesla (T) — 1 T = 1 N/(A·m)
Older unit: Gauss (G) — 1 T = 10⁴ G

Reference values:
• Earth's surface field: ~0.5 × 10⁻⁴ T (0.5 G)
• Bar magnet surface: ~10⁻² T
• Medical MRI: ~1.5 T
• Strong superconducting lab magnet: ~30 T

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Identifying Magnetic Poles
Problem: A bar magnet is cut into three pieces. How many poles does each piece have?
Physics
Magnetic poles always come in pairs — there is no isolated magnetic pole.
Result
Each of the three pieces becomes a complete small magnet with its own N and S pole.
✅ Each piece has 2 poles (N and S) — 6 poles in total.
Example 2Compass Direction
Problem: Why does a compass needle point north?
Earth's field
The Earth has a magnetic field similar to a giant bar magnet.
Alignment
The compass needle is a small magnet — it aligns with Earth's field.
Direction
The N pole of the needle is attracted to Earth's magnetic south pole, which is near the geographic North pole.
✅ Compasses point north because of the Earth's magnetic field.
Example 3Permanent vs Electromagnet
Problem: A scrapyard crane needs to pick up and release metal objects. Should it use a permanent magnet or an electromagnet?
Permanent
Cannot be turned off — would not release objects.
Electromagnet
Can be turned on/off by controlling current — perfect for picking and releasing.
✅ Use an electromagnet — it can be switched on and off.
Example 4Field Direction from Unit
Problem: Convert 5000 gauss to tesla.
Formula
1 T = 10⁴ G
Solve
5000 G = 5000 / 10⁴ = 0.5 T
✅ 5000 gauss = 0.5 T
Example 5Electromagnet Design
Problem: Explain why an electromagnet has an iron core inside its coil.
Function
The iron core concentrates and amplifies the magnetic field.
Physics
Iron is a ferromagnetic material — its atoms align with the coil's field, adding to it.
✅ The iron core multiplies the field strength, making the electromagnet much stronger.

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 4.1
✅ Key Points — Magnets and Magnetic Field
  • Electromagnetism = unified force of electricity + magnetism.
  • Every magnet has two poles: N and S — like poles repel, opposite poles attract.
  • Magnetic poles are always found in pairs — cutting a magnet creates two smaller magnets.
  • Permanent magnets: retain magnetism; electromagnets: use electric current, can be turned off.
  • Electromagnets use an iron core to boost field strength.
  • Earth behaves like a giant bar magnet — magnetic poles are opposite to geographic poles.
  • SI unit of magnetic field: Tesla (T); 1 T = 10⁴ G.
  • Unlike electric field lines, magnetic field lines form closed loops.
  • Field strengths: Earth ~0.5 × 10⁻⁴ T, MRI ~1.5 T, superconducting lab magnet ~30 T.
