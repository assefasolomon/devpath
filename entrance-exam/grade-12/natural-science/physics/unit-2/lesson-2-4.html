🪐 Planetary Motion and Kepler's Laws

How do planets move? Learn how Kepler's three laws describe the motion of planets around the Sun — and how Newton later explained them with gravity.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources

Introduction
What you will learn

Planets orbit the Sun in predictable patterns. For centuries, humans thought Earth was the centre of the universe (geocentric model). In 1543, Copernicus proposed the Sun-centred (heliocentric) model. Then Kepler, using Tycho Brahe's data, discovered three laws that describe planetary motion.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • State and apply Kepler's three laws
  • Explain the concepts of perihelion and aphelion
  • Use T² ∝ R³ to find orbital periods
  • Compare ellipses vs circles in orbits
  • Describe the historical development of planetary models
📖
Explanation
Complete theory from the textbook

4.1 Historical Background

📌 Timeline of Discovery

• Ptolemy (c. 100–170 AD) — elaborated geocentric model
• Copernicus (1473–1543) — proposed heliocentric model
• Tycho Brahe (1546–1601) — precise astronomical observations
• Kepler (1571–1630) — analyzed Brahe's data, discovered three laws
• Newton (1642–1727) — explained WHY Kepler's laws work (using gravity)

4.2 Kepler's First Law — Law of Ellipses

📌 Kepler's First Law

All planets move in elliptical orbits, with the Sun at one focus.

An ellipse is a closed curve where the sum of the distances from any point on the curve to the two foci (f₁ and f₂) is constant. This means the planet–Sun distance is constantly changing as the planet orbits.

4.3 Kepler's Second Law — Law of Equal Areas

📌 Kepler's Second Law

An imaginary line drawn from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal time intervals.

This means planets do not move at constant speed. They speed up when closer to the Sun and slow down when farther away.

• Perihelion — point of nearest approach (fastest)
• Aphelion — point of greatest separation (slowest)

4.4 Kepler's Third Law — Law of Harmonics

📌 Kepler's Third Law

The square of the orbital period (T) is proportional to the cube of the average orbital radius (R):

T² / R³ = K

The constant K is nearly the same for all planets. This equation works for both circular and elliptical orbits.

📝 Sample comparison

Earth: T = 3.156 × 10⁷ s, R = 1.4957 × 10¹¹ m → T²/R³ ≈ 2.977 × 10⁻¹⁹
Mars: T = 5.93 × 10⁷ s, R = 2.278 × 10¹¹ m → T²/R³ ≈ 2.975 × 10⁻¹⁹

The ratios match! The same holds for all planets.

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Pluto's Orbital Period
Problem: Earth's orbital period = 365 days, mean distance = 1.495 × 10⁸ km. Pluto's mean distance = 5.896 × 10⁹ km. Find Pluto's orbital period.
Formula
(T_E)² / (R_E)³ = (T_P)² / (R_P)³
Substitute
(365)² / (1.495×10⁸)³ = (T_P)² / (5.896×10⁹)³
Solve
T_P ≈ 9.0 × 10⁴ days (about 248 Earth years)
✅ Pluto's period ≈ 9.0 × 10⁴ days
Example 2Saturn's Year
Problem: Saturn is on average 9 times farther from the Sun than Earth. How long is its year in Earth years?
Given
R_S = 9 R_E, T_E = 1 year
Formula
(T_E)² / (R_E)³ = (T_S)² / (R_S)³
Substitute
1 / (R_E)³ = (T_S)² / (9 R_E)³ = (T_S)² / 729 (R_E)³
Solve
(T_S)² = 729 → T_S = 27 years
✅ Saturn's year = 27 Earth years
Example 3True or False — Speed Variation
Problem: According to Kepler's laws, a satellite increases its speed as it approaches the Sun and decreases as it moves away. True or false?
Reason
Kepler's second law — equal areas in equal times.
Explain
Closer to Sun → smaller radius → must move faster to sweep the same area in the same time.
✅ True — speed increases closer to the Sun (perihelion) and decreases further away (aphelion).
Example 4Doubling the Orbit Radius
Problem: If the radius of a circular orbit is doubled, how does the period change?
Formula
T² ∝ R³
Ratio
(T_new / T_old)² = (R_new / R_old)³ = (2)³ = 8
Solve
T_new / T_old = √8 = 2√2 ≈ 2.83
✅ The period increases by a factor of 2√2
Example 5Ellipse vs Circle
Problem: Explain the difference between a circle and an ellipse in terms of foci.
Circle
Both foci coincide at the centre. Every point is equidistant from the centre.
Ellipse
Two distinct foci. The sum of distances from the two foci to any point on the ellipse is constant.
Orbit
A circle is a special ellipse where the two foci merge.
✅ Circle: one focus. Ellipse: two foci, constant sum of distances.

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 2.4
✅ Key Points — Kepler's Laws
  • Historical shift from geocentric to heliocentric model of the solar system.
  • Kepler's 1st Law — planets orbit the Sun in ellipses with the Sun at one focus.
  • Kepler's 2nd Law — a line from Sun to planet sweeps equal areas in equal times; planets move faster at perihelion, slower at aphelion.
  • Kepler's 3rd Law — T² ∝ R³ for all planets orbiting the same central body.
  • The constant K = 4π²/(GM_s) is the same for all planets in the solar system.
  • Perihelion = closest point (fastest speed); aphelion = farthest point (slowest speed).
  • Kepler's laws apply to any orbiting system — planets around the Sun, moons around planets, satellites around Earth.
  • Newton later explained why Kepler's laws hold — using gravity.
