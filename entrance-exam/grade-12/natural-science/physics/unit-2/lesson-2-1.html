Unit 2 · Two Dimensional Motion · Natural Science

🎯 Projectile Motion

When you throw a ball, kick a football, or fire a bullet, the object follows a curved path called a trajectory. Learn how to analyze this motion by separating it into independent horizontal and vertical components.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

A projectile is any object in flight after being thrown or projected. Examples: a football kicked in a game, a cannonball fired from a cannon, a bullet fired from a gun, the flight of a golf ball, water from a hose. The curved path is called the trajectory.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Analyze horizontal projection problems
  • Analyze inclined projection problems
  • Derive and use trajectory equations
  • Calculate range, maximum height, and time of flight
  • Apply the concept of independence of x and y motion
📖
Explanation
Complete theory from the textbook

1.1 Assumptions and Key Idea

Projectile motion is simple to analyze if we make two assumptions:

📌 The Two Assumptions

1. Free-fall acceleration is constant — directed downward with magnitude g (≈9.8 or 10 m/s²)
2. Air resistance is negligible

📝 The Key Idea

The horizontal and vertical components of projectile motion are completely independent of each other. They can be handled separately, with time t as the common variable for both.

1.2 Horizontal Projection

The projectile is projected horizontally from a certain height. Initial vertical velocity is zero — only horizontal velocity at the start.

📌 Horizontal Projection — Equations

Horizontal (constant velocity):
v_x = v_0x (unchanged)
Δx = v_0x · t

Vertical (constant acceleration g):
v_y = g·t (starts from 0)
Δy = ½ g t²

Time of flight: t = √(2Δy/g)
Range: R = v_0x · √(2Δy/g)

1.3 Inclined Projectile Motion

An object is projected with initial velocity v₀ at an angle θ with the horizontal. The velocity has two components:

📌 Initial Velocity Components

v_0x = v_0 cos θ (constant throughout flight)
v_0y = v_0 sin θ (changes with time)

📌 Displacements at any time t

Horizontal: Δx = v_0 cos θ · t
Vertical: Δy = v_0 sin θ · t + ½ g t²

1.4 Range and Maximum Height

For a projectile launched and landing at the same height:

📌 Key Formulas

Time to max height: t = v_0 sin θ / g

Total time of flight: t_total = 2 v_0 sin θ / g

Maximum height: H = v_0² sin²θ / (2g)

Range: R = v_0² sin(2θ) / g

Range–height relation: H = R tan θ / 4

📝 Optimal angle

The range is maximum at θ = 45° (when air resistance is neglected). Two angles that sum to 90° give the same range — the lower angle reaches a lower apex, the higher angle reaches a higher apex, but both travel the same horizontal distance.

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Bullet Fired Horizontally (from textbook)
Problem: A rifle is aimed horizontally at a target 30 m away. The bullet hits the target 2 cm below the aiming point. (a) What is the bullet's time of flight? (b) What is the initial velocity of the bullet?
Given
Δx = 30 m, Δy = 2 cm = 0.02 m (downward)
Part a
Δy = ½ g t²
0.02 = ½ (10) t² → t² = 0.004 → t = 0.06 s
Part b
v_0x = Δx / t = 30 / 0.06 = 500 m/s
✅ (a) t = 0.06 s   (b) v₀ = 500 m/s
Example 2Rescue Airplane Drop
Problem: A rescue airplane travelling at 360 km/h (100 m/s) horizontally drops a food package from a height of 300 m. (a) How long will the package take to reach the ground? (b) How far from the car driver should it be dropped?
Given
v₀ = 100 m/s (horizontal), Δy = 300 m
Part a
Δy = ½ g t²
300 = ½ (10) t² → t² = 60 → t ≈ 7.74 s
Part b
Δx = v_0x · t = 100 × 7.74 = 774 m
✅ (a) t = 7.74 s   (b) drop 774 m before the driver
Example 3Football Kick — Range and Max Height
Problem: A football is kicked at 37° with the horizontal. Initial velocity = 40 m/s. Find (a) maximum height and (b) horizontal range.
Given
v₀ = 40 m/s, θ = 37°, g = 10 m/s², sin 37° = 0.6, cos 37° = 0.8
Max height
H = v_0² sin²θ / (2g) = (40² × 0.6²) / (2 × 10) = (1600 × 0.36) / 20 = 28.8 m
Range
R = v_0² sin 2θ / g = (40² × sin 74°) / 10 = (1600 × 0.96) / 10 = 153.6 m
✅ (a) H = 28.8 m   (b) R ≈ 154 m
Example 4Ball Thrown at a Wall
Problem: You throw a ball with a speed of 25 m/s at an angle of 53° above the horizontal directly toward a wall 24 m away. (a) How long does the ball take to reach the wall? (b) How far above the release point does it hit the wall?
Given
Δx = 24 m, θ = 53°, v₀ = 25 m/s, cos 53° = 0.6, sin 53° = 0.8
Part a
Δx = v_0 cos θ · t
24 = 25 × 0.6 × t → t = 1.6 s
Part b
Δy = v_0 sin θ · t + ½ g t²
Δy = 25 × 0.8 × 1.6 + ½ (−10)(1.6)²
Δy = 32 − 12.8 = 19.2 m
✅ (a) t = 1.6 s   (b) hits the wall 19.2 m above release point
Example 5Range Equals 3× Maximum Height
Problem: A projectile is fired so that its horizontal range equals 3× its maximum height. What is the angle of projection?
Relation
H = R tan θ / 4 → R = 4H / tan θ
Given
R = 3H → 3H = 4H / tan θ
Solve
tan θ = 4/3 → θ = 53°
✅ Angle of projection = 53°

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 2.1
✅ Key Points — Projectile Motion
  • A projectile is an object in flight after being thrown or projected; its path is a parabola.
  • Two key assumptions: constant downward acceleration g and negligible air resistance.
  • Horizontal and vertical motions are independent — analyze them separately with time t as the common variable.
  • Horizontal velocity v_x = v_0 cos θ is constant throughout the flight.
  • Vertical velocity changes due to gravity: v_y = v_0 sin θ + g t.
  • Maximum height: H = v_0² sin²θ / (2g).
  • Range: R = v_0² sin(2θ) / g — maximum at θ = 45°.
  • Angles summing to 90° give the same range.
  • For horizontal projection: t = √(2Δy/g) and R = v_0x · t.
