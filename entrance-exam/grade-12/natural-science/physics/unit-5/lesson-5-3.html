🔄 Rectification

Rectification is the process of converting AC to DC — the first step in every power supply. Learn how diodes achieve this with half-wave and full-wave circuits, and how capacitors smooth the output.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources

Introduction
What you will learn

Wall outlets supply alternating current (AC), but most electronics need direct current (DC). The process of converting AC to DC is called rectification — done using diodes.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define rectification and its purpose
  • Explain the half-wave rectifier
  • Explain the full-wave rectifier (bridge circuit)
  • Describe the role of a capacitor in smoothing
  • Recognize other diode applications: LED, photodiode, logic gates
📖
Explanation
Complete theory from the textbook

3.1 What is Rectification?

📌 Definition

Rectification is the process of converting an AC voltage into a unidirectional (DC) voltage. The circuit that performs this is called a rectifier.

Because a diode conducts in only one direction, it's the perfect component for this task — it allows current to flow in one half-cycle and blocks it in the other.

3.2 Half-Wave Rectifier

A single diode blocks one half-cycle of the AC wave, allowing only the other half to pass.

Half-Wave Rectification
The diode passes only the positive half-cycle. The output is pulsating DC — not smooth yet.
📌 Half-Wave Rectifier — Pros and Cons

✓ Simple — only one diode needed
✓ Cheap

✗ Only ~50% of input power is converted to DC
✗ Output is very pulsating — hard to filter

3.3 Full-Wave Bridge Rectifier

A full-wave rectifier uses four diodes arranged in a bridge. Both halves of the AC cycle contribute to the output.

Full-Wave Bridge Rectifier
During the positive half-cycle, diodes D₁ and D₂ conduct. During the negative half-cycle, D₃ and D₄ conduct. Current always flows the same direction through the load.
📌 Full-Wave vs Half-Wave

✓ Double the output — both halves of the wave are used
✓ Smoother DC — easier to filter
✓ Better efficiency

✗ Needs four diodes (or a center-tapped transformer with two)

3.4 Smoothing with a Capacitor

📌 How Capacitors Smooth DC

A capacitor connected across the output:
• Charges during the peak of the rectified voltage
• Discharges when the voltage falls
• Fills in the gaps → smooths the pulsating DC

Result: nearly steady DC voltage for powering electronics.

3.5 Other Diode Applications

📌 LED (Light Emitting Diode)

A PN junction diode that emits light when current flows through it in the forward direction. Electrons and holes recombine and release energy as photons.

• Very efficient — produces light with little heat
• Color depends on semiconductor material
• Used in indicators, displays, lighting

📌 Photodiode

A diode that converts light into electrical current. Photons absorbed in the depletion region create electron-hole pairs → current flows.

Used in: light sensors, solar cells, optical communication, remote controls.

📌 Other Uses of Diodes

• Logic gates — AND, OR circuits using diodes
• Over-voltage protection — diodes shut down converters if voltage spikes
• Voltage clamping — Zener diodes hold a steady voltage

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Half-Wave Output
Problem: An AC signal of peak voltage 10 V passes through a half-wave rectifier. What is the peak output voltage?
Physics
The diode conducts during the positive half-cycle only.
Voltage drop
Silicon diode drops ~0.7 V when conducting.
Result
V_out = 10 − 0.7 = 9.3 V
✅ Peak output ≈ 9.3 V
Example 2Full-Wave Advantage
Problem: Why does a full-wave rectifier produce smoother output than a half-wave rectifier?
Half-wave
Only half of the AC wave is used — large gaps between pulses.
Full-wave
Both halves are used — pulses occur twice as often.
Result
More pulses per second = less time between pulses = easier to smooth.
✅ Full-wave uses both halves → twice the pulse frequency → smoother output.
Example 3Capacitor Function
Problem: What is the role of a capacitor in a rectifier circuit?
Charge
Capacitor charges when rectified voltage is rising (near peak).
Discharge
It discharges when voltage drops — filling the "valleys" in the waveform.
✅ The capacitor smooths the pulsating DC into near-constant DC.
Example 4LED vs Ordinary Diode
Problem: What's the difference between an ordinary diode and an LED?
Ordinary diode
Made of silicon or germanium. Emits no light when current flows.
LED
Made of special semiconductors (GaAs, GaP). When current flows forward, photons are emitted.
Result
LED = diode that produces light. Ordinary diode = just conducts.
✅ LEDs emit light when forward-biased; ordinary diodes don't.
Example 5Photodiode vs LED
Problem: Explain the difference between a photodiode and an LED.
LED
Electricity → light (light emitter).
Photodiode
Light → electricity (light detector).
Applications
LEDs: displays, indicators. Photodiodes: sensors, solar cells, optical receivers.
✅ LED emits light; photodiode detects light. Opposite functions.

Everything you learned in Lesson 5.3
✅ Key Points — Rectification
  • Rectification = converting AC to DC using diodes.
  • Half-wave rectifier: 1 diode, uses only one half-cycle.
  • Full-wave bridge rectifier: 4 diodes, uses both half-cycles.
  • Full-wave produces twice the pulse frequency — smoother output.
  • Silicon diode drops ~0.7 V when conducting.
  • Capacitor across output smooths pulsating DC.
  • LED — emits light when forward-biased.
  • Photodiode — converts light to current.
  • Diodes also used in logic gates, protection circuits, voltage clamps.
