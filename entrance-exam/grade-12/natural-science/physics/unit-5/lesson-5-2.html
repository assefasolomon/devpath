➡️ Diodes and Their Functions

A diode is a one-way valve for electric current. Learn how the P-N junction works, what forward and reverse bias mean, and why diodes are the foundation of all modern electronics.

Introduction
What you will learn

A diode is a two-terminal electronic component that conducts current in one direction only. It's used in nearly every electronic circuit — as a rectifier, a switch, a voltage reference, and more.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe the P-N junction diode
  • Explain forward bias and reverse bias
  • Read I-V characteristics of a diode
  • Recognize the diode schematic symbol
  • Identify the barrier potential for silicon and germanium

Explanation
Complete theory from the textbook

2.1 What is a Diode?

📌 Definition

A diode is a two-terminal electronic component that conducts current in one direction and blocks it in the opposite direction.

• Terminal 1: Anode (positive, connected to P-type)
• Terminal 2: Cathode (negative, connected to N-type)
• Materials: silicon, germanium, gallium arsenide

2.2 The P-N Junction

When P-type and N-type semiconductors are joined, a P-N junction forms — the key structure of every diode.

P-N Junction Diode Structure
P-type has holes; N-type has electrons. The depletion region forms a barrier potential that must be overcome for current to flow.
📌 Barrier Potential

The electric field created by ions in the depletion region prevents further diffusion — it's the barrier potential.

• Silicon: ~0.7 V
• Germanium: ~0.3 V

2.3 Forward and Reverse Bias

📌 Forward Bias

Battery + terminal to P side, − terminal to N side.

• Depletion region narrows
• Barrier potential is reduced
• Current flows freely
• Diode behaves like a closed switch

📌 Reverse Bias

Battery − terminal to P side, + terminal to N side.

• Depletion region widens
• Barrier potential is increased
• Only a tiny leakage current flows
• Diode behaves like an open switch

Forward vs Reverse Bias
Current flows easily in forward bias; blocked in reverse bias. Arrow points from anode to cathode.

2.4 I-V Characteristics

📌 Reading the I-V Curve

Forward region: Current increases slowly until barrier voltage is exceeded, then rises sharply.

Reverse region: Only small leakage current flows due to minority carriers.

Breakdown voltage: At a certain reverse voltage, current increases sharply — the diode may be destroyed (unless it's a Zener diode).

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Identify the Bias
Problem: A diode has its anode connected to the positive terminal of a 5 V battery and its cathode to the negative terminal. Is the diode forward or reverse biased? Will current flow?
Rule
Forward bias: + to anode, − to cathode.
Check
Battery 5 V > silicon barrier 0.7 V → forward bias condition met.
✅ Forward biased — current will flow.
Example 2Reverse Bias Condition
Problem: A diode has its anode connected to the negative terminal of a 12 V battery. Is it forward or reverse biased?
Rule
Reverse bias: − to anode (P side), + to cathode (N side).
Result
Reverse bias → depletion region widens → only tiny leakage current.
✅ Reverse biased — essentially no current flows.
Example 3Voltage Across a Diode
Problem: A silicon diode is forward biased with a 5 V source and a 470 Ω resistor in series. Estimate the voltage across the diode and the current.
Diode drop
Silicon diode has forward drop of ~0.7 V.
Resistor drop
V_R = 5 − 0.7 = 4.3 V
Current
I = V_R / R = 4.3 / 470 ≈ 9.1 mA
✅ V_diode ≈ 0.7 V   I ≈ 9.1 mA
Example 4Choosing Silicon vs Germanium
Problem: Why would you prefer a germanium diode over a silicon diode in a small-signal application?
Compare
Germanium barrier: 0.3 V. Silicon barrier: 0.7 V.
Advantage
Germanium has a lower forward voltage drop — better for small signals where a 0.7 V loss would be significant.
✅ Germanium — lower voltage drop (0.3 V vs 0.7 V)
Example 5Identify the Symbol
Problem: In a diode symbol, which direction does the triangle point?
Convention
The triangle represents the arrow of conventional current flow.
Direction
The triangle points from anode to cathode.
✅ The triangle points from Anode to Cathode (direction of forward current).

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 5.2
✅ Key Points — Diodes and Their Functions
  • A diode conducts current in one direction only.
  • Two terminals: anode (P side) and cathode (N side).
  • The P-N junction forms a depletion region with barrier potential.
  • Barrier potential: ~0.7 V for silicon, ~0.3 V for germanium.
  • Forward bias (+ to P, − to N) → depletion narrows → current flows.
  • Reverse bias (− to P, + to N) → depletion widens → no current (tiny leakage).
  • The diode symbol's arrow points anode → cathode.
  • Applications: rectifiers, protection circuits, voltage references, LED, photodiodes.
