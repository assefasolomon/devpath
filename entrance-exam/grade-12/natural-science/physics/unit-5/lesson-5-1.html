🔬 Semiconductors

Semiconductors sit between conductors and insulators — and they power every electronic device in modern life. Learn how silicon is doped to create N-type and P-type materials that make diodes, transistors, and computer chips possible.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked

Introduction
What you will learn

Electronic devices — calculators, phones, laptops — are built from semiconductor materials. Understanding how semiconductors behave at the atomic level explains how diodes, transistors, and integrated circuits work.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Compare conductors, insulators, and semiconductors
  • Describe the lattice structure of silicon
  • Explain the concepts of holes and free electrons
  • Distinguish intrinsic vs extrinsic semiconductors
  • Describe the doping process for N-type and P-type

Explanation
Complete theory from the textbook

1.1 Conductors, Insulators, Semiconductors

📌 Three Categories of Materials

Conductors — allow electricity to flow easily. Metals like copper and aluminum. They have free electrons.

Insulators — do NOT allow current to flow. Every electron is tightly bound to its parent atom. Plastic, wood, glass, rubber.

Semiconductors — conductivity is between conductors and insulators. Pure elements like silicon (Si) and germanium (Ge) or compounds like gallium arsenide.

📝 Temperature behaviour

Semiconductors act as insulators at absolute zero (0 K) and conductors at higher temperatures. Heat gives electrons enough energy to break free from covalent bonds.

1.2 Lattice Structure of Silicon

Silicon Atom and Covalent Bonding
Each silicon atom forms four covalent bonds with neighboring atoms, giving 8 shared electrons.
📌 Holes and Free Electrons

• Hole — an absence of an electron in the crystal lattice. Acts as a positive charge carrier.
• Free electron — an electron that has broken from its bond and can move freely.
• In pure silicon, the number of holes equals the number of free electrons.

1.3 Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Semiconductors

📌 Intrinsic Semiconductor

A pure semiconductor with no impurities added. Also called undoped semiconductor. Silicon and germanium are common examples. Conductivity is very low at room temperature.

📌 Extrinsic Semiconductor

A semiconductor that has had controlled impurities added (doping) to improve conductivity. Classified into N-type and P-type based on the dopant.

📌 Doping

The process of intentionally adding small amounts of impurities (called dopants) to a pure semiconductor to improve its conductivity.

Purpose: create either a surplus of electrons or a deficiency of electrons (holes).

1.4 N-Type and P-Type Semiconductors

⚡ N-Type (Negative)
  • Doped with Group V elements (5 valence electrons)
  • Examples: phosphorus, arsenic, antimony, bismuth
  • Extra electrons are free to move
  • Majority carriers: electrons
  • Minority carriers: holes
  • Dopants are called donor atoms
✨ P-Type (Positive)
  • Doped with Group III elements (3 valence electrons)
  • Examples: boron, aluminum, gallium, indium
  • Missing bonds create holes
  • Majority carriers: holes
  • Minority carriers: electrons
  • Dopants are called acceptor atoms

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Classify the Material
Problem: Classify each as conductor, semiconductor, or insulator: (a) copper, (b) silicon, (c) glass, (d) germanium, (e) rubber.
Analyze
• Copper — metal → conductor
• Silicon — pure element with 4 valence e⁻ → semiconductor
• Glass — tightly bound electrons → insulator
• Germanium — 4 valence e⁻ → semiconductor
• Rubber — no free electrons → insulator
✅ (a) conductor, (b) semiconductor, (c) insulator, (d) semiconductor, (e) insulator
Example 2Determine Dopant Type
Problem: A silicon crystal is doped with phosphorus. What type of semiconductor is formed, and why?
Group
Phosphorus is in Group V — has 5 valence electrons.
Bonding
4 of its electrons bond with 4 silicon neighbors. The 5th electron is free to move.
Result
Surplus of electrons → N-type semiconductor. Phosphorus is a donor atom.
✅ N-type — phosphorus donates a free electron.
Example 3Identify P-type Dopant
Problem: Which of these elements would make a good P-type dopant for silicon? (a) Gallium, (b) Arsenic, (c) Indium, (d) Antimony.
Rule
P-type needs Group III element (3 valence electrons).
Check each
• Gallium — Group III ✓
• Arsenic — Group V ✗ (would give N-type)
• Indium — Group III ✓
• Antimony — Group V ✗ (would give N-type)
✅ (a) Gallium and (c) Indium
Example 4Majority Carriers
Problem: For a silicon crystal doped with boron, what are the majority and minority charge carriers?
Group
Boron is in Group III — 3 valence electrons.
Result
Missing bond → hole created. Holes become majority carriers.
✅ Majority: holes   Minority: electrons   (P-type)
Example 5Temperature Effect
Problem: Why does a semiconductor's conductivity increase with temperature?
At low T
Electrons stay bound in covalent bonds → few free carriers → low conductivity.
At high T
Thermal energy breaks bonds → releases more free electrons and holes → conductivity rises.
✅ Higher temperature releases more charge carriers → higher conductivity.

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 5.1
✅ Key Points — Semiconductors
  • Three material types: conductors, insulators, semiconductors.
  • Semiconductors have conductivity between metals and insulators.
  • Silicon and germanium are common semiconductors (4 valence electrons each).
  • A hole is a missing electron in a bond — acts like a positive charge.
  • Intrinsic = pure; extrinsic = doped with impurities.
  • Doping adds small amounts of impurities to change conductivity.
  • N-type: doped with Group V (5 valence e⁻) → electrons are majority carriers.
  • P-type: doped with Group III (3 valence e⁻) → holes are majority carriers.
  • Higher temperature → more carriers released → higher conductivity.
