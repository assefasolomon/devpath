🔊 Transistors and Their Application

The transistor is the building block of modern electronics. It can amplify signals and switch currents — and it's found in every computer, phone, and radio.

Introduction
What you will learn

A transistor is a three-terminal, two-junction semiconductor device used to control electron flow. By varying a small voltage, a tiny current can control a much larger current — this is the essence of amplification and switching.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe the structure of Bipolar Junction Transistors (BJTs)
  • Distinguish NPN and PNP transistors
  • Draw the transistor schematic symbols
  • Explain the three transistor configurations
  • Calculate current gain (β) and emitter current

Explanation
Complete theory from the textbook

4.1 What is a Transistor?

📌 Definition

A transistor is a three-terminal, two-junction semiconductor device used to control electron flow. Two main types:

• BJT — Bipolar Junction Transistor
• FET — Field Effect Transistor

This lesson focuses on BJTs.

📝 Three alternately doped regions

A BJT consists of three alternately doped semiconductor regions arranged in one of two ways: NPN or PNP.

4.2 NPN vs PNP Structure

NPN and PNP Transistor Structures
In NPN, the base is P-type (thin and lightly doped). In PNP, the base is N-type. The emitter is always heavily doped.
⚡ NPN Transistor
  • P-type base between two N-type regions
  • Majority carriers: electrons
  • Current direction: Collector → Emitter
  • Arrow on emitter points outward
  • Emitter connected to − terminal
✨ PNP Transistor
  • N-type base between two P-type regions
  • Majority carriers: holes
  • Current direction: Emitter → Collector
  • Arrow on emitter points inward
  • Emitter connected to + terminal

4.3 Transistor Terminals — E, B, C

📌 The Three Regions

Emitter (E): Heavily doped region that supplies charge carriers.

Base (B): Middle region, lightly doped and very thin. Controls the transistor action.

Collector (C): Moderately doped region that collects charge carriers.

4.4 Biasing a Transistor

📌 Correct Biasing

For proper operation:
• Emitter-Base junction → forward biased
• Collector-Base junction → reverse biased

In NPN, emitter is connected to negative terminal and base to positive (forward bias). Collector is connected to a more positive voltage (reverse bias) to attract electrons.

4.5 Current Relationships

📌 Current Conservation

I_E = I_B + I_C

The emitter current equals the sum of base and collector currents.

The base current is very small (microamps), but it controls the much larger collector current (milliamps).

📌 Current Gain (β)

β = I_C / I_B

A small change in base current produces a β times larger change in collector current — this is amplification.

4.6 Transistor Configurations

📌 Three Ways to Connect

Common Emitter (CE): Input B-E, output C-E. Provides voltage, current, AND power gain. Most commonly used.

Common Collector (CC): Input B-C, output E-C. Provides good current gain.

Common Base (CB): Input E, output C. Provides good voltage and power gain.

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Collector Current from β
Problem: A transistor has β = 250 and base current I_B = 20 μA. What is the collector current I_C?
Formula
I_C = β I_B
Substitute
I_C = 250 × 20 μA = 5000 μA = 5 mA
✅ I_C = 5 mA
Example 2Emitter Current
Problem: A transistor has I_B = 30 μA and I_C = 4.5 mA. Find I_E and β.
Convert
I_C = 4.5 mA = 4500 μA
I_E
I_E = I_B + I_C = 30 + 4500 = 4530 μA = 4.53 mA
β
β = I_C / I_B = 4500 / 30 = 150
✅ I_E = 4.53 mA   β = 150
Example 3Base Current Calculation
Problem: If a transistor has β = 100 and collector current 10 mA, what is I_E?
Base current
I_B = I_C / β = 10 mA / 100 = 0.1 mA = 100 μA
Emitter
I_E = I_B + I_C = 0.1 + 10 = 10.1 mA
✅ I_E ≈ 10.1 mA
Example 4Identify the Transistor
Problem: A transistor has its emitter arrow pointing outward, away from the base. What type of transistor is it?
Convention
In circuit symbols, the arrow on the emitter shows the direction of conventional current.
Direction
Arrow pointing outward = current flows out of emitter = NPN.
✅ NPN transistor
Example 5Why Common Emitter?
Problem: Why is the common emitter configuration used most often?
Compare
CE provides voltage gain AND current gain simultaneously.
Result
It gives the highest power gain of the three configurations — best for amplifiers.
✅ CE is most used because it gives both voltage and current gain.

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 5.4
✅ Key Points — Transistors
  • A transistor is a three-terminal, two-junction device used to control current.
  • Two types: NPN and PNP bipolar junction transistors.
  • Three terminals: Emitter (E), Base (B), Collector (C).
  • Base is lightly doped and very thin — it controls the transistor.
  • Emitter is heavily doped — supplies charge carriers.
  • Collector is moderately doped — collects charge carriers.
  • Correct biasing: E-B forward, C-B reverse.
  • Current relations: I_E = I_B + I_C and β = I_C / I_B.
  • Three configurations: CE, CC, CB. Common emitter is most used — provides both voltage and current gain.
  • Transistors are used in amplifiers, switches, oscillators, and digital circuits.
