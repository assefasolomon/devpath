Unit 1 · Application of Physics · Natural Science

🔭 Relation of Physics and Astronomy

Astronomy is applied physics on a cosmic scale. Learn how astronomers use Newton's laws, electromagnetic waves, and atomic physics to study objects millions of light-years away — without ever touching them.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions

Introduction
What you will learn in this lesson

Astrophysics is the study of the physics of heavenly objects — the solar system, stars, galaxies, interstellar gas, and the universe as a whole. Astronomers can't visit these objects, so they use light and the physics of electromagnetic radiation to understand them.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Explain how Newton's laws describe planetary motion
  • Describe how the electromagnetic spectrum is used in astronomy
  • Identify types of telescopes and what each detects
  • Understand how atomic physics reveals compositions and temperatures of stars
  • Apply the inverse square law to determine distances to stars

Explanation
Complete theory from the textbook

4.1 Newton's Laws and Astronomy

Newton's laws of motion and gravity explain why Kepler's laws work. They describe:

📌 What Newton's Laws Explain

• The motion of the Moon around a planet
• The motion of planets around the Sun
• What keeps objects in orbit — the balance of gravity and inertia
• Artificial satellites in Earth orbit
• Centripetal force — gravity provides it

4.2 The Electromagnetic Spectrum and Telescopes

Astronomers study distant objects through the electromagnetic waves they emit. Different wavelengths reveal different information.

📌 Types of Telescopes

• Radio telescopes — detect radio waves
• Infrared telescopes — detect heat radiation
• Optical (visible) telescopes — detect visible light
• X-ray / gamma-ray telescopes — detect high-energy radiation

Each telescope detects only one part of the spectrum. Computers convert invisible data into false-color images we can see.

4.3 Physics of Atoms and Astronomy

When electrons in atoms jump between energy levels, they emit or absorb photons of specific wavelengths. This lets astronomers determine:

📌 What Spectral Lines Reveal

• Composition — which elements are present
• Temperature — from the pattern of emission
• Motion — from Doppler shifts of spectral lines
• Density — from line intensities

📝 Bohr atom transitions

When an electron drops from a higher orbit (n₂) to a lower orbit (n₁), a photon is emitted with energy E = hf = hc/λ. When an electron absorbs a photon, it jumps from a lower to a higher orbit. These transitions create the unique spectral fingerprints of each element.

4.4 The Inverse Square Law and Light Year

An object appears fainter the farther it is. This lets astronomers estimate distances.

📌 Inverse Square Law of Apparent Brightness

Apparent brightness ∝ True brightness / distance²

The true brightness is also called the object's luminosity. If we know an object's true brightness and measure its apparent brightness, we can calculate its distance.

📝 Light year

A light year is the distance light travels in one year.
1 ly = c × t = (3 × 10⁸ m/s) × (3.156 × 10⁷ s) ≈ 9.46 × 10¹² km

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems
Example 1Distance from Brightness Ratio
Problem: Star A appears 25 times brighter than identical Star B. How much closer is Star A?
Formula
Apparent brightness ∝ 1/d²
Ratio
B_A / B_B = (d_B / d_A)² → 25 = (d_B/d_A)²
Solve
d_B / d_A = √25 = 5 → d_A = d_B / 5
✅ Star A is 5 times closer than Star B.
Example 2Kepler's Third Law
Problem: Mars orbits the Sun at 1.52 AU. Using Earth's period (1 yr) and distance (1 AU), find Mars's period in Earth years.
Formula
T² ∝ R³ → (T_M/T_E)² = (R_M/R_E)³
Substitute
(T_M/1)² = (1.52/1)³ = 3.51
Solve
T_M = √3.51 = 1.87 years
✅ Mars's orbital period is about 1.87 Earth years.
Example 3Light Year Calculation
Problem: Calculate the distance light travels in one year (c = 3×10⁸ m/s).
Time
t = 365 × 24 × 3600 = 3.156 × 10⁷ s
Formula
d = c × t
Solve
d = 3×10⁸ × 3.156×10⁷ = 9.47 × 10¹⁵ m = 9.47 × 10¹² km
✅ 1 light year ≈ 9.46 × 10¹² km
Example 4Photon Energy from Transition
Problem: A hydrogen electron drops from n=2 to n=1, releasing a photon of wavelength 122 nm. Find its energy (h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J·s, c = 3×10⁸ m/s).
Formula
E = hc/λ
Convert
λ = 122 nm = 1.22 × 10⁻⁷ m
Solve
E = (6.63×10⁻³⁴ × 3×10⁸) / 1.22×10⁻⁷ = 1.63 × 10⁻¹⁸ J
✅ Photon energy = 1.63 × 10⁻¹⁸ J (about 10.2 eV)
Example 5Why Use Radio Telescopes?
Problem: Why do astronomers use radio telescopes in addition to optical telescopes?
Reason 1
Radio waves pass through clouds and dust that block visible light.
Reason 2
They can be used day and night — the Sun's light doesn't interfere.
Reason 3
They detect objects that emit little or no visible light (like cold gas clouds).
✅ Radio telescopes reveal what optical telescopes miss — hidden objects and cool matter.
