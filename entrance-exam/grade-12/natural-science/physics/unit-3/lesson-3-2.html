🔧 Properties of Pressure in Fluids

Pressure in a fluid is not constant — it changes with depth and transmits through the entire fluid. Learn Pascal's principle, the hydraulic lift, and how a simple tube of mercury can measure atmospheric pressure.

📖 Reading: ~22 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked

🎥 Videos: 4 resources

Introduction
What you will learn

When you squeeze one end of a water-filled tube, water shoots out of holes at the other end. When you dive deeper, pressure on your ears increases. Pressure in a fluid behaves in specific, predictable ways — and physics lets us harness it for incredible mechanical advantage.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • State and apply Pascal's law
  • Analyze a hydraulic lift for force amplification
  • Calculate pressure at a given depth (P = ρgh)
  • Explain how a barometer and manometer work
  • Understand atmospheric pressure variation with altitude
📖
Explanation
Complete theory from the textbook

2.1 Pascal's Law

Discovered by French scientist Blaise Pascal in 1653, Pascal's law explains why pressure transmits uniformly through an enclosed fluid.

📌 Pascal's Law

A change in pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to every point of the fluid and to the walls of its container.

At the microscopic level: fluid particles collide frequently, so an increase in pressure at one point quickly spreads throughout the fluid.

2.2 Hydraulic Lift

A practical application of Pascal's law — a force-multiplying machine.

📌 Hydraulic Lift Equations

Pressure in fluid under piston 1:
P₁ = F₁ / A₁

Since pressure is the same everywhere (Pascal's law):
F₁ / A₁ = F₂ / A₂
F₂ = F₁ × (A₂ / A₁)

Force multiplication occurs when A₂ > A₁. Small force on small piston → large force on large piston.

📝 Work conservation

The work done by both pistons is the same:
F₁ d₁ = F₂ d₂
Even though the force is amplified, the distance moved is proportionally smaller.

2.3 Pressure Variation with Depth

In a fluid at rest, pressure increases with depth because of the weight of the fluid above.

📌 Pressure at Depth h

P = P₀ + ρ g h

• P₀ = pressure at the surface (usually atmospheric)
• ρ = density of the fluid
• g = gravitational acceleration
• h = depth below the surface

Key result: Pressure at a given depth is the same regardless of the container's shape or size.

2.4 Measuring Pressure — Barometer and Manometer

📌 Barometer

Invented by Evangelista Torricelli (1643). A mercury-filled tube inverted into a mercury dish.

P_atm = ρ g h

At sea level, mercury column height is 760 mm — this defines 1 atm.

📌 Manometer

A U-shaped tube with liquid (usually mercury) used to measure gas pressure.

Closed-end: P_gas = ρgh
Open-end: P_gas = P_atm ± ρgh (depends on which arm has higher level)

2.5 Atmospheric Pressure vs Altitude

Atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude for two reasons:

📌 Why P decreases with altitude

1. Density decreases — air molecules pulled closer to Earth by gravity
2. Depth of atmosphere decreases — less air above you

Consequences:
• Water boils at lower temperature at altitude
• Breathing is harder
• Car engines produce less power
• Nosebleeds are common

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Hydraulic Lift Force
Problem: A hydraulic lift has a small piston (area 0.0020 m²) and a large piston (area 0.20 m²). A car of mass 1800 kg sits on the large piston. Find the minimum force needed on the small piston.
Force on large piston
F₂ = mg = 1800 × 9.8 = 17,640 N
Formula
F₁ = F₂ × (A₁ / A₂)
Substitute
F₁ = 17,640 × (0.0020 / 0.20) = 176 N
✅ Force ≈ 180 N — a small child could lift the car!
Example 2Pressure at Ocean Depth
Problem: Find the absolute pressure at 25 m below the ocean surface. (ρ_sea water = 1030 kg/m³, P_atm = 101.3 kPa)
Gauge pressure
ρgh = 1030 × 9.8 × 25 = 252,350 Pa = 252.35 kPa
Absolute
P_abs = P_atm + ρgh = 101.3 + 252.35 = 353.65 kPa
✅ Absolute pressure ≈ 354 kPa
Example 3Force on the Ocean Floor
Problem: A circular area of diameter 0.40 m at the bottom of the ocean (25 m deep). Find the force on it.
Area
A = π(0.20)² ≈ 0.1256 m²
Force
F = P_abs × A = 353,650 × 0.1256 ≈ 4.44 × 10⁴ N
✅ Force ≈ 4.44 × 10⁴ N (about 4.5 tonnes of weight)
Example 4Barometric Pressure
Problem: A barometer reads 740 mm Hg. Find the atmospheric pressure. (ρ_Hg = 13,595 kg/m³, g = 9.805 m/s²)
Formula
P_atm = ρ g h
Convert
h = 740 mm = 0.740 m
Substitute
P = 13,595 × 9.805 × 0.740 ≈ 98.6 kPa
✅ Atmospheric pressure ≈ 98.6 kPa
Example 5Open Manometer
Problem: An open manometer uses a fluid with SG = 0.85. The fluid column in the open arm is 55 cm above the gas-connected arm. P_atm = 96 kPa. Find absolute pressure in the tank.
Density
ρ = SG × ρ_water = 0.85 × 1000 = 850 kg/m³
Formula
P_gas = P_atm + ρgh
Substitute
P_gas = 96,000 + 850 × 9.81 × 0.55 ≈ 100,586 Pa ≈ 100.6 kPa
✅ Absolute pressure ≈ 100.6 kPa

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 3.2
✅ Key Points — Properties of Pressure in Fluids
  • Pascal's law: pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to every point.
  • Hydraulic lift amplifies force: F₂ = F₁ × (A₂ / A₁).
  • Work is conserved in a hydraulic lift: F₁ d₁ = F₂ d₂.
  • Pressure increases with depth: P = P₀ + ρgh.
  • Gauge pressure at depth h: P_gauge = ρgh.
  • Pressure at a given depth is the same regardless of container shape.
  • Barometer measures atmospheric pressure (P = ρgh).
  • Manometer measures gas pressure in a container.
  • Atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude due to lower density and shorter column of air.
  • Standard atmospheric pressure = 760 mm Hg = 101.3 kPa.
