∫ Definite Integrals

Understand what a definite integral means, apply the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to evaluate integrals exactly, and master the key properties that make computation fast on the entrance exam.

Introduction
What you will learn in this lesson

In Lesson 1 you learned that the indefinite integral (antiderivative) answers the question: "What function, when differentiated, gives f(x)?" The result was a family of functions with a constant C.

The definite integral answers a completely different question: "What is the exact area under the curve y = f(x) between two specific x-values?" The result is a single number, not a family of functions — and it has no constant C.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Interpret the meaning of ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx as a signed area under a curve
  • State and apply the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC) — both parts
  • Evaluate definite integrals using the "top minus bottom" rule: F(b) − F(a)
  • Apply the key properties: linearity, reversal, zero-width, splitting intervals
  • Handle definite integrals of polynomials, trig, and exponential functions
  • Avoid the most common exam mistakes with definite integrals

Explanation
Complete theory with visual aids
📌 Definition — Definite Integral

The definite integral of f(x) from a to b, written ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx, is the signed area between the curve y = f(x) and the x-axis, from x = a to x = b.

a = lower limit of integration  |  b = upper limit  |  f(x) = integrand  |  dx = variable of integration

2.1 Visualising the Definite Integral as Area

The definite integral measures signed area. Area above the x-axis counts as positive; area below the x-axis counts as negative.

Area above x-axis is positive; area below is negative. The integral gives the net signed area.

⚠ Signed vs Total Area

∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx gives signed area (positive minus negative). To get the total area regardless of sign, split at the zeros and use |∫| for each piece. The entrance exam sometimes asks for total area — read carefully.

2.2 The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC)

The FTC is the most important theorem in calculus. It connects differentiation and integration — two operations that seem completely unrelated — by showing they are inverse processes.

📌 FTC Part 1 — The Evaluation Theorem

If F is any antiderivative of f (i.e., F'(x) = f(x)), then:

∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)

This is often written as [F(x)]ₐᵇ = F(b) − F(a).
The constant C cancels out, so you can use any antiderivative.

📌 FTC Part 2 — The Derivative of an Integral

If g(x) = ∫ₐˣ f(t) dt, then:

g'(x) = f(x)

Differentiating an integral with a variable upper limit gives back the integrand. This is less commonly tested at Grade 12 level but good to know.

Differentiation and integration are inverse operations — this is the central insight of the FTC.

2.3 Step-by-Step: Evaluating a Definite Integral

Use this 4-step process on every definite integral:

Step 1
Find the antiderivative F(x)
Integrate f(x) — do NOT include + C
Step 2
Write [F(x)]ₐᵇ
Place limits using bracket notation
Step 3
Evaluate F(b) and F(a)
Substitute b first, then a
Step 4
Subtract: F(b) − F(a)
The answer is a single number
⚠ Most Common Exam Mistake

Students compute F(a) − F(b) instead of F(b) − F(a). Always subtract the lower limit value from the upper limit value. If you get the sign wrong you lose the mark.

2.4 Key Properties of Definite Integrals

These properties let you simplify or restructure integrals before evaluating:

Property Rule Note
Reversal ∫ₐᵇ f dx = −∫ᵦₐ f dx Swapping limits changes the sign
Zero width ∫ₐᵃ f dx = 0 Same upper and lower limit → zero
Constant factor ∫ₐᵇ k·f dx = k∫ₐᵇ f dx Pull constants outside
Sum / Difference ∫ₐᵇ (f ± g) dx = ∫ₐᵇ f dx ± ∫ₐᵇ g dx Split term by term
Interval split ∫ₐᵇ f dx = ∫ₐᶜ f dx + ∫ᶜᵇ f dx Split at any interior point c

2.5 Definite vs Indefinite — Side by Side

Indefinite Integral
∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + C
  • No limits
  • Result is a function
  • Always includes +C
  • Represents a family of curves
Definite Integral
∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b)−F(a)
  • Has limits a and b
  • Result is a number
  • No +C (it cancels out)
  • Represents a signed area

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1 Basic Polynomial — Applying the FTC
Problem: Evaluate ∫₁³ (2x + 1) dx
Step 1
Find the antiderivative of (2x + 1):
F(x) = x² + x   (no +C needed for definite integrals)
Step 2
Apply the FTC: write [F(x)]₁³
[x² + x]₁³
Step 3
Evaluate at upper limit b = 3:
F(3) = 3² + 3 = 9 + 3 = 12
Step 4
Evaluate at lower limit a = 1:
F(1) = 1² + 1 = 1 + 1 = 2
Step 5
Subtract F(b) − F(a):
12 − 2 = 10
✅ ∫₁³ (2x + 1) dx = 10
Example 2 Polynomial with Multiple Terms
Problem: Evaluate ∫₀² (3x² − 4x + 1) dx
Step 1
Find the antiderivative term by term:
F(x) = x³ − 2x² + x
Step 2
Evaluate F(2):
F(2) = 2³ − 2(2²) + 2 = 8 − 8 + 2 = 2
Step 3
Evaluate F(0):
F(0) = 0 − 0 + 0 = 0
Step 4
Subtract: F(2) − F(0) = 2 − 0 = 2
✅ ∫₀² (3x² − 4x + 1) dx = 2
Example 3 Using the Reversal Property
Problem: If ∫₂⁵ f(x) dx = 9, find ∫₅² f(x) dx.
Step 1
Recall the reversal property:
∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = −∫ᵦₐ f(x) dx
Step 2
Here the limits are swapped (5 is now the lower, 2 is now the upper):
∫₅² f(x) dx = −∫₂⁵ f(x) dx = −9
✅ ∫₅² f(x) dx = −9
Example 4 Trigonometric Definite Integral
Problem: Evaluate ∫₀^(π/2) cos x dx
Step 1
Antiderivative of cos x is sin x:
F(x) = sin x
Step 2
Evaluate at upper limit b = π/2:
F(π/2) = sin(π/2) = 1
Step 3
Evaluate at lower limit a = 0:
F(0) = sin(0) = 0
Step 4
Subtract: 1 − 0 = 1
✅ ∫₀^(π/2) cos x dx = 1
Example 5 Using the Interval Split Property
Problem: Given ∫₀³ f(x) dx = 7 and ∫₀⁵ f(x) dx = 12, find ∫₃⁵ f(x) dx.
Step 1
Use the interval split property:
∫₀⁵ f(x) dx = ∫₀³ f(x) dx + ∫₃⁵ f(x) dx
Step 2
Substitute the known values:
12 = 7 + ∫₃⁵ f(x) dx
Step 3
Solve:
∫₃⁵ f(x) dx = 12 − 7 = 5
✅ ∫₃⁵ f(x) dx = 5

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 2
✅ Key Points — Definite Integrals
  • ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx is the signed area under y = f(x) between x = a and x = b — the result is a number, not a function.
  • The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a), where F'(x) = f(x). This is the main tool for evaluation.
  • To evaluate: find antiderivative F(x), substitute b to get F(b), substitute a to get F(a), then compute F(b) − F(a).
  • No + C is written in definite integrals — the constant cancels in F(b) − F(a).
  • Reversal: ∫ₐᵇ f dx = −∫ᵦₐ f dx. Swapping the limits negates the answer.
  • Same limits: ∫ₐᵃ f dx = 0. Zero-width interval has zero area.
  • Interval split: ∫ₐᵇ f dx = ∫ₐᶜ f dx + ∫ᶜᵇ f dx. Useful for finding unknown pieces.
  • Area above the x-axis is positive; area below is negative. Total area uses absolute values of each piece.
