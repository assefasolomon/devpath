Unit 2 · Limits and Continuity

→ Concept of a Limit

Understand one of the most fundamental ideas in calculus — the limit. Learn what it means for a function to approach a value, how to read limit notation, and how to evaluate simple limits intuitively and numerically.

✏️ Examples: 5 worked

Introduction
What is a limit and why does it matter?

Imagine you are driving toward a city. As you get closer and closer, you approach the city — but you may never actually enter it. In mathematics, a limit describes exactly this idea: what value does a function approach as the input gets closer and closer to some number?

The concept of a limit is the foundation of all calculus. Without limits, we cannot define derivatives, integrals, or continuity. The Ethiopian Grade 12 entrance exam tests limits in several ways — direct substitution, factoring, and rationalization.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Explain the concept of a limit in plain language
  • Read and write limit notation correctly: lim(x→a) f(x) = L
  • Evaluate limits numerically using tables of values
  • Evaluate limits by direct substitution when possible
  • Recognise when a limit does not exist
  • Distinguish between the limit of f at a and the value f(a)

Explanation
Complete theory with visual aids
📌 Definition — The Limit

We say the limit of f(x) as x approaches a is L, written:

lim(x→a) f(x) = L

if f(x) gets closer and closer to L as x gets closer and closer to a (from either side), without x ever equaling a.

2.1 Understanding Limits Numerically

Consider f(x) = (x² − 1)/(x − 1). What happens as x → 1?
Note: f(1) is undefined (division by zero). But we can approach from both sides:

x approaches 1 from left f(x) x approaches 1 from right f(x)
0.9 1.9 1.1 2.1
0.99 1.99 1.01 2.01
0.999 1.999 1.001 2.001
0.9999 1.9999 1.0001 2.0001

From both sides, f(x) → 2. Therefore lim(x→1) (x²−1)/(x−1) = 2, even though f(1) is undefined.

⚠ Key Insight

The limit describes what f(x) approaches, NOT what f(x) equals at that point. The function does not need to be defined at x = a for the limit to exist.

2.2 Direct Substitution

For most polynomial and rational functions where f(a) is defined, you can evaluate the limit by simply substituting x = a:

If f is continuous at a, then lim(x→a) f(x) = f(a)

Example: lim(x→3) (x² + 2x − 1) = 3² + 2(3) − 1 = 9 + 6 − 1 = 14

⚠ When Direct Substitution Fails

If substituting x = a gives 0/0 (called an indeterminate form), you cannot use direct substitution. Instead, try factoring, rationalizing, or simplifying the expression first.

2.3 Factoring to Resolve Indeterminate Forms

When direct substitution gives 0/0, factor the numerator or denominator, cancel common factors, then substitute:

lim(x→1) (x²−1)/(x−1)
= lim(x→1) (x+1)(x−1)/(x−1)    ← factor numerator
= lim(x→1) (x+1)              ← cancel (x−1), x≠1
= 1 + 1 = 2

2.4 When Does a Limit NOT Exist?

A limit does not exist (DNE) in three main situations:

Left ≠ Right
Left-hand limit ≠ right-hand limit
Unbounded
f(x) → ∞ or −∞ (vertical asymptote)
Oscillation
f(x) oscillates and never settles

2.5 Limits at Infinity

We can also ask: what does f(x) approach as x → ∞ or x → −∞?

Key Rules for Limits at Infinity

• lim(x→∞) 1/xⁿ = 0 for any n > 0
• lim(x→∞) c = c (constant stays constant)
• For rational functions: divide numerator and denominator by highest power of x in denominator

Example: lim(x→∞) (3x²+2)/(x²−1) = lim (3+2/x²)/(1−1/x²) = 3/1 = 3

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1 Direct Substitution
Problem: Evaluate lim(x→2) (3x² − 4x + 1)
Check
f(x) = 3x²−4x+1 is a polynomial — defined everywhere. Use direct substitution.
Substitute
lim(x→2) (3x²−4x+1) = 3(2)²−4(2)+1 = 12−8+1 = 5
✅ lim(x→2) (3x²−4x+1) = 5
Example 2 Factoring — 0/0 Form
Problem: Evaluate lim(x→3) (x²−9)/(x−3)
Check
Substituting x=3: (9−9)/(3−3) = 0/0 — indeterminate. Must factor.
Factor
= lim(x→3) (x+3)(x−3)/(x−3)
Cancel
= lim(x→3) (x+3) = 3+3 = 6
✅ lim(x→3) (x²−9)/(x−3) = 6
Example 3 Factoring Cubic Expression
Problem: Evaluate lim(x→2) (x³−8)/(x−2)
Check
x=2 gives 0/0. Factor using difference of cubes: a³−b³ = (a−b)(a²+ab+b²)
Factor
x³−8 = (x−2)(x²+2x+4)
= lim(x→2) (x−2)(x²+2x+4)/(x−2)
Cancel
= lim(x→2) (x²+2x+4) = 4+4+4 = 12
✅ lim(x→2) (x³−8)/(x−2) = 12
Example 4 Limit at Infinity
Problem: Evaluate lim(x→∞) (5x² + 3x)/(2x² − 7)
Strategy
Divide every term by x² (highest power in denominator)
Divide
= lim(x→∞) (5 + 3/x)/(2 − 7/x²)
Apply
As x→∞: 3/x → 0 and 7/x² → 0
= (5 + 0)/(2 − 0) = 5/2
✅ lim(x→∞) (5x²+3x)/(2x²−7) = 5/2
Example 5 Limit That Does Not Exist
Problem: Evaluate lim(x→0) |x|/x
Left side
As x→0⁻ (x is negative): |x| = −x, so |x|/x = −x/x = −1
Right side
As x→0⁺ (x is positive): |x| = x, so |x|/x = x/x = +1
Compare
Left limit = −1 ≠ Right limit = +1
✅ lim(x→0) |x|/x does NOT EXIST — left and right limits are different

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Unit 2 Lesson 1
✅ Key Points — Concept of a Limit
  • A limit describes what f(x) approaches as x gets closer to a — not necessarily the value at x = a.
  • Notation: lim(x→a) f(x) = L means f(x) → L as x → a from both sides.
  • The function does NOT need to be defined at x = a for the limit to exist there.
  • Direct substitution works when f is a polynomial or when f(a) is defined and continuous.
  • If substitution gives 0/0, factor and cancel the common factor, then substitute.
  • A limit does NOT exist when left and right limits differ, or when f(x) → ±∞.
  • For limits at infinity: divide numerator and denominator by highest power of x.
  • Important: lim(x→0) sin(x)/x = 1 — memorise this fundamental limit.
