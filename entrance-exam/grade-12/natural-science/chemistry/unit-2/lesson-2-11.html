📏 Standard Electrode Potentials & EMF

How do we know which metal will oxidize and which will reduce? By comparing their standard reduction potentials against the SHE (Standard Hydrogen Electrode). This lesson shows you how to measure those values and use them to predict any redox reaction.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources

Introduction
What you will learn

When you connect two half-cells, how do you know which will be the anode and which the cathode? How do you know the voltage the cell will produce? The answer lies in standard electrode potentials — a table of measured voltages for every common half-reaction, all measured relative to a single reference: the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE).

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define standard electrode potential E°
  • Describe the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE) and why it's used
  • Explain how electrode potentials are measured experimentally
  • Interpret the standard reduction potential table
  • Apply E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
  • Predict spontaneity of redox reactions from E°cell
  • Use the diagonal rule to identify spontaneous reactions
⚡
The Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)
The universal reference
📌 The measurement problem

It is impossible to measure the potential of just a single electrode. But if we arbitrarily set the potential of one particular electrode to zero, we can measure every other electrode relative to it.

📌 Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

The SHE consists of a platinum electrode with H₂ gas at 1 atm bubbling through it, immersed in 1 M strong acid (H⁺). Its potential is defined as exactly 0.00 V by convention.

The Standard Hydrogen Electrode
The SHE by definition has a reduction potential of exactly 0.00 V. Every other electrode potential is measured against it.
📌 The Pt electrode has two functions

1. Provides a surface on which H₂ molecules can dissociate: H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻
2. Conducts electricity to the external circuit

Standard Hydrogen Electrode — half-reaction
2H⁺(1 M) + 2e⁻ → H₂(1 atm)    E° = 0.00 V
📝 Standard-state conditions

For any standard electrode potential E°, all solutes must be 1 M, all gases at 1 atm, and the temperature must be 25 °C (298 K).

🔬
Measuring Standard Electrode Potentials
How E° values are obtained

To find an unknown standard electrode potential, we construct a voltaic cell consisting of the SHE reference half-cell and the unknown half-cell. The measured EMF gives us the unknown E°.

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
where E°(SHE) = 0.00 V

Two Measurement Scenarios

📝 Scenario 1 — Unknown electrode is the CATHODE

H₂ is oxidized at the reference half-cell (SHE is the anode). Reduction occurs at the unknown half-cell.

E°cell = E°unknown − E°reference = E°unknown − 0.00 = E°unknown
So the measured EMF directly gives the unknown reduction potential.

📝 Scenario 2 — Unknown electrode is the ANODE

H₂ is reduced at the reference half-cell (SHE is the cathode). Oxidation occurs at the unknown half-cell.

E°cell = E°reference − E°unknown = 0.00 − E°unknown = −E°unknown
The measured EMF is the negative of the unknown reduction potential.

Example — Measuring E° for Zn²⁺/Zn

Zn | SHE Cell — Zn is the Anode
Anode (Oxidation)
Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(1 M) + 2e⁻
Cathode (Reduction)
2H⁺(1 M) + 2e⁻ → H₂(1 atm)

The measured EMF is 0.76 V and Zn is being oxidized. Apply the formula:

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
0.76 = E°(H⁺/H₂) − E°(Zn²⁺/Zn)
0.76 = 0.00 − E°(Zn²⁺/Zn)
E°(Zn²⁺/Zn) = −0.76 V

📝 Similar for copper

For the Cu²⁺/Cu half-cell, Cu is the cathode (mass increases during operation). The measured EMF = 0.34 V.
E°cell = E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) − E°(H⁺/H₂) → 0.34 = E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) − 0.00
E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) = +0.34 V

How to Read This Table

📌 Rule 1 — More positive E° means more easily reduced

The species on the left of a half-reaction with a very positive E° (like F₂) is a strong oxidizing agent — it wants electrons badly.

📌 Rule 2 — More negative E° means harder to reduce

The species with a very negative E° (like Li⁺, K⁺, Na⁺) is very hard to reduce. The metal form (Li, K, Na) is a strong reducing agent — it gives away electrons easily.

📌 Rule 3 — Oxidizing and reducing strengths

• Oxidizing agents (left side) increase in strength from bottom to top of the table
• Reducing agents (right side) increase in strength from top to bottom

🧮
Calculating EMF (Cell Potential)
The master formula
E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
using standard reduction potentials for both
📌 Key rule

Both E°cathode and E°anode are the standard reduction potentials from the table. Do NOT flip the sign of the anode's E° value — the subtraction in the formula already handles the sign correctly.

📝 Alternative method (also valid)

Some textbooks use: E°cell = E°(reduction) + E°(oxidation) where E°(oxidation) = −E°(reduction for that half-cell from the table).
Both methods give the same answer. Pick one and stick with it.

Example — The Daniell Cell

Anode (oxidation): Zn(s) → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻   [E°(Zn²⁺/Zn) = −0.76 V]
Cathode (reduction): Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)   [E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) = +0.34 V]

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
E°cell = E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) − E°(Zn²⁺/Zn)
E°cell = 0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V

📝 Why don't we multiply E° when balancing?

E° is an intensive property — like density or temperature. It does not depend on how many times the half-reaction occurs. So even if you multiply the Cu²⁺ half-reaction by 2 to balance electrons, E° stays 0.34 V.

🎯
Predicting Spontaneity
The sign of E° tells you everything
📌 Two possible outcomes

E°cell > 0 (positive) → reaction is spontaneous; forward direction favored; ΔG° is negative

E°cell < 0 (negative) → reaction is non-spontaneous; reverse direction favored; ΔG° is positive

The Diagonal Rule (Textbook p. 112)

📌 The rule

Under standard-state conditions, any species on the left of a given half-cell reaction will react spontaneously with a species that appears on the right of any half-cell reaction located below it in the table.

Diagonal Rule — Reading the Table
📝 Example — Can Zn reduce Cu²⁺?

Zn is on the right of the Zn²⁺/Zn half-reaction. Cu²⁺ is on the left of a half-reaction above Zn²⁺/Zn.
The diagonal rule says: spontaneous if right-species (Zn) is below left-species (Cu²⁺). Zn is below Cu²⁺ in the table → spontaneous ✓

Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)   E°cell = +1.10 V

📝 Example — Can Sn reduce Zn²⁺?

Sn is on the right of Sn²⁺/Sn (E° = −0.14 V); Zn²⁺ is on the left of Zn²⁺/Zn (E° = −0.76 V).
Sn is above Zn²⁺/Zn, so it cannot reduce Zn²⁺.
E°cell = E°(Zn²⁺/Zn) − E°(Sn²⁺/Sn) = (−0.76) − (−0.14) = −0.62 V → non-spontaneous

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1E°cell for Cu-Zn (Daniell)
Problem: Calculate the standard EMF of the Daniell cell.
Given: E°(Zn²⁺/Zn) = −0.76 V, E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) = +0.34 V
Identify electrodes
Zn is oxidized (anode) because its E° is more negative; Cu²⁺ is reduced (cathode)
Formula
E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
Substitute
E°cell = 0.34 − (−0.76)
Compute
E°cell = 1.10 V
✅ E°cell = +1.10 V — the reaction is spontaneous
Example 2Mg-Ag Cell (Textbook Example 2.9)
Problem: A galvanic cell consists of a Mg electrode in 1.0 M Mg(NO₃)₂ and an Ag electrode in 1.0 M AgNO₃. Calculate the standard EMF and determine if the reaction is spontaneous.
E° values
Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag   E° = +0.80 V
Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mg   E° = −2.37 V
Identify electrodes
Mg has more negative E° → oxidized (anode); Ag⁺ reduced (cathode)
Balance
Anode: Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻
Cathode: 2Ag⁺ + 2e⁻ → 2Ag
Formula
E°cell = E°(Ag⁺/Ag) − E°(Mg²⁺/Mg)
= 0.80 − (−2.37) = +3.17 V
✅ E°cell = +3.17 V — spontaneous (positive E°cell)
Example 3Can Sn Reduce Zn²⁺?
Problem: Can Sn metal reduce Zn²⁺(aq) under standard-state conditions?
E° values
Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn   E° = −0.76 V
Sn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Sn   E° = −0.14 V
Hypothetical cell
If Sn reduces Zn²⁺:
Anode (Sn oxidized): Sn → Sn²⁺ + 2e⁻
Cathode (Zn²⁺ reduced): Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn
Compute E°cell
E°cell = E°(Zn²⁺/Zn) − E°(Sn²⁺/Sn)
= −0.76 − (−0.14) = −0.62 V
✅ No — E°cell is negative (−0.62 V) → the reaction is non-spontaneous
Example 4Cu-Mg Cell (Textbook Exercise)
Problem: Given the reaction Cu²⁺(1.0 M) + Mg(s) → Cu(s) + Mg²⁺(1.0 M), sketch the galvanic cell, identify the cathode and anode, and show the direction of electron flow.
E° values
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu   E° = +0.34 V
Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mg   E° = −2.37 V
Identify electrodes
Mg is oxidized (more negative E°) → anode (−)
Cu²⁺ is reduced → cathode (+)
Electron flow
Electrons flow from Mg (anode) → Cu (cathode) in the external wire
E°cell
E°cell = 0.34 − (−2.37) = +2.71 V
✅ Anode = Mg (−); Cathode = Cu (+); e⁻ flow Mg → Cu; E°cell = +2.71 V
Example 5Cd-Cr Cell (Textbook Exercise)
Problem: What is the standard EMF of a galvanic cell made of a Cd electrode in 1.0 M Cd(NO₃)₂ and a Cr electrode in 1.0 M Cr(NO₃)₃ at 25 °C?
E° values
Cd²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cd   E° = −0.40 V
Cr³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Cr   E° = −0.74 V
Identify electrodes
Cr has more negative E° → oxidized (anode); Cd²⁺ reduced (cathode)
Formula
E°cell = E°(Cd²⁺/Cd) − E°(Cr³⁺/Cr)
= −0.40 − (−0.74)
Compute
E°cell = +0.34 V
✅ E°cell = +0.34 V — spontaneous
