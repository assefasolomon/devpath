🏭 Industrial Applications of Electrolysis

From extracting aluminum to refining copper to coating a key with chrome — electrolysis is one of the most versatile tools in industry. This lesson surveys the four big categories: extraction, refining, plating, and electrosynthesis.

📖 Reading: ~22 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions

Introduction
What you will learn

Electrolysis isn't just a lab curiosity — it drives some of the largest industries in the world. Aluminum production, copper refining, chrome plating, chlorine manufacture, and sodium hydroxide production all rely on it. This lesson connects everything you've learned in Unit 2 to real industrial processes.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • List the four major categories of industrial electrolysis applications
  • Explain electrolytic extraction of aluminum from Al₂O₃
  • Describe the electrolytic refining of copper (electrorefining)
  • Explain electroplating and its purpose
  • Describe the chlor-alkali process for making Cl₂ and NaOH
  • Predict products of any industrial electrolysis setup

Overview of Industrial Applications
The four big categories
📌 Electrosynthesis (MoE Textbook, p. 94)

Electrosynthesis is the method of producing substances through electrolysis reactions. Its main industrial applications are:

1. Extraction of metals
2. Electrorefining of metals
3. Electroplating of metals
4. Electrosynthesis of nonmetals and compounds

⛏️
1. Extraction
Extract reactive metals (Al, Na, Mg, Ca) from their molten compounds.
🔬
2. Refining
Purify impure metals (Cu, Zn, Ni, Ag, Au) using electrolysis.
✨
3. Plating
Coat one metal with another (chrome, silver, gold, nickel) for protection or appearance.
🧪
4. Electrosynthesis
Produce nonmetals (Cl₂, F₂) and compounds (NaOH) from raw materials.
⛏️
Extraction of Metals — Aluminum from Al₂O₃
Only electrolysis works for reactive metals
📌 Why electrolysis for reactive metals?

If a metal is less reactive than carbon, it can be extracted from its ore by reacting with carbon (a displacement reaction). But metals more reactive than carbon — like aluminum — must be extracted by electrolysis.

The Aluminum Extraction Cell

Electrolytic Extraction of Aluminum
Aluminum oxide is dissolved in molten cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) to lower the melting point from ~2000°C to ~1000°C.
Cathode (Reduction)
4Al³⁺ + 12e⁻ → 4Al(l)
Anode (Oxidation)
6O²⁻ → 3O₂(g) + 12e⁻
Overall Reaction
4Al³⁺ + 6O²⁻ → 4Al(l) + 3O₂(g)
📝 Key industrial facts

• The cathode is the carbon lining of the cell; molten aluminum collects at the bottom
• The anodes are graphite rods that are gradually consumed by the O₂ produced
• Aluminum is a very reactive metal, yet it is the most abundant metal in Earth's crust

🔬
Electrolytic Refining of Copper
Electrorefining
📌 Definition (MoE Textbook, p. 96)

Electrorefining is the process of purifying an impure metal using electrolysis. Many metals — copper, zinc, tin, nickel, silver, gold — are refined this way.

The Setup

ComponentMaterial
AnodeImpure metal (e.g. impure Cu)
CathodeThin strip of pure metal (pure Cu)
ElectrolyteSolution of the metal salt (acidified CuSO₄)
Electrorefining of Copper
Anode (Oxidation)
Cu(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻
Cathode (Reduction)
Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)
📌 What happens during electrorefining

• Pure metal from the anode dissolves into the electrolyte
• An equivalent amount of pure metal deposits on the cathode
• Insoluble impurities (silver, gold, platinum) settle to the bottom as anode mud
• Soluble impurities go into the solution

📝 The anode mud bonus

Anode mud often contains valuable metals like silver and gold. Refineries extract these as a profitable by-product.

✨
Electroplating of Metals
Coating for protection and appearance
📌 Definition (MoE Textbook, p. 97)

Electroplating is the process of plating one metal onto another by electrolysis, most commonly for decorative purposes or to prevent corrosion of a metal.

Why Electroplate?

💰
Save Cost
Use cheap metals (steel, zinc) for the bulk of the product, then apply a thin expensive layer for appearance or properties.
🛡️
Protect from Corrosion
Chrome, nickel, and zinc coatings prevent rust and tarnish.
💎
Improve Appearance
Silver, gold, and chrome plating for jewelry, silverware, and household fittings.

The Setup

ComponentFunction
Cathode (−)The object to be plated (e.g. iron key)
Anode (+)The plating metal (e.g. copper plate)
ElectrolyteSalt solution of the plating metal (e.g. CuSO₄)
📌 Types of electroplating

Copper plating · Silver plating · Chromium plating · Nickel plating · Gold plating · Zinc plating (galvanizing)

📝 Choosing the right side

The object to be plated always becomes the cathode (−). The metal that will form the coating always becomes the anode (+). The electrolyte is a salt of the plating metal.

Experiment 2.3 — Electroplating an Iron Key
Textbook pp. 98–99
🧪 Electroplating of an Iron Key with Copper Metal
Objective: To conduct electroplating of an iron key with copper metal.

Apparatus & Chemicals: Beaker, sand paper, copper wire, power supply (DC) or dry cells, distilled water, 1 M CuSO₄ solution, 1 M H₂SO₄ solution, copper rod, and an iron key.

Procedure:
  • Dissolve 2 teaspoonfuls of CuSO₄ in 250 mL distilled water. Add a few drops of dilute H₂SO₄ to increase conduction.
  • Clean the copper plate (10 cm × 4 cm) and iron key with sandpaper. Rinse and dry.
  • Place the copper plate in the CuSO₄ solution and connect it to the positive terminal — it becomes the anode.
  • Place the clean iron key in the solution at a small distance from the copper plate and connect the negative terminal — the key becomes the cathode.
  • Switch on the current and let it pass for about 15 minutes.
  • Remove the copper plate and iron key from the solution.
Key Questions (from textbook):
  • What are the purposes of electroplating?
  • Why is it important to polish the copper plate and iron key before use?
  • What do you observe on the surface of the iron key?
  • What happens to the free copper ions in the CuSO₄ solution?
  • What happens to the copper plate connected to the positive terminal?
📝 Why polish the metals first?

Oxide layers and grease on the surfaces would prevent proper contact with the solution and interfere with the copper deposition. Polishing exposes clean metal.

Electrosynthesis of Nonmetals & Compounds
The chlor-alkali industry

Many nonmetals and compounds are produced using electrolysis. The most important industrial example is the chlor-alkali industry, which produces chlorine (a nonmetal) and sodium hydroxide (a compound) from brine (concentrated NaCl).

📌 The Chlor-Alkali Process

Electrolysis of concentrated NaCl (brine) using graphite electrodes produces three valuable products:

Cl₂ (chlorine gas) at the anode
H₂ (hydrogen gas) at the cathode
NaOH (sodium hydroxide) in solution

Anode Reaction
2Cl⁻(aq) → Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻
Cathode Reaction
2H₂O(l) + 2e⁻ → H₂(g) + 2OH⁻(aq)
Overall Cell Reaction
2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2Na⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) + Cl₂(g) + H₂(g)

Uses of Chlor-Alkali Products

ProductMain Uses
Cl₂Water treatment, PVC plastic, bleach, disinfectants
H₂Ammonia production (Haber process), fuel cells, hydrogenation
NaOHSoap manufacturing, paper industry, aluminium extraction, drain cleaners

Other Electrosynthesis Products

🔶
Fluorine (F₂)
Produced by electrolysis of molten KF·2HF mixture. Used in Teflon and refrigerants.
💧
H₂ and O₂
From electrolysis of water (with a small electrolyte added for conductivity).
🧂
NaOH
A major by-product of the chlor-alkali process.
⚡
Al, Na, Mg, Ca
Reactive metals extracted from molten salts by electrolysis.

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1Why Electrolysis for Aluminum?
Problem: Aluminum is the most abundant metal in Earth's crust. Explain why it cannot be extracted by heating with carbon.
Reactivity
Aluminum is more reactive than carbon in the activity series
Displacement
Carbon can only displace metals below it in the series — Al is above, so carbon cannot reduce Al₂O₃
Solution
Aluminum is extracted from molten Al₂O₃ using electrolysis
✅ Al is too reactive — only electrolysis can extract it from its ore
Example 2Anode Mud in Cu Refining
Problem: During electrorefining of copper, what are (a) the anode mud and (b) the fate of soluble impurities?
Anode mud
The insoluble impurities (Ag, Au, Pt) settle to the bottom as a solid deposit — called anode mud
Soluble impurities
The soluble impurities (e.g. Zn, Fe) dissolve into the CuSO₄ solution and stay there
Why it matters
Anode mud is a valuable by-product — it contains refined silver and gold
✅ Anode mud = insoluble impurities; soluble impurities go into solution
Example 3Which Electrode for Plating?
Problem: You want to silver-plate a steel spoon. Which electrode should the spoon be? What should the anode be? What electrolyte should be used?
Cathode
The object to be plated (the spoon) is always the cathode (−)
Anode
The plating metal (pure silver) is the anode (+)
Electrolyte
A salt of the plating metal — AgNO₃ solution
✅ Spoon = cathode; silver rod = anode; AgNO₃ = electrolyte
Example 4Chlor-Alkali Products
Problem: In the chlor-alkali industry, list the three products of electrolysis of concentrated brine and one major use of each.
Anode product
Cl₂ — used in water treatment and PVC manufacture
Cathode product
H₂ — used in Haber process for ammonia and in fuel cells
Solution product
NaOH — used in soap making, paper industry, and aluminium extraction
✅ Products: Cl₂ (anode), H₂ (cathode), NaOH (in solution)
Example 5Same Principle, Three Industries
Problem: Explain how the same basic principle of "active anode dissolving" is used in both electrorefining of copper and electroplating of copper.
Common principle
In both cases, a Cu anode dissolves to form Cu²⁺ in solution, and Cu²⁺ deposits as Cu at the cathode
Refining
The anode is impure Cu, cathode is pure Cu → the impure metal is purified
Plating
The anode is pure Cu, cathode is iron key → the key is coated with copper
✅ Same chemistry (Cu ⇌ Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻) — different intent (purification vs coating)

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 2.9
✅ Key Points — Industrial Applications of Electrolysis
  • Four categories of industrial electrolysis: extraction, refining, plating, electrosynthesis
  • Aluminum extraction: electrolysis of molten Al₂O₃ (with cryolite); cathode: 4Al³⁺ + 12e⁻ → 4Al; anode: 6O²⁻ → 3O₂ + 12e⁻
  • Only electrolysis can extract metals more reactive than carbon (Al, Na, Mg, Ca)
  • Electrorefining of copper: impure Cu is the anode, pure Cu is the cathode, acidified CuSO₄ is the electrolyte
  • Anode mud = insoluble impurities (Ag, Au, Pt) that settle at the bottom
  • Electroplating: object to be plated = cathode; plating metal = anode; electrolyte = salt of plating metal
  • Chlor-alkali process: electrolysis of brine produces Cl₂ (anode), H₂ (cathode), and NaOH (solution)
  • Electrosynthesis also produces F₂, H₂, O₂ and reactive metals
  • Common principle: active anode dissolves; pure metal deposits at cathode
