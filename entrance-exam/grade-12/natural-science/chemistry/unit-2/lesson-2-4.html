🔌 Conductivity — Metallic vs Electrolytic

Why does a copper wire conduct electricity without changing, but salt water conducts only when ions move and chemical reactions occur? Understanding these two different mechanisms is the foundation of electrochemistry.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Before we can study electrolysis (Lesson 2.5) or galvanic cells (Lesson 2.10), we need to understand how electricity travels through matter. There are two fundamentally different mechanisms — one in metals, one in electrolyte solutions — and mixing them up is one of the most common sources of confusion in electrochemistry.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define electrical conductivity and conductors
  • Explain how metallic (electronic) conduction works
  • Explain how electrolytic (ionic) conduction works
  • Compare the two mechanisms — carriers, temperature effect, chemical change
  • Classify substances as conductors, strong electrolytes, weak electrolytes, or non-electrolytes
  • Describe the conductivity test experiment
⚡
What is a Conductor?
Definition and classification
📌 Electrical Conductivity

Electrical conductivity is the ability of a substance to transmit electricity. Substances that allow the passage of electric current are called conductors.

📌 Two Types of Conductors

Conductors are classified into two types depending on their mode of transport of electrical charges:

• Electronic (metallic) conductors — charge carried by mobile electrons
• Electrolytic conductors — charge carried by mobile ions

Classification of Conductors
📝 The core difference

In metals, only electrons move. In electrolytes, entire ions (atoms or molecules with charge) physically migrate through the solution — meaning matter moves too.

🔴
Metallic (Electronic) Conduction
Movement of delocalized electrons
📌 Definition (MoE Textbook, p. 75)

Metallic conduction — metals and alloys conduct electricity due to the movement (mobility) of delocalized outer-shell electrons present inside them. These are also called electronic conductors.

Metallic Conduction — Electron Sea Model
Key FeatureMetallic Conduction
Charge carrierDelocalized electrons (e⁻)
Transfer of matter?No — only energy flows
Chemical change?No chemical transformation
ExamplesCu, Ag, Al, Fe, graphite, alloys
State requiredSolid (or molten metal)
Effect of temperature ↑Conductivity decreases
📝 Why does heat decrease metallic conductivity?

Higher temperature makes the metal atoms vibrate more. These vibrations disrupt the smooth flow of electrons through the lattice, so conductivity drops.

🔵
Electrolytic (Ionic) Conduction
Movement of ions in solution
📌 Definition (MoE Textbook, p. 75)

Electrolytic conduction — electrovalent (ionic) compounds conduct electricity in their dissolved state (in solution) or in their fused (molten) state only. In the presence of an applied electric field, the ions migrate to their respective electrodes, exhibiting electrical conductivity.

Electrolytic Conduction — Ion Migration
Cations (Na⁺) migrate toward the negative electrode (cathode). Anions (Cl⁻) migrate toward the positive electrode (anode).
Key FeatureElectrolytic Conduction
Charge carrierIons (cations + anions)
Transfer of matter?Yes — ions physically move
Chemical change?Yes — reactions at electrodes
ExamplesNaCl(aq), H₂SO₄(aq), molten KCl
State requiredDissolved or molten
Effect of temperature ↑Conductivity increases
📝 Why does heat increase electrolytic conductivity?

Higher temperature increases ionic mobility — the ions move faster through the solution, so more charge is carried per second and conductivity rises.

Experiment 2.1 — Electrical Conductivity Test
Textbook pp. 76–77
🧪 Experiment 2.1 — Classifying Substances as Conductors and Insulators
Objective: To classify substances as electrical conductors and insulators.

Materials & Chemicals: Copper wire, light bulb, power supply (DC) or dry cells, scotch tape, distilled water, 1 M NaCl solution, 1 M H₂SO₄ solution, 1 M CH₃COOH solution, 1 M sugar solution, magnesium ribbon, iron nail.

Procedure:
  • Polish a copper strip and connect it as shown in Figure 2.2a. Repeat with the iron nail and magnesium ribbon.
  • Rinse a 100 mL beaker with distilled water, fill to 50 mL mark, and insert the two ends of the copper wire (Figure 2.2b).
  • Clean the wire ends, then insert into beakers containing 1 M NaCl, 1 M H₂SO₄, 1 M CH₃COOH, and 1 M sugar solution. Record observations.
Key Questions (from textbook):
  • Why is it important to polish the magnesium ribbon and iron nail before use?
  • Classify the materials you used as conductors, strong electrolytes, weak electrolytes, and non-electrolytes.
  • Label the two ends of the wire in Figure 2.2b as cathode and anode.
📝 Why polish metals first?

A layer of oxide (rust) or tarnish on the surface is an insulator. Polishing removes it so the metal underneath can make proper contact with the circuit.

🧪
Strong vs Weak Electrolytes
Not all electrolytes conduct equally well

Electrolytes are substances whose aqueous solutions conduct electricity. The brightness of the bulb in the experiment tells you how well they conduct — which depends on how fully they ionize.

CategoryIonizationBulb brightnessExamples
Strong electrolyte~100% (complete)BrightNaCl, H₂SO₄, HCl, NaOH
Weak electrolytePartial (<5%)DimCH₃COOH, NH₃, H₂CO₃
Non-electrolyteNone — molecularOffSugar, ethanol, distilled water
📌 Key rule

Conductivity depends on the concentration of free ions in solution.
More ions → more charge carriers → brighter bulb.

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1Classify a Substance
Problem: Classify each of the following as a metallic conductor, strong electrolyte, weak electrolyte, or non-electrolyte: (a) iron, (b) solid NaCl, (c) molten CaCl₂, (d) sugar solution.
(a) Iron
Metal → metallic conductor
(b) Solid NaCl
Ions locked in crystal lattice cannot move → does not conduct (insulator)
(c) Molten CaCl₂
Ions free to move → strong electrolyte
(d) Sugar solution
Molecular — no ions formed → non-electrolyte
✅ (a) metallic | (b) insulator | (c) strong electrolyte | (d) non-electrolyte
Example 2Temperature Effect
Problem: Explain why increasing the temperature increases the conductivity of an electrolyte solution but decreases the conductivity of a metal.
Electrolyte
Higher T → higher ionic mobility → more ions reach electrodes per second → conductivity ↑
Metal
Higher T → stronger thermal vibrations of metal atoms → more collisions with electrons → conductivity ↓
✅ Opposite effects because the charge carriers are different (ions vs electrons)
Example 3Why Solid NaCl Doesn't Conduct
Problem: Solid NaCl does not conduct electricity, but NaCl solution does. Explain why.
Solid state
Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are locked into fixed positions in the crystal lattice. They cannot move → no charge flow
Dissolved state
When dissolved in water, the lattice breaks apart and ions become free to move. They migrate toward the electrodes → solution conducts
✅ Conductivity requires mobile charge carriers — locked ions cannot carry charge
Example 4Compare Brightness
Problem: Three beakers contain 1 M NaCl, 1 M CH₃COOH, and 1 M glucose. A conductivity test is performed. Predict the order of bulb brightness from brightest to dimmest.
1 M NaCl
Strong electrolyte — fully ionized → brightest
1 M CH₃COOH
Weak electrolyte — partially ionized → dim
1 M glucose
Non-electrolyte — no ions → off
✅ NaCl (brightest) > CH₃COOH (dim) > glucose (off)
Example 5Graphite vs Diamond
Problem: Both graphite and diamond are made of pure carbon. Graphite conducts electricity; diamond does not. Explain why.
Graphite
Each C atom bonds to only 3 others; the 4th electron is delocalized across the layers → free to move → conducts
Diamond
Each C atom bonds to 4 others in a rigid tetrahedral lattice — no free electrons → does not conduct
✅ Graphite = metallic conductor; Diamond = insulator (no delocalized electrons)

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 2.4
✅ Key Points — Conductivity
  • Electrical conductivity = ability to transmit electricity; substances that do this are conductors
  • Two types: electronic (metallic) and electrolytic
  • Metallic conduction: carried by delocalized electrons; no matter transfer; no chemical change; conductivity ↓ with temperature ↑
  • Electrolytic conduction: carried by migrating ions; involves matter transfer and chemical reactions at electrodes; conductivity ↑ with temperature ↑
  • Solid ionic compounds do not conduct — ions are locked in the lattice
  • Ionic compounds do conduct when dissolved or molten — ions are free to move
  • Strong electrolytes (NaCl, H₂SO₄, HCl) → bright bulb
  • Weak electrolytes (CH₃COOH, NH₃) → dim bulb
  • Non-electrolytes (sugar, ethanol) → bulb off
  • Reference: MoE Chemistry Grade 12 Student Textbook, §2.2 intro + Experiment 2.1 (pp. 74–77)
