🧪 Electrolytic Cells & Preferential Discharge

When more than one ion arrives at an electrode, which one gets discharged? The answer depends on three factors — position in the electrochemical series, ion concentration, and the nature of the electrode.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources

Introduction
What you will learn

An electrolytic cell uses electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous chemical reaction. When an aqueous solution is electrolyzed, there are usually multiple ions competing at each electrode — and only one of them actually gets discharged. Understanding which one is the heart of this lesson.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe the structure of an electrolytic cell
  • Explain the migration of ions during electrolysis
  • Define preferential discharge
  • Apply the three factors: position in the electrochemical series, concentration, and nature of the electrode
  • Distinguish between inert and active (reactive) electrodes
  • Predict products at anode and cathode for a given setup

Electrolytic Cell — Structure & Function
MoE Textbook §2.2.1
📌 Electrochemical Cell

An electrochemical cell is a device that can generate electrical energy from a chemical reaction, or use electrical energy to drive a chemical reaction. There are two types:

• Galvanic (Voltaic) cells — spontaneous reaction → produce electricity (Lesson 2.10)
• Electrolytic cells — electrical energy supplied → drive non-spontaneous reaction (this lesson)

📌 Electrolytic Cell

An electrolytic cell is an electrochemical cell that uses an external source of electrical energy to force a non-spontaneous redox reaction to occur.

Structure & Charge Sign Convention

Electrolytic Cell — Basic Setup
ElectrodeChargeProcessIons attracted
CathodeNegative (−)ReductionCations (+)
AnodePositive (+)OxidationAnions (−)
📝 Key terminology reminder

In both galvanic and electrolytic cells:
• Cathode = reduction always (Red Cat)
• Anode = oxidation always (An Ox)
But the charge signs are opposite between the two cell types!

📌 What happens during electrolysis (MoE, p. 75)

• Cations migrate to the cathode, take electrons, and are reduced to neutral atoms
• Anions give up electrons to the anode and are oxidized to neutral atoms
• Electrons released by anions travel through the external circuit to complete the flow

🎯
Preferential Discharge
MoE Textbook §2.2.2
📌 Definition (MoE Textbook, p. 70)

During electrolysis, if more than one cation or more than one anion arrive at an electrode, only one of them preferentially gets discharged. This choice among competing ions is called preferential discharge.

📌 The three factors

Which ion is discharged first depends on:

1. Its position in the electrochemical (reactivity) series
2. Its concentration in the solution
3. The nature of the electrode (inert vs active)

Conceptual View
📊
Factor 1 — Position in the Electrochemical Series
The most important factor
📌 The Rule (MoE Textbook, p. 81)

The ions that are lower in the electrochemical series get discharged in preference to those above them.

📝 Worked example (from textbook)

If a solution contains both K⁺ and Cu²⁺:
• Cu²⁺ is lower in the series → Cu²⁺ accepts electrons first → copper metal is deposited
• K⁺ stays in solution

Similarly, if a solution has Cl⁻ and OH⁻, the OH⁻ (lower) is preferentially discharged.

💧
Factor 2 — Concentration of Ions
When concentration overrides position
📌 The Rule (MoE Textbook, p. 81)

The higher the concentration of an anion in the electrolytic solution, the greater its probability of being discharged at the anode.

The Classic Example — NaCl Solution

A sodium chloride solution contains two competing anions at the anode:

IonPosition in seriesWhat happens
OH⁻Lower (more easily discharged)Discharged if concentration is normal
Cl⁻Higher (harder to discharge)Discharged if concentration is high
💧 Dilute NaCl

OH⁻ is discharged preferentially.
Product at anode: O₂ gas
Product at cathode: H₂ gas
(Essentially electrolysis of water)

🧂 Concentrated NaCl (brine)

Cl⁻ concentration is high enough that it overrides the position rule.
Product at anode: Cl₂ gas
Product at cathode: H₂ gas

📝 The lesson

Position in the series is the default rule, but concentration can override it. When Cl⁻ is very concentrated, it gets discharged in preference to OH⁻ even though OH⁻ is lower in the series.

🔬
Factor 3 — Nature of the Electrode
Inert vs Active
🔵 Inert (Unreactive) Electrode

Made of graphite, platinum, gold
Does not take part in the reaction
Only the ions in solution are discharged
Anode itself does not dissolve

🔴 Active (Reactive) Electrode

Made of Cu, Ag, Ni, Zn
Does take part in the reaction
Anode itself dissolves instead of anions being discharged
Used in electroplating and electrorefining

The Classic Example — CuSO₄ Solution

Electrode TypeAnode ReactionCathode ReactionNet Effect
Inert (Pt / graphite) 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu(s) Cu deposited at cathode; O₂ at anode; solution becomes acidic (H₂SO₄ forms)
Active (Cu electrode) Cu(s) → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu(s) Copper is transferred from anode to cathode; solution concentration stays constant
📝 The key insight

With an active anode, the anode itself loses electrons and dissolves — so no anions are discharged. This is the basis for electrolytic refining and electroplating.

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1Identify Electrodes in a Cell
Problem: In an electrolytic cell with a 9 V battery, which electrode is the cathode and which is the anode? What process happens at each?
Definition
The electrode connected to the negative terminal of the battery is the cathode
Cathode
(−) charged → reduction occurs; cations migrate here
Anode
(+) charged → oxidation occurs; anions migrate here
✅ Cathode (−) = reduction; Anode (+) = oxidation
Example 2Preferential Discharge — Cu²⁺ vs Na⁺
Problem: An aqueous solution contains both Cu²⁺ and Na⁺ ions. Which is discharged at the cathode? Explain.
Check series
Na⁺ is very high in the series (very reactive metal); Cu²⁺ is much lower (unreactive metal)
Apply rule
Lower in series is discharged first → Cu²⁺ is discharged
Half-reaction
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu(s) (copper metal deposits on cathode)
✅ Cu²⁺ is discharged; Na⁺ stays in solution
Example 3Concentration Override
Problem: In concentrated NaCl solution, why is Cl₂ (not O₂) produced at the anode, even though OH⁻ is lower in the electrochemical series?
Normal rule
By position, OH⁻ would be discharged first (lower in the series)
But…
In concentrated brine, [Cl⁻] is very high → far more Cl⁻ ions reach the anode than OH⁻
Result
2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ — the concentration factor overrides the position rule
✅ High [Cl⁻] overrides the series position, so Cl₂ is produced
Example 4Inert vs Active Electrode
Problem: Compare the electrolysis of CuSO₄ solution using (a) platinum electrodes and (b) copper electrodes.
(a) Pt — inert
Anode: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻
Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)
(b) Cu — active
Anode: Cu(s) → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ (anode dissolves)
Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)
✅ Pt: O₂ at anode + Cu at cathode. Cu: Cu transferred from anode to cathode (concentration unchanged)
Example 5Predict Products — Dilute NaCl
Problem: Predict the products at the anode and cathode when dilute NaCl solution is electrolyzed with graphite electrodes.
Ions present
Na⁺, Cl⁻, H⁺, OH⁻
Cathode competition
Na⁺ vs H⁺ — H⁺ is much lower in series → H⁺ is discharged → H₂ gas
Anode competition
Cl⁻ vs OH⁻ — in dilute solution, OH⁻ is lower → OH⁻ discharged → O₂ gas
✅ Cathode: H₂ gas; Anode: O₂ gas (effectively electrolysis of water)

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 2.5
✅ Key Points — Electrolytic Cells
  • An electrochemical cell either generates or uses electrical energy from redox reactions
  • Electrolytic cell uses external electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous reaction
  • In electrolytic cells: Cathode (−) = reduction, Anode (+) = oxidation
  • Preferential discharge = the choice among competing ions at an electrode
  • Factor 1: The ion lower in the electrochemical series is discharged first
  • Factor 2: Higher concentration increases the chance of discharge (can override Factor 1)
  • Factor 3: An active electrode dissolves at the anode instead of anions being discharged
  • Inert electrodes (Pt, graphite) do not participate; only ions are discharged
  • In CuSO₄: Pt electrodes → O₂ + Cu; Cu electrodes → Cu transferred anode to cathode
  • In dilute NaCl: H₂ at cathode, O₂ at anode; in concentrated NaCl: H₂ at cathode, Cl₂ at anode
  • Reference: MoE Chemistry Grade 12 Student Textbook, §2.2.1–2.2.2 (pp. 69–82)
