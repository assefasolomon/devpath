📈 Faraday's Second Law of Electrolysis

When the same charge passes through several cells in series, different masses are deposited — but they are all in the same ratio as their equivalent weights. This is Faraday's Second Law, and it lets you predict any metal from any other.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Faraday's First Law tells you about one cell. His Second Law tells you how multiple cells behave together when they share the same current. It's the reason you can measure silver deposited in one cell and immediately calculate the copper deposited in another — without ever using Faraday's constant.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • State Faraday's Second Law
  • Explain the concept of cells connected in series
  • Apply m₁/E₁ = m₂/E₂ to any two cells sharing charge
  • Compute equivalent weights from molar masses and valencies
  • Calculate the mass deposited in one cell given data from another
  • Distinguish clearly between Faraday's First and Second Laws
📜
The Law Statement
MoE Textbook, p. 84
📌 Faraday's Second Law of Electrolysis

The masses of different substances, liberated or dissolved by the same amount of electricity, are proportional to their equivalent masses.

📌 Restated in detail

When the same quantity of electricity is passed through several electrolytic solutions connected in series, the masses of the different materials liberated or dissolved (m₁, m₂, m₃, …) at the respective electrodes are in the ratio of their equivalent masses (E₁, E₂, E₃, …).

m ∝ E
mass is proportional to equivalent weight (for the same charge)
m₁ / E₁ = m₂ / E₂ = m₃ / E₃
★ the master equation — one ratio to solve them all
📝 What this really means

If you deposit 1 g of Ag in one cell and 0.294 g of Cu in another when they share the same charge, then the ratio 1 / 0.294 must equal 108 / 31.75 — the ratio of their equivalent weights. That's the whole law in one sentence.

🔗
Cells in Series
The experimental setup behind the law

To test Faraday's Second Law, you connect two or more electrolytic cells in series so that the same current flows through all of them for the same time. This guarantees they all receive the same charge Q.

Three Cells in Series
In series, the same current I flows through all cells for the same time t → all receive the same charge Q = It.
📌 Why series connection matters

The same charge Q passes through each cell. But because different ions have different valencies and molar masses, the masses deposited will differ — in exact proportion to their equivalent weights.

🧮
All the Formulas
Equivalent weights and ratios
E = M / n
equivalent weight = molar mass ÷ valency
m₁ / E₁ = m₂ / E₂
★ the Second Law — same charge flowing through both cells
m₂ = m₁ × (E₂ / E₁)
rearranged — find the unknown mass from the known mass

📝 Remember the valency!

The most common mistake is using the wrong value of n. For example:
• Fe²⁺ → n = 2 (not 3)
• Cu²⁺ → n = 2 (not 1)
• Ag⁺ → n = 1
Always check the half-reaction.

📝
Problem-Solving Steps
A repeatable procedure
  1. Identify both substances being deposited in the two cells.
  2. Write the half-reaction for each and determine the value of n (electrons transferred per ion).
  3. Compute the equivalent weights E₁ = M₁/n₁ and E₂ = M₂/n₂.
  4. Set up the ratio m₁/E₁ = m₂/E₂ and substitute all known values.
  5. Solve for the unknown and check that units and significant figures are correct.
📝 Common pitfalls

• Using molar mass M where you should use equivalent weight E
• Using the wrong valency n for an ion (e.g. n = 1 for Cu²⁺)
• Mixing up which mass belongs to which cell
• Forgetting that the law only applies when the same charge passes through each cell (i.e. cells in series)

Worked Examples
Textbook Example 2.5 + more
Example 1Textbook Example 2.5 — Cu from Ag
Problem: Calculate the mass of copper deposited by the electrolysis of CuSO₄ solution placed in series with a silver nitrate solution, when 0.108 g of Ag is being deposited.
Step 1
Write half-reactions and find E for each:
Ag⁺ + 1e⁻ → Ag   → E(Ag) = 108 / 1 = 108 g/eq
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu   → E(Cu) = 63.5 / 2 = 31.75 g/eq
Step 2
Given: m(Ag) = 0.108 g. Find: m(Cu)?
Step 3
Apply the Second Law: m(Ag) / E(Ag) = m(Cu) / E(Cu)
Step 4
Rearrange: m(Cu) = E(Cu) × m(Ag) / E(Ag)
m(Cu) = (31.75 × 0.108) / 108
m(Cu) = 3.429 / 108 ≈ 0.032 g
✅ Mass of Cu deposited = 0.032 g
Example 2Ag from Al (textbook Exercise 2.7 Q1)
Problem: Two cells are connected in series. One contains AlCl₃ and the other AgNO₃. What mass of Ag is deposited when 18 g of Al is deposited?
E values
Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al   → E(Al) = 27 / 3 = 9 g/eq
Ag⁺ + 1e⁻ → Ag   → E(Ag) = 108 / 1 = 108 g/eq
Set up ratio
m(Al) / E(Al) = m(Ag) / E(Ag)
Solve
m(Ag) = E(Ag) × m(Al) / E(Al)
m(Ag) = (108 × 18) / 9
m(Ag) = 1944 / 9 = 216 g
✅ Mass of Ag deposited = 216 g
Example 30.2 mol e⁻ through 3 cells (textbook Exercise 2.7 Q2)
Problem: 0.2 moles of electrons are passed through three electrolytic cells in series containing silver ions, zinc ions, and iron(III) ions. How many grams of each metal will be deposited at the cathode?
Key insight
0.2 mol of e⁻ is the same charge flowing through all 3 cells. Use the charge-to-mole ratio for each ion.
Ag⁺ (n = 1)
mol Ag = 0.2 / 1 = 0.2 mol
m(Ag) = 0.2 × 108 = 21.6 g
Zn²⁺ (n = 2)
mol Zn = 0.2 / 2 = 0.1 mol
m(Zn) = 0.1 × 65.4 = 6.54 g
Fe³⁺ (n = 3)
mol Fe = 0.2 / 3 ≈ 0.0667 mol
m(Fe) = 0.0667 × 56 ≈ 3.73 g
✅ Ag = 21.6 g  |  Zn = 6.54 g  |  Fe = 3.73 g
Example 4Find the Unknown Equivalent Weight
Problem: In two cells in series, 0.559 g of metal X is deposited in the first cell while 1.080 g of silver is deposited in the second cell. If M(X) = 56 g/mol, what is the valency of X?
E(Ag)
E(Ag) = 108 g/eq
Ratio
m(X) / E(X) = m(Ag) / E(Ag)
0.559 / E(X) = 1.080 / 108
Solve E(X)
E(X) = 0.559 × 108 / 1.080 = 55.9 / 1.08 ≈ 55.9 / 1.08 = 55.9 / 1.08
Cleanly: E(X) = 0.559 × 108 / 1.080 = 55.9 / 1.08 = 55.9 / 1.08 = ≈ 27.95 g/eq wait — this is M/2
E(X) = 56 / n → n = 56 / 27.95 ≈ 2
✅ Valency of X = 2
Example 5Predict Mass in a Third Cell
Problem: A current passes through three cells in series containing CuSO₄, AgNO₃, and FeCl₃. If 0.635 g of Cu is deposited in the first cell, what masses of Ag and Fe are deposited in the other two?
E values
E(Cu) = 63.5 / 2 = 31.75
E(Ag) = 108 / 1 = 108
E(Fe) = 56 / 3 ≈ 18.67
Find m(Ag)
m(Ag) = m(Cu) × E(Ag) / E(Cu)
= 0.635 × 108 / 31.75 ≈ 2.16 g
Find m(Fe)
m(Fe) = m(Cu) × E(Fe) / E(Cu)
= 0.635 × 18.67 / 31.75 ≈ 0.373 g
✅ Ag = 2.16 g  |  Fe = 0.373 g

How to know which one to use
FeatureFaraday's First LawFaraday's Second Law
FocusMass vs charge in one cellMass ratios across multiple cells
Main formulam = z Q = M I t / (n F)m₁ / E₁ = m₂ / E₂
Uses F (96,500 C/mol)?Yes — alwaysNo — F cancels out
Uses current & time?Yes, if given I and tNo — only masses and equivalent weights
Typical question"How much Cu is deposited by 2 A for 30 min?""If 1 g Ag deposits, how much Cu deposits in the same series?"
📝 Quick decision rule

One cell only? → Use Faraday's First Law (m = MIt/nF).
Two or more cells in series? → Use Faraday's Second Law (m₁/E₁ = m₂/E₂).

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 2.8
✅ Key Points — Faraday's Second Law
  • Faraday's Second Law: masses of different substances deposited by the same charge are proportional to their equivalent weights
  • Formula: m ∝ E   →   m₁ / E₁ = m₂ / E₂
  • Applies when cells are connected in series — same current, same time, same charge Q = It
  • Equivalent weight: E = M / n where M is molar mass and n is valence
  • F (Faraday constant) cancels out in Second Law calculations — you never need it
  • Common E values: Ag = 108, Cu = 31.75, Zn = 32.7, Al = 9, Fe³⁺ = 18.67, Ca = 20, Na = 23
  • Rearranged form: m₂ = m₁ × E₂ / E₁
  • Moles deposited = (moles of e⁻) / n
  • Use 1st Law for one cell with I and t; use 2nd Law for two or more cells in series
  • Reference: MoE Chemistry Grade 12 Student Textbook, §2.3.2 (pp. 84–85)
