💧 Electrolysis of Selected Aqueous Solutions

Five classic electrolysis setups every Grade 12 student must know: concentrated NaCl, dilute NaCl, dilute H₂SO₄, CuSO₄ with inert electrodes, and CuSO₄ with copper electrodes. This lesson walks through each one with full half-reactions and overall reactions.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

In an aqueous solution, water itself can be oxidized or reduced — so both the ions from the solute and the water molecules compete at each electrode. This makes predictions trickier than for molten salts. This lesson works through the five most important cases from your textbook.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Write the electrode half-reactions and overall reaction for each classic case
  • Predict which products form at cathode and anode
  • Explain why concentration matters for NaCl solutions
  • Describe the effect of electrode type on the CuSO₄ electrolysis
  • Explain why dilute NaCl and dilute H₂SO₄ essentially decompose water
🧂 Concentrated NaCl 💧 Dilute NaCl 🔬 Dilute H₂SO₄ 🔵 CuSO₄ (Inert) 🔴 CuSO₄ (Cu)
🧂
Case 1 — Concentrated NaCl (Brine)
Cl₂ at anode, H₂ at cathode

In concentrated NaCl solution there are four ions: Na⁺, Cl⁻, H⁺, OH⁻ (the last two from water's autoionization). All four compete at the electrodes.

Concentrated NaCl Electrolysis

Why Cl₂ and H₂?

📌 Cathode — H₂ (not Na)

Na⁺ and H⁺ both migrate to the cathode. The reduction potential of water (−0.83 V) is much less negative than that of Na⁺ (−2.71 V), so H⁺ is reduced in preference to Na⁺.

📌 Anode — Cl₂ (not O₂)

OH⁻ and Cl⁻ both migrate to the anode. Normally OH⁻ is preferred (lower in series). But when Cl⁻ is very concentrated, enough Cl⁻ ions reach the anode to override the position rule — so Cl₂ is produced.

Cathode (Reduction)
2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → H₂(g)
Anode (Oxidation)
2Cl⁻(aq) → Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻
Overall
2H⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq) → H₂(g) + Cl₂(g)
📝 Industrial by-product

As Cl⁻ is consumed and OH⁻ accumulates, NaOH builds up in the solution. Evaporating the leftover solution gives useful sodium hydroxide — this is the basis of the chlor-alkali industry (see Lesson 2.9).

💧
Case 2 — Dilute NaCl
Essentially water decomposition

Dilute NaCl solution contains the same four ions. But now Cl⁻ is at low concentration, so the position rule governs the anode — and OH⁻ is discharged instead of Cl⁻.

Which ions are discharged?

ElectrodeCompeting ionsPreferentially dischargedProduct
Cathode (−)Na⁺, H⁺H⁺H₂ gas
Anode (+)Cl⁻, OH⁻OH⁻O₂ gas
Cathode (Reduction)
4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ → 2H₂(g)
Anode (Oxidation)
4OH⁻(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g) + 4e⁻
Overall (essentially decomposition of water)
2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g)
📝 Same result as dilute H₂SO₄

Electrolysis of both dilute NaCl and dilute H₂SO₄ essentially decomposes water into hydrogen and oxygen. The ions of the solute are spectators in the net reaction.

🔬
Case 3 — Dilute H₂SO₄
H₂ at cathode, O₂ at anode

Dilute sulfuric acid contains H⁺, SO₄²⁻ from H₂SO₄ and H⁺, OH⁻ from water. At the anode, two negative ions compete: OH⁻ and SO₄²⁻.

📌 Preferential discharge

• Cathode: H⁺ is discharged → H₂ gas
• Anode: OH⁻ is discharged in preference to SO₄²⁻, because OH⁻ is lower in the electrochemical series than SO₄²⁻

Anode (Oxidation)
4OH⁻(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g) + 4e⁻
Cathode (Reduction)
4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ → 2H₂(g)
Overall
2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g)
📝 Textbook note (p. 86)

The electrolysis of dilute NaCl and dilute H₂SO₄ solutions both result in the decomposition of water to oxygen and hydrogen gases. This is why these two cases are often presented together.

🔵
Case 4 — CuSO₄ with Inert Electrodes
Cu at cathode, O₂ at anode

Aqueous CuSO₄ contains Cu²⁺, SO₄²⁻ from the salt and H⁺, OH⁻ from water. Using platinum or graphite electrodes (which don't participate), we get:

📌 Why Cu²⁺ is discharged at the cathode

Cu²⁺ has a higher (less negative) reduction potential than H⁺, so it is preferentially reduced at the cathode.

📌 Why OH⁻ is discharged at the anode

OH⁻ has a higher oxidation potential than SO₄²⁻, so it is preferentially oxidized at the anode.

At Cathode (Reduction)
2Cu²⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ → 2Cu(s)
At Anode (Oxidation)
4OH⁻(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g) + 4e⁻
Overall Reaction
2Cu²⁺(aq) + 4OH⁻(aq) → 2Cu(s) + 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g)
Observations with Inert Electrodes

• Cathode: reddish-brown copper metal deposits on the surface
• Anode: oxygen gas bubbles rise from the surface
• Solution: becomes acidic because H⁺ and SO₄²⁻ left behind form H₂SO₄

📝 Solution becomes more acidic

As Cu²⁺ is removed and OH⁻ is consumed, H⁺ and SO₄²⁻ build up → H₂SO₄ forms. This is why the solution pH drops during the electrolysis.

🔴
Case 5 — CuSO₄ with Copper Electrodes
Anode dissolves; copper is refined

When we replace the inert electrodes with clean copper plates, the anode reaction changes completely. No oxygen is produced — instead, the copper anode dissolves.

🔵 Inert Anode (Pt / graphite)

OH⁻ discharged → O₂ gas
Cu²⁺ discharged at cathode → Cu deposits
Solution becomes acidic

🔴 Active Anode (Cu)

Cu anode dissolves → Cu²⁺
Cu²⁺ discharged at cathode → Cu deposits
Solution concentration stays constant

Anode (Oxidation — active Cu electrode)
Cu(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻
Cathode (Reduction)
Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)
📌 Key observation (MoE Textbook, p. 87)

During this electrolysis, the mass of copper gained at the cathode is equal to the mass lost at the anode. This is the essence of electrolytic refining of copper.

📝 Industrial significance

This setup is used industrially to refine copper: impure copper is made the anode, pure copper the cathode, and CuSO₄ solution the electrolyte. Impurities fall to the bottom as "anode mud". (See Lesson 2.9.)

Experiment 2.2 — Electrolysis of NaCl
Textbook p. 85
🧪 Investigating Electrolysis of Sodium Chloride Solution
Objective: To electrolyze sodium chloride solution and observe the reactions occurring at the electrodes.

Apparatus & Chemicals: Water, sodium chloride, beakers, measuring cylinder, graphite electrodes, batteries, connecting wires.

Procedure:
  • Prepare 0.1 M sodium chloride solution.
  • Transfer the solution to a beaker.
  • Immerse the graphite electrodes in the sodium chloride solution.
  • Arrange the setup as shown in Figure 2.5 (9 V voltage source across two graphite rods).
Results and Discussion (from textbook):
  • What do you observe at the surface of the electrodes?
  • Identify the cathode and anode.
  • Name the gases evolved at the cathode and anode.
  • Write the equations for anode and cathode reactions.
📝 Expected observations (0.1 M = dilute)

Since the solution is only 0.1 M, Cl⁻ is not concentrated enough to override the position rule. You would see H₂ bubbles at the cathode and O₂ bubbles at the anode — essentially electrolysis of water, not brine.

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1Concentrated vs Dilute NaCl
Problem: Compare the products formed during electrolysis of concentrated NaCl and dilute NaCl using inert electrodes.
Conc. NaCl
Cathode: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂
Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
Dilute NaCl
Cathode: 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂
Anode: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻
✅ Conc.: H₂ + Cl₂ (with NaOH by-product). Dilute: H₂ + O₂ (water decomposition)
Example 2Why OH⁻ over SO₄²⁻?
Problem: In the electrolysis of dilute H₂SO₄, explain why OH⁻ is discharged at the anode rather than SO₄²⁻.
Position rule
OH⁻ is lower in the electrochemical series than SO₄²⁻
Consequence
The lower ion is preferentially discharged → OH⁻ oxidizes to O₂
Half-reaction
4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻
✅ OH⁻ discharges; SO₄²⁻ stays in solution as a spectator
Example 3CuSO₄ with Pt Electrodes
Problem: Write the anode and cathode reactions for electrolysis of aqueous CuSO₄ using platinum electrodes. What happens to the pH of the solution?
Cathode
2Cu²⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2Cu(s) — copper deposits
Anode
4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ — oxygen gas forms
pH change
H⁺ and SO₄²⁻ remain → H₂SO₄ builds up → pH drops (more acidic)
✅ Cu at cathode; O₂ at anode; solution becomes acidic
Example 4CuSO₄ with Cu Electrodes
Problem: What is the net result of electrolyzing CuSO₄ solution using copper electrodes?
Anode
Cu(s) → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ — copper anode dissolves
Cathode
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu(s) — copper deposits
Net effect
Pure copper is transferred from anode to cathode; solution concentration is unchanged
✅ Copper is refined — mass lost at anode = mass gained at cathode
Example 5Why Two Solutions Give the Same Products
Problem: Explain why dilute NaCl and dilute H₂SO₄ both give H₂ at the cathode and O₂ at the anode.
Common factor
Both solutions contain water, which supplies H⁺ and OH⁻
Cathode
H⁺ is lower in the series than Na⁺ → H⁺ discharged in both → H₂
Anode
OH⁻ is lower in the series than both Cl⁻ (dilute) and SO₄²⁻ → OH⁻ discharged in both → O₂
✅ In both cases, the ions of the solute act as spectators — water is what actually decomposes

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 2.6
✅ Key Points — Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions
  • In aqueous solutions, both the solute ions and water (H⁺ / OH⁻) compete at each electrode
  • Concentrated NaCl: H₂ at cathode, Cl₂ at anode (concentration overrides position rule); NaOH is a by-product
  • Dilute NaCl: H₂ at cathode, O₂ at anode; net reaction = 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂
  • Dilute H₂SO₄: Same result as dilute NaCl — 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂
  • CuSO₄ with inert (Pt) electrodes: Cu deposits at cathode, O₂ evolves at anode; solution becomes acidic (H₂SO₄ forms)
  • CuSO₄ with copper electrodes: Copper anode dissolves; copper deposits on cathode; concentration unchanged — the basis of copper refining
  • In every case, lower-in-series wins unless concentration overrides
  • Active electrodes can replace the anion discharge entirely at the anode
  • Reference: MoE Chemistry Grade 12 Student Textbook, §2.2.3 (pp. 74–88)
