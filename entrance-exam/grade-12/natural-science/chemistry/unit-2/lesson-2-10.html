🔋 Galvanic Cells & the Daniell Cell

When a spontaneous redox reaction is split into two half-cells, the electrons flowing through the external wire do useful work. This is a galvanic cell — the principle behind every battery, from your phone to the Daniell cell that powered the first telegraphs.

📖 Reading: ~22 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources

Introduction
What you will learn

When zinc metal is dropped into copper sulfate solution, the reaction happens directly — heat is released, but no useful work is done. If instead we physically separate the two reactants and force the electrons to travel through an external wire, we can make the same reaction do electrical work. That's the idea behind every battery.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define galvanic (voltaic) cell
  • Describe the structure of the Daniell cell (Zn-Cu)
  • Explain the function of the salt bridge
  • Identify anode and cathode in a galvanic cell
  • Write cell notation (cell diagram) for any galvanic cell
  • Distinguish galvanic from electrolytic cells
💡
What is a Galvanic Cell?
Spontaneous redox → electricity
📌 Definition (MoE Textbook, p. 101)

A galvanic cell (also called a voltaic cell) is an experimental apparatus for generating electricity through the use of a spontaneous redox reaction.

📌 The essential idea

When Zn is placed in CuSO₄ solution, the reaction Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu happens directly — the electrons transfer from Zn to Cu²⁺ on contact, releasing heat but doing no useful work.

But if we physically separate the oxidizing agent (Cu²⁺) from the reducing agent (Zn), the electrons must travel through an external wire. This sets up a constant flow of electrons — an electric current that can drive a motor or light a bulb.

📌 Key terminology (Memorize this!)

• Anode = electrode where oxidation occurs (in galvanic cell: negative electrode)
• Cathode = electrode where reduction occurs (in galvanic cell: positive electrode)
• Electrons flow from anode → external wire → cathode

📝 Memory aid

An Ox — Anode = Oxidation
Red Cat — Reduction at the Cathode
This holds true in both galvanic and electrolytic cells.

🔋
The Daniell Cell
The classic Zn-Cu galvanic cell

The most famous galvanic cell is the Daniell cell, invented by John Frederic Daniell in 1836. It consists of a zinc electrode in ZnSO₄ solution and a copper electrode in CuSO₄ solution, connected by a salt bridge and an external wire.

The Daniell Cell — Full Structure
The Daniell cell converts the energy of the Zn + Cu²⁺ reaction into electrical energy. The salt bridge maintains charge neutrality in both compartments.

Reactions at each electrode

Oxidation (Zn electrode = anode)
Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻
Reduction (Cu electrode = cathode)
Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)
Overall Cell Reaction
Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)    E°cell = +1.10 V
📌 Why this cell works

Zinc loses electrons more readily than copper, so zinc atoms lose electrons to form Zn²⁺. These electrons flow through the external wire to the copper electrode, where they reduce Cu²⁺ to copper metal. The net effect: zinc reacts with copper ions to produce zinc ions and copper metal, and electric current flows through the external wire.

📝 What you'd observe

• Zinc electrode: slowly dissolves (corrodes)
• Copper electrode: gains mass (copper deposits on it)
• ZnSO₄ solution: Zn²⁺ concentration increases
• CuSO₄ solution: blue color fades as Cu²⁺ is consumed
• Voltmeter: reads about 1.10 V

🌉
The Salt Bridge
Why we need it
📌 Definition (MoE Textbook, p. 102)

A salt bridge is an inverted U-tube containing an inert electrolyte solution (such as KCl, KNO₃, or NH₄NO₃) whose ions will not react with other ions in solution or with the electrodes.

📌 Two main functions

1. Completes the electrical circuit — provides a conducting path between the two solutions

2. Maintains charge neutrality — allows ions to migrate between the two compartments, preventing buildup of charge

What happens without a salt bridge?

Charge Buildup Without a Salt Bridge
📝 Textbook explanation (p. 102)

Without the salt bridge connecting the two solutions, the buildup of positive charge in the anode compartment (due to Zn²⁺ formation) and negative charge in the cathode compartment (created when Cu²⁺ is reduced) would quickly prevent the cell from operating.

Ion movement with a salt bridge

IonMigrates towardReason
Cations (K⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺)Cathode (right)Attracted to the positive-charge deficit side
Anions (Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻)Anode (left)Attracted to the positive-charge buildup side

Cell Notation (Cell Diagram)
How chemists write a galvanic cell in one line

The cell diagram (or cell notation) is a compact way to represent the components of a galvanic cell in one line. For the Daniell cell:

Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) ‖ Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s)
anode → cathode (electrons flow left to right in the external wire)

Rules for Writing Cell Notation

SymbolMeaning
| (single vertical line)Phase boundary (e.g., between solid electrode and aqueous solution)
‖ (double vertical line)Salt bridge — separates the two half-cell compartments
, (comma)Separates different species in the same phase (e.g., MnO₄⁻(aq), H⁺(aq), Mn²⁺(aq))
📌 The universal pattern

anode | anode electrolyte ‖ cathode electrolyte | cathode

• Anode half-cell is always written on the LEFT
• Cathode half-cell is always written on the RIGHT
• Electrodes appear at the far left and far right

Including Concentrations

If the concentrations of dissolved components are known (e.g., 1 M), include them in parentheses:

Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq, 1 M) ‖ Cu²⁺(aq, 1 M) | Cu(s)
This is the standard-state version of the Daniell cell

A More Complex Example (Textbook, p. 103)

Oxidation half-reaction
2I⁻(aq) → I₂(s) + 2e⁻
Reduction half-reaction
MnO₄⁻(aq) + 8H⁺(aq) + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺(aq) + 4H₂O(l)

The cell notation for this cell (with graphite electrodes on both sides):

graphite | I⁻(aq) | I₂(s) ‖ MnO₄⁻(aq), H⁺(aq), Mn²⁺(aq) | graphite
Note the commas between species in the same aqueous solution
📝 Ions in the salt bridge are NOT included

KCl, KNO₃, and other salt bridge ions do not appear in the notation because they are not part of the cell reaction.

⚖️
Galvanic vs Electrolytic Cells
Textbook Table 2.4
🟢 Galvanic (Voltaic) Cell

• Spontaneous redox reaction
• Converts chemical → electrical energy
• Anode is negative (−)
• Cathode is positive (+)
• Electrons from the oxidizing species itself
• ΔG < 0 (releases energy)
• Example: Daniell cell

🔴 Electrolytic Cell

• Non-spontaneous redox reaction
• Uses electrical → chemical energy
• Anode is positive (+)
• Cathode is negative (−)
• Electrons from an external source (battery)
• ΔG > 0 (requires energy)
• Example: electrolysis of water

FeatureGalvanicElectrolytic
Reaction typeSpontaneousNon-spontaneous
Energy conversionChemical → ElectricalElectrical → Chemical
Anode charge− (negative)+ (positive)
Cathode charge+ (positive)− (negative)
Electron sourceOxidation reaction itselfExternal battery
ΔG< 0> 0
📝 What stays the same

In both cell types:
• Anode = oxidation (An Ox)
• Cathode = reduction (Red Cat)
Only the charge signs of the electrodes flip.

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1Identify Anode and Cathode
Problem: In the Daniell cell Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s), identify which electrode is the anode and which is the cathode. Explain.
Oxidation
Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ (electrons released) → this is oxidation
Reduction
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (electrons consumed) → this is reduction
Assign
Oxidation happens at the anode; reduction at the cathode
✅ Anode = Zn electrode (−); Cathode = Cu electrode (+)
Example 2Direction of Electron Flow
Problem: In the Daniell cell, in which direction do electrons flow through the external wire? In which direction do cations move through the salt bridge?
Electrons
Electrons flow from the anode (Zn) to the cathode (Cu)
Cations in salt bridge
Cations (K⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺) move toward the cathode side — they travel in the same direction as the electrons would if they could pass through solution
Anions in salt bridge
Anions (Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻) move toward the anode side
✅ Electrons: Zn → Cu (through wire). Cations: toward cathode. Anions: toward anode
Example 3Write Cell Notation
Problem: Write the cell notation for a galvanic cell based on the reaction: Cr(s) + 3Ag⁺(aq) → Cr³⁺(aq) + 3Ag(s). Assume solution concentrations are 0.20 M.
Identify anode
Cr → Cr³⁺ + 3e⁻ (oxidation) → Cr is the anode (left side)
Identify cathode
Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag (reduction) → Ag is the cathode (right side)
Write notation
Cr(s) | Cr³⁺(0.20 M) ‖ Ag⁺(0.20 M) | Ag(s)
✅ Cr(s) | Cr³⁺(0.20 M) ‖ Ag⁺(0.20 M) | Ag(s)
Example 4Explain the Salt Bridge's Role
Problem: Explain why removing the salt bridge from a Daniell cell would stop the current.
Without salt bridge
The two solutions are physically separated → the circuit is incomplete → no continuous flow of ions
Charge buildup
• Anode side: Zn²⁺ builds up → positive charge
• Cathode side: SO₄²⁻ builds up as Cu²⁺ is consumed → negative charge
Result
The charge buildup opposes further reaction → current stops
✅ The salt bridge allows ions to migrate, maintaining charge neutrality and completing the circuit
Example 5Compare with an Electrolytic Cell
Problem: Both a Daniell cell and the electrolysis of water use electrodes and electrolyte solutions. Give two differences between them.
Difference 1
Reaction type: Daniell is spontaneous; electrolysis of water is non-spontaneous
Difference 2
Energy conversion: Daniell converts chemical → electrical; electrolysis uses electrical → chemical
Difference 3
Electrode charges: Daniell: anode (−), cathode (+). Electrolytic: anode (+), cathode (−)
✅ Galvanic: spontaneous, generates electricity, anode negative. Electrolytic: non-spontaneous, uses electricity, anode positive
