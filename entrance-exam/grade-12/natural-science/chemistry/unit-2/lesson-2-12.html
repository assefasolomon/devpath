🌐 Nernst Equation, Concentration Cells & Corrosion

Real cells don't operate at standard conditions. The Nernst equation corrects for that — and lets you determine pH from a cell potential. Concentration cells exploit concentration differences directly, and corrosion is the same electrochemistry working against us.

📖 Reading: ~28 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources

Introduction
The final piece of Unit 2

Standard electrode potentials assume all concentrations are exactly 1 M and all gases at 1 atm. But real cells — a battery in your phone, a nerve cell, a rusting car — operate at non-standard conditions. The Nernst equation is the bridge between standard values and real behavior. In this final lesson we also see how the same electrochemistry drives concentration cells and corrosion.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • State and apply the Nernst equation
  • Calculate cell potential at non-standard concentrations
  • Determine pH from cell potential
  • Explain concentration cells and calculate their EMF
  • Describe the electrochemistry of iron corrosion
  • List and explain corrosion prevention methods
🧮
The Nernst Equation
Cell potential at any concentration
📌 Why we need it

Standard potentials E° only apply at 1 M / 1 atm / 25 °C. In real cells, concentrations change as the reaction proceeds. The Nernst equation tells us how Ecell depends on the actual concentrations at any moment.

Ecell = E°cell − (RT / nF) ln Q
the general Nernst equation
Ecell = E°cell − (2.303 RT / nF) log Q
using common logarithms
Ecell = E°cell − (0.0592 / n) log Q
★ at 25 °C — the exam version

What Each Symbol Means

SymbolMeaning
EcellActual cell potential at the given concentrations
E°cellStandard cell potential (from E°cathode − E°anode)
RGas constant = 8.314 J/(mol·K)
TTemperature in Kelvin
nNumber of moles of electrons transferred in the balanced cell reaction
FFaraday constant = 96,500 C/mol
QReaction quotient = [products]/[reactants], each raised to its stoichiometric coefficient
📌 The reaction quotient Q

For a general cell reaction: aA + bB → cC + dD
Q = ([C]c · [D]d) / ([A]a · [B]b)

Pure solids and liquids are NOT included in Q (their activity = 1).
Gases are entered as partial pressures in atm.

📝 What the Nernst equation tells us

As the reaction proceeds, products increase, Q increases, log Q increases, and the correction term subtracts more from E°cell. So Ecell decreases as the cell discharges. Eventually Ecell = 0 and the cell is "dead" — the reaction has reached equilibrium.

🧪
pH from Cell Potential
An electrochemical pH meter

Because cell potential depends on [H⁺], we can measure pH by measuring a cell potential. This is the principle behind the pH meter.

📌 The setup

A hydrogen electrode (bubbled with H₂ at 1 atm) is dipped into the test solution and connected to a standard Zn electrode. The cell is:

Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(1 M) ‖ H⁺(test) | H₂(1 atm) | Pt

Cell Reaction
Zn(s) + 2H⁺(test) → Zn²⁺(1 M) + H₂(1 atm)

The standard cell potential is E°cell = 0.76 V. Applying the Nernst equation and substituting pH = −log[H⁺]:

Ecell = 0.76 − 0.0592 pH
rearranged to solve for pH
pH = (0.76 − Ecell) / 0.0592
measure Ecell → get pH
📝 Why this matters

A simple voltage measurement can give you the pH of a solution to very high accuracy. This is how pH meters work — an internal reference electrode + a glass electrode sensitive to H⁺ + a voltmeter calibrated in pH units.

🔋
Concentration Cells
A cell driven only by concentration difference
📌 Definition (MoE Textbook, p. 123)

A concentration cell is a galvanic cell made from two half-cells composed of the same material but with different ion concentrations. The two solutions are separated, and their concentrations become equal as the cell operates.

📌 Key features

• Both half-cells have the same electrode and same half-reaction
• Therefore E°cell = 0 (both standard potentials are identical — they cancel)
• But because concentrations differ, Ecell ≠ 0
• The dilute solution is the anode (oxidation); the concentrated solution is the cathode (reduction)
• The cell operates until the two concentrations become equal — then Ecell = 0

Worked Example — Cu/Cu²⁺ Concentration Cell

A Cu/Cu²⁺ concentration cell has [Cu²⁺] = 0.10 M in the anode compartment and [Cu²⁺] = 1.0 M in the cathode compartment.

Anode (dilute)
Cu(s) → Cu²⁺(0.10 M) + 2e⁻
Cathode (concentrated)
Cu²⁺(1.0 M) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)

Apply the Nernst equation with E°cell = 0, n = 2:

Ecell = 0 − (0.0592 / 2) log([Cu²⁺]dilute / [Cu²⁺]conc.)
Ecell = − (0.0592 / 2) log(0.10 / 1.0)
Ecell = − (0.0296) × (−1.00)
Ecell = +0.0296 V

📝 How it works

In the anode (dilute) half-cell, Cu atoms lose electrons and Cu²⁺ ions enter solution, making it more concentrated.
In the cathode (concentrated) half-cell, Cu²⁺ ions gain electrons and deposit on the electrode, making it less concentrated.

The cell runs until both concentrations are equal — then Ecell = 0.

Corrosion of Iron (Rusting)
Electrochemistry working against us
📌 Definition

Corrosion is the deterioration of metals by an electrochemical process. The most familiar form is the rusting of iron.

📌 Not a direct reaction

Iron rust is NOT a direct product of iron reacting with oxygen. It arises through a complex electrochemical process that shares features with a voltaic cell — anodic and cathodic regions on the same metal surface.

Step 1 — Anodic Region (Iron Loss)

A strain, dent, or scratch in contact with water becomes the anode. Iron atoms lose electrons and dissolve into solution, creating a small pit.

Anodic Region — Oxidation
Fe(s) → Fe²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻

Step 2 — Cathodic Region (Oxygen Reduction)

The electrons travel through the metal to a different spot (the cathode), where they reduce oxygen in the presence of water.

Cathodic Region — Reduction
O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O(l)

The Overall Reaction (before rust forms)

Sum of the two half-reactions
2Fe(s) + O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) → 2Fe²⁺(aq) + 2H₂O(l)

Step 3 — Rust Formation

The Fe²⁺ ions disperse through the water and react further with O₂ — often away from the original pit — to form the Fe³⁺ in rust.

Rust-Forming Reaction
2Fe²⁺(aq) + ½O₂(g) + (2+n)H₂O(l) → Fe₂O₃·nH₂O(s) + 4H⁺(aq)
📌 The real damage is at the pit

The rust deposit is incidental to the real damage — the actual harm is the loss of iron that weakens the metal. That's why a car can look fine but have structural rust damage hidden under the paint.

Factors That Accelerate Rusting

FactorWhy it speeds up corrosion
Low pH (high [H⁺])H⁺ acts as a catalyst — consumed in one step, regenerated in another
Ionic solutions (salt)Salt improves conductivity of the aqueous medium, accelerating electron transfer
Contact with less active metalFe behaves as anode; loses electrons more readily (e.g. Fe in contact with Cu)
Moisture and oxygenBoth are required — that's why rusting is faster in humid, coastal climates
📝 Why doesn't aluminum corrode as fast as iron?

Aluminum has a greater tendency to oxidize than iron (its E° is more negative), but it forms a thin, tightly adhering layer of Al₂O₃ that protects the metal underneath. Iron's rust layer (Fe₂O₃·nH₂O) is porous and lets water and oxygen through, so it does not protect the iron beneath.

🛡️
Preventing Corrosion of Iron
Textbook pp. 133–135

Most methods aim to prevent rust formation. The key insight: iron is lost only when it acts as the anode. So we protect it by making it behave as the cathode instead.

🎨
Painting / Coating
Physical barrier keeps H₂O and O₂ away from the iron. Downside: if scratched, rust forms under the paint.
⚙️
Alloying (Stainless Steel)
Iron alloyed with chromium forms a protective Cr₂O₃ layer. Chemically inert, resists oxidation.
🔩
Galvanizing (Zn coating)
Zinc is more active than iron, so it acts as a sacrificial anode. Even if scratched, Zn corrodes instead of Fe.
⚡
Cathodic Protection
Connect iron to a more active metal (Zn, Mg) which acts as a sacrificial anode. Iron becomes the cathode and is protected.
🧴
Tin-plating (Tin cans)
A thin layer of tin coats the iron. Tin is less active than iron, so if scratched, iron becomes the anode and corrodes faster.
🔌
Impressed Current
An external DC source drives electrons onto the iron, forcing it to be the cathode. Used for underground pipelines and ship hulls.
📝 The core principle

Corrosion increases when iron is in contact with a less active metal (like Cu) — iron behaves as the anode and loses electrons.
Corrosion decreases when iron is in contact with a more active metal (like Zn or Mg) — the other metal acts as the anode, and iron is protected as the cathode.

📝 The sacrificial anode

When iron is coated with zinc (galvanizing), the zinc is "sacrificed" — it is oxidized instead of the iron. This is why galvanized steel lasts decades even when scratched. Sacrificial anodes are used underwater and underground to protect iron and steel pipes, tanks, and oil rigs.

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1Non-Standard Zn-Cu Cell
Problem: What is the cell potential of the voltaic cell
Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(1.0 × 10⁻⁵ M) ‖ Cu²⁺(0.100 M) | Cu(s)
at 25 °C? E°cell = +1.10 V.
Cell reaction
Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu → n = 2
Reaction quotient
Q = [Zn²⁺] / [Cu²⁺] = (1.0 × 10⁻⁵) / 0.100 = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴
Nernst equation
E = 1.10 − (0.0592/2) log(1.0 × 10⁻⁴)
E = 1.10 − (0.0296)(−4)
E = 1.10 + 0.118
✅ Ecell = +1.22 V
Example 2pH from Cell Potential
Problem: A Zn | H⁺(test) | H₂(1 atm) | Pt cell has a measured EMF of 0.50 V. What is the pH of the test solution?
Formula
pH = (0.76 − Ecell) / 0.0592
Substitute
pH = (0.76 − 0.50) / 0.0592
Compute
pH = 0.26 / 0.0592 ≈ 4.39
✅ pH ≈ 4.4 — the solution is acidic
Example 3Ag Concentration Cell
Problem: A concentration cell consists of two Ag/Ag⁺ half-cells. In half-cell A the concentration is 0.010 M AgNO₃; in half-cell B it is 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ M AgNO₃. What is Ecell at 298 K?
Identify
A = 0.010 M = concentrated (cathode); B = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ M = dilute (anode)
n value
Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag → n = 1
Formula
E = −(0.0592/1) log([dilute]/[conc.])
E = −0.0592 log(4.0×10⁻⁴ / 0.010)
E = −0.0592 log(4.0×10⁻²)
E = −0.0592 × (−1.40) ≈ 0.083 V
✅ Ecell ≈ 0.083 V
Example 4Zn/Ag Cell at Non-Standard Conditions
Problem: What is the cell potential of Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(0.200 M) ‖ Ag⁺(0.002 M) | Ag(s) at 25 °C? E°cell = 1.56 V.
Cell reaction
Zn + 2Ag⁺ → Zn²⁺ + 2Ag → n = 2
Q
Q = [Zn²⁺] / [Ag⁺]² = 0.200 / (0.002)² = 0.200 / 4×10⁻⁶ = 5.0 × 10⁴
Nernst
E = 1.56 − (0.0592/2) log(5.0 × 10⁴)
E = 1.56 − (0.0296)(4.70)
E = 1.56 − 0.139 ≈ 1.42 V
✅ Ecell ≈ 1.42 V (less than E°cell because Ag⁺ is depleted)
Example 5Why Iron Rusts Faster Near the Sea
Problem: Explain why cars near the ocean rust faster than cars in dry inland areas.
Factor 1 — Salt
Sea spray provides NaCl — an ionic solution that greatly improves the conductivity of the water layer on the metal surface
Factor 2 — Moisture
Coastal air is humid — water is essential for the corrosion half-reactions
Factor 3 — O₂
Oxygen from the air completes the cathodic reaction on the iron surface
✅ Salt improves conductivity + humidity provides water + O₂ accepts electrons = rapid rusting

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 2.12
✅ Key Points — Nernst, Concentration Cells & Corrosion
  • The Nernst equation corrects E° for non-standard concentrations
  • General form: E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q
  • At 25 °C: E = E° − (0.0592/n) log Q
  • Q = reaction quotient (products/reactants); pure solids and liquids are omitted
  • Ecell decreases as the reaction proceeds; it reaches zero at equilibrium
  • pH can be measured from cell potential: pH = (0.76 − E) / 0.0592 for the Zn/H⁺ cell
  • Concentration cells have identical half-reactions → E°cell = 0; EMF comes purely from concentration difference
  • Dilute half-cell = anode; concentrated half-cell = cathode
  • Corrosion of iron is electrochemical — anodic region (Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻), cathodic region (O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O)
  • Rust: 2Fe²⁺ + ½O₂ + (2+n)H₂O → Fe₂O₃·nH₂O + 4H⁺
  • Rusting is accelerated by low pH, ions (salt), moisture, and contact with less active metals
  • Prevention: painting, alloying (stainless steel), galvanizing (Zn sacrificial anode), cathodic protection
  • Reference: MoE Chemistry Grade 12 Student Textbook, §2.5 (pp. 118–135)
