🌍 Introduction to Environmental Chemistry

Environmental chemistry is the science of what happens to chemicals once they're released into the environment. This lesson introduces the four spheres of the environment, the natural cycles that keep Earth balanced, and the key terms you need to understand pollution.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

When we release a chemical into the air, water, or soil, where does it go? What does it react with? How does it affect living things? These are the questions of environmental chemistry. In this lesson we set the framework: the four components of the environment, the natural cycles that maintain balance, and the vocabulary of pollution.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define environmental chemistry
  • List the four components of the environment
  • Describe key reactions in each component
  • Explain the natural cycles of water, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and carbon
  • Define key terms: pollutant, contaminant, sink, DO, BOD, TLV
📌
What is Environmental Chemistry?
The definition
📌 Environmental Chemistry (MoE Textbook, p. 242)

Environmental chemistry is a branch of chemical science that deals with the production, transport, reactions, effects, and fates of chemical species in the water, air, terrestrial, and biological environment — and the effects of human activities thereon.

📌 The example of sulfur

Sulfur in coal is oxidized to sulfur dioxide gas, which is released into the atmosphere. SO₂ can be oxidized to SO₃ and eventually converted to sulfuric acid, which falls back to Earth as acid rain. This affects plants (a "receptor") and ends up in a sink — like a body of water or soil.

📝 Activity 5.1 (Textbook p. 241)
Discuss the following in groups and present your opinion to the class:

1. Why is environmental chemistry seen as a multidisciplinary field?
2. Take CO₂ as an example of a typical pollutant. Describe its interaction with the ecosystem and its eventual fate in the environment.
📝 Why it's multidisciplinary

Environmental chemistry combines chemistry, biology, geology, meteorology, and engineering — because chemicals don't respect disciplinary boundaries. A single pollutant may travel through air, water, soil, and living organisms.

🌐
Components of the Environment
The four spheres

The environment consists of four compartments:

🌬️
1. Atmosphere
Layers of gases surrounding Earth — about 78% N₂, 21% O₂, 1% other gases.

Protects from UV, controls temperature, allows visible light

💧
2. Hydrosphere
All water on, under, and above Earth's surface — oceans, seas, rivers, lakes, streams, glaciers, groundwater.

Only ~1% of global water supply is fresh water

🪨
3. Lithosphere
Earth's crust + upper mantle. The crust is the Earth's outer skin — contains rocks and soil.

Most important part to humans and the environment

🌱
4. Biosphere
Realm of living organisms and their interactions with the environment. Divided into smaller units called ecosystems.

Manifests as natural cycles — hydrologic, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur

The Four Spheres — Overview
🌬️
Atmosphere & Its Key Reactions
MoE Textbook, p. 243
📌 Benefits of the atmosphere

• Protective blanket of gas (0–50 km above Earth)
• Absorbs infrared (IR) radiation emitted by the sun and re-emitted from Earth
• Controls Earth's temperature
• Allows transmission of significant radiation from near UV (300 nm) to near IR (2500 nm)
• Blocks damaging UV radiation

Key Reactions in the Atmosphere

I. Nitric oxide → nitrogen dioxide (UV light)
2NO(g) + O₂(g) —[UV]→ 2NO₂(g)
II. Acid rain formation
SO₂, SO₃, NO₂ + rainwater → H₂SO₃, H₂SO₄, HNO₃
III. CFC depletion of ozone layer
CF₂Cl₂ + UV → CF₂Cl· + Cl·
IV. Chlorine free radical attacks ozone
Cl· + O₃ → ClO + O₂
📝 The ozone problem

CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) were used as refrigerants, solvents, and plastic foam-blowing agents. When they reach the upper atmosphere, UV radiation breaks them apart, releasing chlorine free radicals. Each Cl· can destroy thousands of ozone molecules before being removed.

💧
Hydrosphere & Its Key Reactions
MoE Textbook, p. 244
📌 What is the hydrosphere?

The combined mass of water found on, under, and above Earth's surface — oceans, seas, rivers, lakes, streams, reservoirs, glaciers, and groundwater.

• Only ~1% of global water supply is fresh water
• The greatest source of water on the planet is the ocean (all salt water)
• The ocean contains at least 77 important elements — sodium, chlorine, magnesium, bromine

Key Reactions in the Hydrosphere

I. Ammonia toxicity → nitrification
NH₄⁺ + 2O₂ → NO₃⁻ + 2H⁺ + H₂O
📝 Why these reactions matter

Ammonia (NH₃) and ammonium (NH₄⁺) discharged from agriculture, aquaculture, industry, and urban areas into water bodies cause toxicity to fish and aquatic ecosystems.

The biological oxidation of NH₄⁺ to nitrite (NO₂⁻) and then nitrate (NO₃⁻) is a key part of the nitrogen cycle — a fundamental process in aquatic environments that profoundly influences ecosystem stability.

Nitrate and nitrite discharged into water bodies accelerate aquatic plant growth, leading to eutrophication (excess nutrients → algal blooms → oxygen depletion).

🪨
Lithosphere & Its Key Reactions
MoE Textbook, p. 245
📌 What is the lithosphere?

Earth's crust + upper mantle. The crust is the Earth's outer skin — accessible to humans — and consists of rocks and soil (most important part to humans and the environment).

Key Reactions in the Lithosphere

I. Bacterial oxidation of minerals
2FeCO₃ + ½O₂ + 3H₂O —[Ferrobacillus]→ 2Fe(OH)₃ + 2CO₂
II. Chemical weathering (hydration of rocks)
Fe₂O₃ + 3H₂O → Fe₂O₃·3H₂O
📝 Biological vs chemical weathering

Different bacteria (mainly autotrophic — not dependent on organic material for carbon) can effect oxidation or reductions of minerals. This biological weathering is a key part of soil formation.

🌱
Biosphere & Its Key Reactions
MoE Textbook, p. 246
📌 What is the biosphere?

The realm of living organisms and their interactions with the environment. Divided into smaller units called ecosystems. Each ecosystem contains dynamic interrelationships between living forms and their physical environment.

Key Reactions in the Biosphere

I. Photosynthesis (endothermic)
6CO₂ + 6H₂O —[sunlight]→ C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
II. Respiration (exothermic)
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + heat energy
📝 The cycle of life

Photosynthesis and respiration are the two halves of the same cycle. Photosynthesis uses CO₂ and produces O₂; respiration uses O₂ and produces CO₂. Together they maintain the balance of oxygen and carbon dioxide in the atmosphere.

🔄
Natural Cycles in the Environment
MoE Textbook, p. 247

Natural cycles continuously circulate the constituents essential to life and other Earth processes. The six important cycles are:

💧
Hydrologic Cycle
Continuous exchange of water between environmental compartments. 10% of ocean evaporation falls on land as precipitation.
🫁
Oxygen Cycle
Energy production, degradation of organic matter, weathering of minerals, photosynthesis, and ozone as sunscreen.
🌾
Nitrogen Cycle
N₂ fixation by bacteria/algae → nitrification → nitrate reduction → denitrification. Essential for plant growth.
🦴
Phosphorus Cycle
Phosphorus for DNA, RNA, and bones/teeth. Soil microorganisms convert organic P to soluble inorganic phosphate.
🌋
Sulfur Cycle
Sulfur occurs in all living matter (amino acids). Microbial transformations produce sulfates usable by plants.
🌳
Carbon Cycle
Photosynthesis converts CO₂ into glucose. Respiration, decomposition, and combustion return CO₂ to the atmosphere.

Detailed Equations from the Cycles

Nitrogen Fixation (by bacteria/algae)
2N₂ + 3CH₂O + 3H₂O + 4H⁺ → 4NH₄⁺ + 3CO₂
Nitrification (by Nitrosomonas & Nitrobacter)
2O₂ + 3NH₄⁺ → NO₃⁻ + 2H⁺ + H₂O
Denitrification (returns N to atmosphere)
2NO₃⁻ + 5CH₂O + 4H⁺ → N₂ + 7H₂O + 5CO₂
Sulfate formation (in hydrosphere/soil)
2H₂S + 4O₂ → SO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺
Carbon cycle — photosynthesis
6CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l) → C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(g)    ΔH = +2803 kJ/mol
Carbon cycle — respiration
C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(g) → 6CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l)    ΔH = −2803 kJ/mol
📝 The carbon cycle and climate change

According to the law of conservation of mass, the total number of carbon atoms on Earth is constant. But the amount that exists as carbon dioxide (a greenhouse gas) is growing, because humans burn fossil fuels faster than photosynthesis and ocean absorption can remove the CO₂. That's the heart of global warming.

📚
Key Concepts in Environmental Chemistry
MoE Textbook, p. 253

Before we study pollution, we need to define the vocabulary:

Pollutant

A substance whose concentration has increased due to human activity, ultimately having detrimental effects on the environment. Examples: SO₂, CO₂, O₃, Pb, Hg, excess heat, light, and sound.

Contaminant

A substance that does not occur in nature but is introduced into the environment through human activity. A contaminant is called a pollutant when it exerts detrimental effects on human health.

Sink

The medium that interacts with and retains pollutants or converts them chemically. Example: a body of water or soil.

Dissolved Oxygen (DO)

Oxygen dissolved in water. Vital for aquatic life. Optimum levels for good quality water: 4–8 mg/L. Below 4 mg/L = polluted and unfit for consumption.

Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD)

Indicates the capacity of dissolved organic matter (DOM) in a water sample to consume oxygen. Measured experimentally by measuring DO at the beginning and end of a 5-day period in a sealed sample.

Threshold Limit Value (TLV)

The permissible level of a toxic pollutant in the atmosphere to which a healthy individual can be exposed for an 8-hour day without adverse effects. Determined by experimentation, medical knowledge, and environmental studies.

📝 Pollutant vs Contaminant

All pollutants are contaminants, but not all contaminants are pollutants. A substance becomes a pollutant when it causes harm to a receptor (a body of water, humans, trees, animals, or fish). Before that, it's just a contaminant.

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1Identify the Sphere
Problem: Match each process to the environmental sphere where it primarily occurs: (a) photosynthesis, (b) rock hydration, (c) ocean evaporation, (d) CFC-ozone destruction.
(a) Photosynthesis
Biosphere — carried out by plants
(b) Rock hydration
Lithosphere — chemical weathering of rocks
(c) Ocean evaporation
Hydrosphere — water moving to atmosphere
(d) CFC-ozone destruction
Atmosphere — upper atmospheric chemistry
✅ Photosynthesis = biosphere; Rock hydration = lithosphere; Evaporation = hydrosphere; CFC destruction = atmosphere
Example 2Pollutant vs Contaminant
Problem: Explain the difference between a contaminant and a pollutant. Give an example that could be both, depending on circumstances.
Definition 1
Contaminant = a substance that does not occur in nature, introduced through human activity
Definition 2
Pollutant = a contaminant that exerts detrimental effects on a receptor (humans, trees, animals, fish, water bodies)
Example — CO₂
CO₂ at natural levels (~0.04%) is not harmful — it is part of the natural carbon cycle. But at elevated concentrations from burning fossil fuels, it becomes a pollutant (greenhouse gas).
✅ All pollutants are contaminants, but a contaminant becomes a pollutant only when it causes harm
Example 3Balance Photosynthesis & Respiration
Problem: Write the equations for photosynthesis and respiration. Explain why they balance in nature.
Photosynthesis
6CO₂ + 6H₂O —[sunlight]→ C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
Respiration
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + heat
Balance
Photosynthesis consumes CO₂ and produces O₂; respiration consumes O₂ and produces CO₂. Together they cycle carbon and oxygen through the biosphere.
✅ They are exact reverses of each other. The balance is broken when excess CO₂ is added by burning fossil fuels.
Example 4Why BOD Matters
Problem: A water sample has a BOD of 12 mg/L. Is this water safe for aquatic life? Explain using DO values.
Optimal DO
Good quality water has DO between 4–8 mg/L
What BOD means
BOD = how much oxygen dissolved organic matter consumes over 5 days
Interpretation
BOD of 12 mg/L means 12 mg of oxygen per liter gets consumed by decomposers — often more than what's available → DO drops below 4 mg/L
Result
Water is polluted — fish and other aerobic organisms would suffocate
✅ High BOD → low DO → unfit for aquatic life
Example 5Why Environmental Chemistry is Multidisciplinary
Problem: Explain why environmental chemistry is considered a multidisciplinary field, using the sulfur dioxide/acid rain example.
Chemistry
Chemical reactions: S + O₂ → SO₂; SO₂ + ½O₂ → SO₃; SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄
Meteorology
Atmospheric transport of SO₂ — how far it travels and where it falls
Geology
Acid rain dissolves limestone (CaCO₃) in buildings and statues
Biology
Effects on plants (defoliation), fish (lake acidification), and ecosystems
Engineering
Solution requires scrubbers on smokestacks, alternative fuels, and emission controls
✅ A single pollutant touches chemistry, meteorology, geology, biology, and engineering — it doesn't respect disciplines

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 5.1
✅ Key Points — Introduction to Environmental Chemistry
  • Environmental chemistry = branch of chemistry dealing with production, transport, reactions, effects, and fates of chemical species in water, air, terrestrial, and biological environments
  • It's a multidisciplinary field — combining chemistry, biology, geology, meteorology, and engineering
  • The environment consists of four components (spheres): atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere, biosphere
  • Atmosphere — 78% N₂, 21% O₂, 1% other gases; protects from UV, controls temperature
  • Hydrosphere — all water; only ~1% fresh; oceans are the greatest source of water vapor; 77 important elements
  • Lithosphere — Earth's crust + upper mantle; contains rocks and soil
  • Biosphere — realm of living organisms; divided into ecosystems
  • Key atmospheric reactions: NO oxidation by UV; SO₂/SO₃/NO₂ → acid rain; CFC + UV → Cl· → O₃ destruction
  • Key biosphere reactions: photosynthesis (6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂) and respiration (reverse)
  • Six natural cycles: hydrologic, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, carbon
  • Nitrogen cycle: fixation → nitrification → nitrate reduction → denitrification
  • Key concepts: pollutant (concentration increased by human activity → harm), contaminant (introduced but not yet harmful), sink (retains pollutants), DO (dissolved oxygen, 4–8 mg/L optimum), BOD (oxygen consumed by DOM over 5 days), TLV (8-hour permissible exposure)
  • Reference: MoE Chemistry Grade 12 Student Textbook, §5.1 (pp. 241–254)
