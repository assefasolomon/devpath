🏗️ Manufacturing Industries in Ethiopia (Part 1)

Ethiopia's chemical industry is still developing, but five manufacturing sectors are already significant: glass, ceramics, cement, sugar, and paper. This lesson traces how each one turns Ethiopian raw materials into finished products.

Introduction
What you will learn

Ethiopia's chemical industry is still at a nascent stage — most chemicals are still imported. But five major manufacturing sectors already operate inside the country: glass, ceramics, cement, sugar, and paper. In this lesson, we'll examine the chemistry behind each one.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe the manufacture of glass — 3 types and their reactions
  • Explain ceramics manufacturing — moulding and densification
  • Describe the cement manufacturing process and its reactions
  • Trace the 8 steps of sugar production
  • Compare mechanical vs chemical pulping for paper
  • Identify Ethiopian industries in each sector

3.8.1 Glass Manufacturing
MoE Textbook, pp. 183–185
📌 What is glass?

Glass is an amorphous (non-crystalline) solid material. It's inexpensive to make, easy to shape when molten, reasonably heat-resistant when set, chemically inert (doesn't react with contents), and can be recycled any number of times.

📌 Main component

The main component of glass is silica (SiO₂).

Three Types of Glass

TypeCompositionMelting TemperatureUses
Quartz glass Pure silica (SiO₂) ~2300 °C High strength, low thermal expansion, highly transparent
Soda-lime glass Sodium silicate + calcium silicate (from SiO₂, Na₂CO₃, CaCO₃) ~1600 °C Ordinary glass — window panes, bottles, dishes (90% of all glass)
Borosilicate glass (Pyrex) Boron(III) oxide (B₂O₃) instead of limestone ~1600 °C High chemical & thermal resistance — ovenware, lab glassware

Formation Reactions for Soda-Lime Glass

Sodium Carbonate + Silica
Na₂CO₃ + SiO₂ → Na₂SiO₃ + CO₂
Calcium Carbonate + Silica
CaCO₃ + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ + CO₂
📝 Why is soda-lime glass so common?

Soda-lime glass accounts for about 90% of manufactured glass. It uses cheap, abundant raw materials (silica sand, sodium carbonate, limestone) and its properties are ideal for most everyday uses.

The Manufacturing Steps

  1. Batch preparation — Raw materials mixed in proportion: 60% sand, 21% sodium carbonate, 19% limestone
  2. Glass melting — Materials fed into a glass-melting furnace, heated to about 1600 °C to form a molten viscous liquid. Furnace operates continuously, 24 hours a day. Colorants and property-enhancing additives are added here.
  3. Glass forming — Melted glass shaped into the required form (bottles, sheets, etc.)
  4. Annealing — Removal of internal stresses by reheating then controlled slow cooling
  5. Inspection — Testing of the glass product to check quality
  6. Packing and dispatching — Final stage before distribution
📝 Why glasses are separated by color

Recycled glass is separated by color before re-melting because mixed colors produce an undesirable tint. Each color batch must be processed separately to maintain the intended color of the final product.

3.8.2 Manufacturing of Ceramics
MoE Textbook, pp. 186–187
📌 What are ceramics?

Ceramics are inorganic, non-metallic solids prepared by the action of heat and subsequent cooling. Traditional ceramics (porcelain, tiles, pottery) are formed from minerals like clay, talc, and feldspar. Industrial ceramics use extremely pure powders of specialty chemicals.

📌 Raw materials

• Traditional ceramics — clay, talc, feldspar
• Industrial ceramics — silicon carbide, alumina, barium titanate, titanium carbide (pure powders)

The Two Key Steps

  1. Moulding — After purification, small amounts of wax (as a binder) or plastics (for pliability) are added. The powder is shaped into different objects by various moulding processes.
  2. Densification — The moulded object is heated in an electric furnace to 1000–1700 °C. The ceramic particles merge (coalesce), and the object shrinks by up to 20% of its original size while becoming very dense and strong.
📝 General properties of ceramics

Most ceramics are: hard, wear-resistant, brittle, refractory (heat-resistant), thermal and electrical insulators, non-magnetic, oxidation-resistant, and chemically stable.

Uses of Ceramics

CategoryExamples
Well-known usesArt sculptures, dishes, platters, kitchenware, kitchen tiles, bathroom structures
Less-known usesElectrical insulators, computer parts, tools, dental replacements, engine parts, space shuttle tiles, hip/knee/shoulder bone replacements
Future usesRemoving impurities from drinking water; replacing diseased heart valves

3.8.3 Cement Manufacturing
MoE Textbook, pp. 188–190
📌 Raw materials for cement

The major raw materials are limestone (CaCO₃), clay, silica sand, gypsum, calcium silicate, calcium aluminate, iron(III) oxide, magnesium oxide, and pumice. Limestone is the main ingredient.

📌 Ethiopia's advantage

Ethiopia has huge reserves of the raw materials for cement. This is why many cement factories have been built in the country.

The Manufacturing Process

Cement is made by heating limestone (chalk), alumina (Al₂O₃), and silica-bearing materials (like clay) to 1450 °C in a kiln. This process is called calcination. Calcination produces a hard substance called clinker. The clinker is then ground with a small amount of gypsum into a powder — this is Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC).

Reactions in the Kiln

Upper part of kiln — drying
Raw material → Complete elimination of moisture
Middle part — limestone decomposition
CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)
Lower end (about 1600 °C) — formation of silicates and aluminates
2CaO + SiO₂ → 2CaO·SiO₂ (dicalcium silicate)
Tricalcium silicate
3CaO + SiO₂ → 3CaO·SiO₂ (tricalcium silicate)
Tricalcium aluminate
3CaO + Al₂O₃ → 3CaO·Al₂O₃
Tetracalcium aluminoferrite
4CaO + Al₂O₃ + Fe₂O₃ → 4CaO·Al₂O₃·Fe₂O₃

Composition of Cement

ComponentPercentage
Calcium oxide (CaO)50–60%
Silica (SiO₂)20–25%
Alumina (Al₂O₃)5–10%
Magnesium oxide (MgO)2–3%
Ferric oxide (Fe₂O₃)1–2%
Sulfur trioxide (SO₃)1–2%
📝 The role of gypsum

Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) is added at 2–3% to slow down the rate of setting of cement, so that the cement hardens adequately (rather than setting instantly).

📝 Setting of cement

When cement is mixed with water, it forms a plastic mass that hardens after some time due to the formation of three-dimensional cross-links between –Si–O–Si– and –Si–O–Al– chains. The first setting occurs within 24 hours; complete hardening requires about two weeks.

3.8.4 Sugar Manufacturing
MoE Textbook, pp. 191–192
📌 Raw material

Sugarcane is a perennial herb belonging to the grass family. Native to tropical and subtropical regions, this tropical grass is about 3 meters tall and is used as the raw material for sugar production.

The Eight Steps of Sugar Production

  1. Collecting the harvest — Mature canes are cut at ground level, leaves removed, tops trimmed, then transported to a sugar factory.
  2. Cleansing and grinding — Stalks are washed and cut. Rotating knives shred the cane into pieces. Hot water is sprayed to dissolve the sugar. The shredded cane spreads on a conveyor belt.
  3. Juicing — Shredded cane travels through heavy-duty rollers that extract juice from the pulp. The remaining pulp ("bagasse") is dried and used as fuel. The raw juice moves on for clarification.
  4. Clarifying — Carbon dioxide and milk of lime are added and heated until boiling. CO₂ forms calcium carbonate which attracts non-sugar debris (fats, gums, waxes) away from the juice. The juice is filtered.
  5. Evaporation — The clear juice is boiled under a vacuum at low temperature. Water evaporates, and the juice becomes thick brown syrup.
  6. Crystallization — Pulverized sugar is fed into a sterilized vacuum pan. As liquid evaporates, crystals form. The thick mass is spun and dried in a centrifuge → raw sugar.
  7. Refinery — Raw sugar is mixed with a sugar-water solution to loosen molasses ("magma"). Centrifuged, washed, dissolved, and filtered. SO₂ is passed through to bleach. Concentrated clear syrup is again evaporated.
  8. Separation and packaging — Screens separate the different-sized crystals. Large and small crystals are packaged and labeled as white refined sugar.
📝 Useful by-product: Bagasse

The fibrous pulp left after juicing — called bagasse — is not wasted. It's dried and burned as fuel to power the sugar factory itself. This makes sugar production more energy-efficient.

3.8.5 Paper and Pulp
MoE Textbook, pp. 193–194
📌 What is paper?

Paper is not a chemical compound — it's a mixture made from rags and wood pulp, glued together with some additives, bleached, and dried.

📌 What is wood made of?

Wood is composed of cellulose, lignin, oils, and resins. Lignin binds the cellulose fibers together. To make wood pulp, the cellulose must be separated from the lignin.

The Five Steps of Paper Manufacturing

  1. Harvesting — Cutting down trees and transporting timber to the paper and pulp industry.
  2. Preparation for pulping — Bark is removed; wood is chipped and screened into uniform-sized pieces.
  3. Pulping — The step that makes wood pulp from chipped wood. Either mechanical or chemical (see below).
  4. Bleaching — Removing coloring matter and increasing brightness. Uses strong oxidizing agents like chlorine, chlorine oxide, ozone, and hydrogen peroxide.
  5. Making paper from pulp — The bleached pulp is processed into liquid stock. The suspension is poured onto a moving screen belt; liquor drains by gravity. The continuous sheet moves through rollers that compress fibers and remove remaining water.

Two Types of Pulping

⚙️ Mechanical Pulping

• Uses steam, pressure, and high temperatures instead of chemicals to tear the fibers apart
• Fibre quality is greatly reduced — creates short, weak fibers that still contain lignin
• Produces newspaper-quality paper
• Cheaper, but lower quality

🧪 Chemical Pulping

• Combines wood chips and chemicals in digesters
• Heat and chemicals break down lignin without degrading cellulose fibers
• Two main methods:
  Kraft process — NaOH + Na₂S at 170 °C for ~4 h (basic medium)
  Sulphite process — NaHSO₄ or Mg(HSO₄)₂ at pH ~3, 60 °C for 6–12 h
• Produces higher quality paper

📝 Kraft is the dominant method

The Kraft process is the dominant chemical pulping method — the most widely used method for making pulp from all types of trees. It uses a basic digestion medium (NaOH + Na₂S) and yields strong, high-quality pulp.

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1Identify the Type of Glass
Problem: A beaker in a chemistry lab needs to withstand rapid temperature changes and resist chemical attack. What type of glass is it made of, and why?
Requirement
Thermal resistance + chemical resistance — used in lab glassware
Type
Borosilicate glass (Pyrex) — made with B₂O₃ instead of limestone
Why
Boron(III) oxide gives high resistance to chemical corrosion and temperature changes
✅ Borosilicate (Pyrex) glass — high chemical and thermal resistance
Example 2Why is Gypsum Added to Cement?
Problem: Gypsum is added to cement at 2–3%. What is its role?
Function
Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) slows down the rate of setting of cement
Consequence
Without it, cement would set too quickly — becoming unworkable before use
Result
The cement hardens adequately and predictably, allowing construction workers to use it properly
✅ Gypsum slows the setting rate so that cement hardens adequately
Example 3Mechanical vs Chemical Pulping
Problem: Compare mechanical and chemical pulping in terms of (a) process, (b) fibre quality, and (c) typical products.
(a) Process
Mechanical: steam + pressure + heat tears fibres apart
Chemical: digester chemicals break down lignin selectively
(b) Fibre quality
Mechanical: short, weak fibres; still contain lignin → low quality
Chemical: long fibres; cellulose intact → high quality
(c) Products
Mechanical → newspaper
Chemical → writing paper, high-quality sheets
✅ Mechanical = cheap but low quality (newspaper); Chemical = more expensive but high quality
Example 4Sugar — Why Clarify?
Problem: Why is CaCO₃ (formed from CO₂ + milk of lime) added during the clarifying step of sugar production?
Purpose
CaCO₃ forms in the liquid sugar mixture when CO₂ and lime react
Action
The CaCO₃ particles attract non-sugar debris — fats, gums, waxes — and pull them away from the juice
Removal
The juice is then pushed through filters to remove any remaining impurities, producing a clear juice
✅ CaCO₃ is a clarifying agent that adsorbs non-sugar impurities, allowing them to be filtered out
