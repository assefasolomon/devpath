🧪 Manufacturing Valuable Products — Ammonia & the Haber Process

How are raw materials turned into the products we use? This lesson introduces the general manufacturing workflow, then dives into ammonia — the compound behind nearly all the world's fertilizers — and the famous Haber-Bosch process that produces it.

Introduction
What you will learn

Manufacturing involves a number of chemical processes designed to produce a desired product from a variety of starting raw materials, using energy through a succession of treatment steps integrated in a rational fashion. Today we focus on the most important chemical in world agriculture: ammonia (NH₃).

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe the general manufacturing workflow (physical + chemical treatment steps)
  • List the physical and chemical properties of ammonia
  • List the major uses of ammonia in industry and agriculture
  • Describe laboratory preparation of ammonia
  • Explain the Haber-Bosch process — its chemistry and industrial conditions
  • Apply Le Chatelier's principle to optimize ammonia yield
⚙️
The General Manufacturing Workflow
How products are made

Manufacturing of valuable products involves a number of chemical processes. The process is designed to produce a desired product from a variety of starting raw materials, using energy through a succession of treatment steps integrated in a rational fashion.

📌 Two types of treatment steps

1. Physical steps — separation, filtration, distillation, heating, cooling, compression
2. Chemical steps — reactions, catalysis, neutralization, oxidation/reduction

Basic Steps in a Manufacturing Process
Raw materials → pretreatment → chemical reaction → product. By-products may be recycled, sold, or treated.
📝 Every product is different

Valuable products include every industrial product. They are either directly used by consumers or serve as raw materials to synthesize products that solve societal demands. Both organic and inorganic chemicals can be used in manufacturing.

💨
Ammonia (NH₃) — Properties
MoE Textbook, p. 151
📌 Physical Properties

• Lighter than air — density 0.769 kg/m³ at STP
• Colorless gas at room temperature
• Sharp, intensely irritating odor
• Melting point = −77.7 °C
• Boiling point = −33.35 °C
• Highly soluble in water — 34% (w/w) at 25 °C
• Commercially available as a 28–30% aqueous solution

📌 Chemical Properties

Weak base — partially ionizes in water:

NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻

Often referred to as ammonium hydroxide (NH₄OH).

Ionization in Water
NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
📝 Why is NH₃ so soluble in water?

Ammonia is a polar molecule with a lone pair of electrons on nitrogen. It forms hydrogen bonds with water molecules, making it very soluble. Additionally, the reaction with water to form NH₄⁺ and OH⁻ pulls more NH₃ into solution.

🌾
Uses of Ammonia
Why it's so important

Ammonia is an important compound, essential to man for a variety of diverse uses. Such a diverse applicability has caused large demand for its production.

🌾
Fertilizers
The biggest use — as a raw material for nitrogen fertilizers like urea, ammonium nitrate, and DAP.
🧽
Cleaning Agent
Household ammonia solution is a common glass and surface cleaner.
🦠
Antimicrobial Agent
Used in some disinfectants and food processing.
💥
Explosives
Raw material for nitrocellulose and trinitrotoluene (TNT).
🧂
Soda Ash Production
Used in the Solvay process to make Na₂CO₃ (see Lesson 3.6).
⚗️
Nitric Acid (Ostwald)
Ammonia is the starting material for the Ostwald process (see Lesson 3.4).
🧊
Refrigeration
Liquid ammonia is an excellent refrigerant (high latent heat of vaporization).
🧵
Textiles & Dyes
Used in the production of synthetic fibers and dye intermediates.
📝 The scale of ammonia production

Globally, over 180 million tonnes of ammonia are produced each year, making it one of the most-produced industrial chemicals. About 80–90% goes into fertilizers — without it, we could not feed the world's population.

🔬
Laboratory Preparation of Ammonia
Heating an ammonium salt with a strong alkali
📌 Lab Method

Ammonia is easily made in the laboratory by heating an ammonium salt such as ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl) with a strong alkali such as sodium hydroxide (NaOH) or calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂).

Preferred Lab Method (using Ca(OH)₂)
2NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCl₂ + 2H₂O + 2NH₃(g)
Alternative (using NaOH)
NH₄Cl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O + NH₃(g)
📌 Why Ca(OH)₂ is preferred

Ca(OH)₂ is preferred over NaOH in the lab because it is cheaper, safer to handle, and less hygroscopic. The gas produced is dried using quicklime (CaO).

📝 Collection method

Ammonia is lighter than air, so it is collected by upward displacement of air — the collecting tube is held upside down.

📝 Alternative lab method

Ammonia gas may also be made by warming concentrated ammonium hydroxide (NH₄OH). But the alkali method above is standard in textbooks and exams.

The Haber-Bosch Process
Industrial ammonia production
📌 Historical background (MoE Textbook, p. 152)

The development of the Haber-Bosch process for ammonia production made it possible to meet the world's demand. German chemist Fritz Haber discovered the process in the early 20th century, using atmospheric hydrogen and nitrogen to produce ammonia. Carl Bosch then scaled the process up to industrial production. Both men were awarded Nobel Prizes (Haber in 1918, Bosch in 1931).

Haber-Bosch Reaction
N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)    ΔH = −92 kJ/mol
📝 The reaction is exothermic and reversible

The forward reaction releases heat (−92 kJ/mol) and is a reversible equilibrium. This means the yield can be optimized using Le Chatelier's principle (see next section).

Step-by-Step Production

  1. Obtain the raw materials. Hydrogen (H₂) is obtained by electrolyzing water or (more commonly today) from methane (natural gas). Nitrogen (N₂) is obtained by fractional distillation of liquid air.
  2. React the gases. The H₂ and N₂ are introduced into a chamber containing iron particles (catalyst) at a pressure of 15–25 MPa and temperature of 300–500 °C. The reaction produces ammonia: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃.
  3. Separate and recycle. The hot gas mixture is passed through a condenser. Ammonia condenses easily compared to N₂ and H₂, so liquefied ammonia is collected, and the leftover nitrogen and hydrogen gases are re-introduced into the reactor. This recycling allows about 97% conversion of initial reactants into ammonia.

Le Chatelier's Principle & Ammonia Yield
Why the process conditions were chosen

The Haber reaction is reversible and exothermic. We can use Le Chatelier's principle to figure out what conditions favor the forward reaction (more ammonia).

The equilibrium
N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)    ΔH = −92 kJ/mol
✅ Favors MORE NH₃ (forward)

High pressure — 4 moles gas → 2 moles gas, so increasing pressure shifts right

Low temperature — forward reaction is exothermic, so cooling shifts right

Removing NH₃ — condensing it out pulls the equilibrium right

Adding more N₂ or H₂ — pushes equilibrium right

Catalyst (Fe) — doesn't shift equilibrium but speeds up reaching it

⚠️ Favors LESS NH₃ (reverse)

Low pressure — shifts left, favoring N₂ + H₂

High temperature — shifts left, favoring the endothermic reverse reaction

Adding NH₃ — shifts left

Removing N₂ or H₂ — shifts left

📝 The engineering compromise

Low temperature favors more NH₃ (thermodynamics), but the reaction would be too slow at low T (kinetics). So industry uses a moderate temperature (300–500 °C) with a catalyst (Fe) to speed it up. High pressure favors NH₃, so industry uses 15–25 MPa.

Result: A practical compromise that maximizes yield AND reaction rate.

📝 Recycled gases — 97% conversion

Even after condensation, some unreacted N₂ and H₂ remain. Rather than waste them, industry recycles them back into the reactor. Combined with continuous removal of NH₃, this raises the overall conversion to about 97%.

Experiments 3.1 & 3.2
Textbook pp. 154–159
🧪 Experiment 3.1 — Preparation and Testing of Ammonia
Objective: To prepare ammonia, test its solubility, and test its alkaline nature.

Apparatus: Eye protection, stand and clamp, dried boiling tube with stopper and delivery tube, 100 mL beaker, 250 mL beaker, Bunsen burner, spatula, test tubes.

Chemicals: Ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl), calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂), calcium oxide (CaO), concentrated HCl, red/blue litmus paper, universal indicator paper.

Safety: Wear eye protection throughout. Ammonia gas is toxic, pungent, and dangerous for the environment — must not be inhaled. Perform in a fume cupboard or well-ventilated lab.

Procedure:
  • Mix 2 spatulas of NH₄Cl with 2 spatulas of Ca(OH)₂ — reaction begins immediately.
  • Hold litmus paper over the mixture — observe color change. Test with universal indicator paper.
  • Transfer mixture to boiling tube and set up apparatus (see Figure 3.4).
  • Optional: add a lump of CaO to absorb water and dry the ammonia.
  • Gently warm the mixture and collect ammonia gas (tube held upside down since NH₃ is lighter than air).
  • Test with universal indicator paper at the open end.
  • Hold the tube of ammonia upside down and quickly place it, mouth down, into water — the level should rise dramatically (high solubility).
Key Questions:
  • Why is a dried boiling tube required?
  • What is the purpose of the CaO?
  • What is the white "smoke" produced when NH₃ meets HCl?
  • Is ammonia acidic or alkaline? Write the reaction equation.
📝 Answer to the "white smoke" question

When NH₃ gas meets HCl gas, they react to form solid ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl), which appears as a white smoke:
NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s)

🧪 Experiment 3.2 — Ammonia Fountain Demonstration
Objective: To make a miniature chemical fountain using soluble ammonia and atmospheric pressure.

Materials: Water, 2-L round-bottomed flask, 2-L beaker, 2-hole stopper, syringe, glass tube with rubber tube, stand with ring and clamp, test tube, tong, Bunsen burner, heat-proof mat.

Chemicals: NH₄Cl and NaOH (or NH₄OH solution or dry NH₃ gas), water, phenolphthalein.

Procedure — Preparing dry ammonia:
  • Add 50 mL concentrated ammonia solution to a 100 mL test tube.
  • Place the test tube in a holder fitted to the mouth of an inverted conical flask.
  • Heat with a Bunsen burner to generate ammonia gas, collected by upward displacement of air.
  • Alternatively, use a 1:1 mixture of NH₄Cl and NaOH in the flask.
Procedure — Demonstration:
  • Fill a beaker with water and add 1 mL phenolphthalein indicator.
  • Clamp the flask with its tube below the water level. Fill the syringe with water, dry the nozzle, and fit into the second hole of the stopper.
  • Remove the plain stopper from the inverted gas-filled flask and quickly fit the stopper holding the jet and syringe.
  • Squirt a few mL of water into the flask using the syringe.
  • As the gas dissolves, a partial vacuum forms — external air pressure forces water up the tube and through the jet, forming a fountain.
  • The indicator changes color as the ammonia dissolves in the water.
📝 Why the fountain works

Ammonia is extremely soluble in water. When a small amount of water enters the flask, it rapidly dissolves a large volume of NH₃ gas, creating a partial vacuum. Atmospheric pressure then pushes water up the tube and out the jet, producing the fountain. The pink color appears because NH₃ dissolves to form a basic solution (NH₄OH).

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1Identify the Raw Materials
Problem: What are the two primary raw materials for the Haber-Bosch process, and where does each come from?
Hydrogen (H₂)
Obtained by electrolysis of water or more commonly from methane (natural gas)
Nitrogen (N₂)
Obtained by fractional distillation of liquid air
Source spheres
N₂ from the atmosphere; H₂ from water (hydrosphere) or natural gas (lithosphere)
✅ H₂ (from water or methane) + N₂ (from air) → NH₃
Example 2Effect of Pressure
Problem: Predict the effect of increasing the pressure on the Haber equilibrium: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃
Count moles
Reactants: 1 + 3 = 4 moles gas. Products: 2 moles gas
Le Chatelier
Increasing pressure shifts equilibrium toward the side with fewer gas moles
Direction
4 moles → 2 moles: shift right → more NH₃
✅ Higher pressure → more NH₃ (why industry uses 15–25 MPa)
Example 3Effect of Temperature
Problem: The Haber reaction has ΔH = −92 kJ/mol (exothermic). Predict the effect of increasing the temperature on NH₃ yield.
Direction
Forward reaction is exothermic (releases heat)
Le Chatelier
Adding heat (higher T) shifts equilibrium in the endothermic direction — which is the reverse
Result
Higher T → less NH₃ at equilibrium
Compromise
Industry uses 300–500 °C (not lower) because kinetics dominate — a low-T reaction would be too slow
✅ Higher T → lower equilibrium yield, but necessary for a practical reaction rate
Example 4The Role of the Catalyst
Problem: Finely divided iron is used as a catalyst in the Haber process. Does it affect the equilibrium position? Explain.
Definition
A catalyst speeds up both the forward and reverse reactions equally
Equilibrium
It helps the system reach equilibrium faster but does not shift the equilibrium position
Yield vs rate
Yield is unchanged; only the time to reach equilibrium is reduced
✅ The Fe catalyst speeds up the reaction but does not change the equilibrium yield
Example 5Why 97% Conversion?
Problem: In the Haber process, only about 15–20% of the N₂ and H₂ reacts on a single pass through the reactor. Yet the overall conversion is ~97%. Explain.
Single pass
A single pass reaches equilibrium at ~15–20% NH₃ — but the gases don't disappear
Separation
The NH₃ is liquefied and removed by condenser — the N₂ and H₂ stay as gas
Recycle
The leftover N₂ and H₂ are sent back into the reactor for another pass
Result
With continuous recycling, total conversion reaches ~97%
✅ Recycling the unreacted gases raises overall conversion from 15–20% per pass to ~97%
