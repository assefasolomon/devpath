⚗️ Nitric Acid & Nitrogen Fertilizers

Nitric acid is a highly corrosive mineral acid and the key to nitrogen fertilizers. Made industrially from ammonia via the Ostwald process, it feeds the world's crops through urea, DAP, and other fertilizer products.

Introduction
What you will learn

In Lesson 3.3 we produced ammonia. Now we take it further: ammonia is the starting material for nitric acid, which in turn is the key ingredient in most nitrogen fertilizers. This lesson traces that chain — from NH₃ → HNO₃ → fertilizer → food.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • List the properties and uses of nitric acid
  • Describe the Ostwald process — the 3-step industrial synthesis of HNO₃
  • Explain laboratory preparation of nitric acid
  • Identify the main nitrogen-based fertilizers: anhydrous ammonia, urea, UAN, DAP
  • Describe the soil reactions of each fertilizer
  • Explain the role of nitrifying bacteria
💧
Nitric Acid — Properties
MoE Textbook, p. 160
📌 Physical Properties

• Density = 1.51 g/cm³ (pure)
• Colorless liquid with a highly pungent odor
• Appearance similar to water
• On exposure to light, turns brown due to slight decomposition
• Commercial concentration: 65–68%

Decomposition on Exposure to Light
4HNO₃(l) → 4NO₂(g) + O₂(g) + 2H₂O(l)

(The brown color comes from NO₂ gas produced by this decomposition.)

📌 Chemical Properties

• Strong acid — dissociates completely in dilute aqueous solution
• Forms salts called nitrates, typically very soluble in water
• Highly corrosive mineral acid
• Strong oxidizing agent, especially when hot and concentrated
• Reacts readily with metals (violent reactions)
• Produces heat when dissolved in water
• Some reactions are even explosive
• Highly hazardous — causes severe burns and necrosis on contact with skin

🌾
Uses of Nitric Acid
MoE Textbook, p. 160
📌 The biggest use: fertilizers

Neutralization of nitric acid with ammonia produces ammonium nitrate (NH₄NO₃) — the most important component of mineral fertilizers used worldwide.

🌾
Fertilizers
Ammonium nitrate (from NH₃ + HNO₃) — largest single use.
💥
Explosives
TNT (trinitrotoluene) and nitroglycerin — made by nitrating aromatic compounds.
🧪
Organic Nitrogen Compounds
Precursor to nitrobenzenes and other industrial chemicals.
🚀
Rocket Fuel
Red fuming nitric acid (HNO₃ + N₂O₄ + H₂O₂ mixture).
🎨
Dyes and Lacquers
Used to produce organic dyes and industrial lacquers.
💊
Pharmaceuticals
Used in the synthesis of many drugs.
🍄
Fungicides
Production of agricultural fungicides.
🔧
Metal Cleaning & Etching
Refining precious metals; cleaning and etching metal surfaces.
📝 Other less common uses

• Soil acidification in horticulture
• Artificial ageing of wood to obtain desired shade
• Household cleaning products
• Detection of trace metals in analytical labs

🔬
Laboratory Preparation of Nitric Acid
Displacement of a volatile acid
📌 The lab method

Nitric acid is prepared by reacting potassium nitrate (KNO₃) with concentrated sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄).

Laboratory Preparation
2KNO₃ + H₂SO₄ → K₂SO₄ + 2HNO₃
📌 The general principle

Salt of a more volatile acid + less volatile acid → displaces the more volatile acid

In this case:
• KNO₃ = salt of the volatile acid HNO₃
• H₂SO₄ = the less volatile acid
• The less volatile H₂SO₄ displaces the more volatile HNO₃ from its salt

📝 Industrial vs lab

The laboratory method uses KNO₃ and H₂SO₄. But for commercial production, the industry uses the Ostwald process (next section) — much more efficient and cheaper.

🏭
The Ostwald Process
Industrial production of nitric acid
📌 Definition (MoE Textbook, p. 161)

The Ostwald process is the catalytic oxidation of ammonia to nitric oxide, which, using special absorption towers, yields concentrated HNO₃ acid. It's the basic commercial production method.

📌 The principle

The conversion of ammonia to nitric acid occurs as a result of oxidation. The oxidation reaction gives nitric oxide (NO). When NO is further oxidized, nitrous gases (NO₂) are formed, and those gases trap water molecules — resulting in nitric acid.

The Three Steps

Step 1 — Oxidation of NH₃

Ammonia is burned in excess oxygen over a platinum catalyst at ~850 °C to form nitric oxide (NO):

4NH₃(g) + 5O₂(g) → 4NO(g) + 6H₂O(g)

Step 2 — Oxidation of NO

Additional air is added to cool the mixture and oxidize NO to NO₂:

2NO(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g)

Step 3 — Absorption in Water

The NO₂ gas is bubbled into warm water to give nitric acid and NO (which is recycled):

3NO₂(g) + H₂O(l) → 2HNO₃(aq) + NO(g)

Ostwald Process — Simplified Flow
📝 Why recycle NO?

Step 3 produces NO gas as a by-product. Rather than waste it, the NO is recycled back into Step 2, where it is re-oxidized to NO₂. This improves the overall yield of nitric acid.

Nitrogen-Based Fertilizers
MoE Textbook, p. 166
📌 The main forms

Common forms of N-based fertilizer:
• Anhydrous ammonia (NH₃)
• Urea — (NH₂)₂CO
• Urea-ammonium nitrate (UAN) solutions
• Diammonium monohydrogen phosphate (DAP) — (NH₄)₂HPO₄

💨
Anhydrous Ammonia
NH₃
Most basic N fertilizer. Gas at room temperature — must be compressed into liquid and injected into soil. 82% N content.
🧂
Urea
(NH₂)₂CO
Solid fertilizer with 46% N content. World's most widely used N fertilizer. Made from CO₂ + NH₃.
💧
UAN Solution
Urea + NH₄NO₃ + H₂O
Liquid fertilizer — 35% urea, 45% ammonium nitrate, 20% water. Easy to apply by spraying.
💎
DAP
(NH₄)₂HPO₄
White crystalline solid. Contains BOTH nitrogen AND phosphorus. Made from NH₃ + H₃PO₄.
📝 Why so many different fertilizers?

Each has advantages and disadvantages:
• Anhydrous NH₃ — highest N content, but requires special equipment (pressure tank, injection)
• Urea — easy to handle, store, and transport; can be blended
• UAN solutions — easy liquid application by spraying
• DAP — provides TWO nutrients (N + P) in one product

🌍
Soil Reactions of Fertilizers
What happens after application

Anhydrous Ammonia in Soil

📌 Application

Anhydrous ammonia is applied by injection 6–8 inches below the soil surface to minimize escape of gaseous NH₃ into the air.

📌 Once in the soil

NH₃ is a very hygroscopic compound. Once in the soil, it reacts quickly with water and changes to the ammonium (NH₄⁺) form.

📌 Binding to soil

As a positively charged ion, NH₄⁺ reacts and binds with negatively charged soil constituents (clay and organic matter). Thus, it is held on the soil exchange complex and is not subject to movement with water (i.e., it does not leach away).

Nitrification — The Two-Step Conversion

Over time (with appropriate soil temperature and biological activity), NH₄⁺ ions are converted to the nitrate (NO₃⁻) form by specific soil bacteria — a process called nitrification.

Step 1 — Nitrosomonas bacteria
NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ (nitrite)
Step 2 — Nitrobacter bacteria
NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻ (nitrate)
📝 Conditions for nitrification

Nitrification generally occurs at soil temperatures above 50 °F (10 °C) and increases as temperatures rise. Some limited activity occurs below 50 °F as well.

Urea in Soil

Urea readily dissolves in water (including soil moisture), so it can be "incorporated" into the soil by rainfall or irrigation.

📌 Volatilization losses

If urea is applied to the soil surface and not incorporated by water or tillage, it is subject to volatilization losses of nitrogen. This occurs as urea undergoes hydrolysis:

Urea Hydrolysis in Soil
(NH₂)₂CO + H₂O → CO₂ + 2NH₃
📝 Why incorporate urea?

If applied on the surface, the released NH₃ can escape into the atmosphere as gas — wasting the fertilizer. Incorporating urea into the soil (by tillage or irrigation) traps the NH₃ as NH₄⁺ (which binds to soil particles).

💎
DAP — Diammonium Monohydrogen Phosphate
The dual-nutrient fertilizer
📌 What is DAP?

Diammonium monohydrogen phosphate (DAP), with the formula (NH₄)₂HPO₄, is a white crystalline compound. It contains both nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) — two of the three primary macronutrients plants need.

Production of DAP — Two Steps

Step 1 — Ammonia + Phosphoric acid
3NH₃(g) + 2H₃PO₄(l) → NH₄H₂PO₄(s) + (NH₄)₂HPO₄(s)

(Produces a mixture of monoammonium dihydrogen phosphate + diammonium monohydrogen phosphate.)

Step 2 — Recycling for further reaction
NH₄H₂PO₄(s) + NH₃(g) → (NH₄)₂HPO₄(s)

(Recycling the monoammonium salt for further reaction with anhydrous ammonia yields DAP.)

Uses of DAP

🌾
Fertilizer
The main use — supplies both N and P for plant growth.
🔥
Fire Retardant
Lowers combustion temperature, decreases weight-loss rates, increases char production.
🍷
Yeast Nutrient
Used in wine making and beer brewing.
📝 Soil acidification by DAP

DAP temporarily increases soil acidity. Over the long term, the soil becomes more acidic than before, upon nitrification of the ammonium (NH₄⁺ → NO₃⁻ releases H⁺ ions).

Experiments 3.3 & 3.4
Textbook pp. 163–165
🧪 Experiment 3.3 — Preparation of Nitric Acid in Laboratory
Objective: To prepare nitric acid in the laboratory.

Apparatus: Round-bottomed borosilicate flask (thick-walled preferred), delivery tube, glass stopper, glass retort, tripod stand, Bunsen burner, ice-cold water.

Chemicals: Potassium nitrate (KNO₃), concentrated sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄).

Safety: Concentrated nitric acid is corrosive and oxidizing — wear goggles or face shield. Use nitrile gloves.

Procedure:
  • Place 50 g of KNO₃ and 25 mL of concentrated H₂SO₄ in a round-bottom flask.
  • Heat the reactants to about 200 °C — do not let the temperature exceed 200 °C.
  • Observe nitric acid vapors cooled and condensed in a receiver cooled under cold water.
  • Collect the brown liquid (nitric acid) in the receiving flask.
Reaction: 2KNO₃ + H₂SO₄ → K₂SO₄ + 2HNO₃
🧪 Experiment 3.4 — Properties of Nitric Acid (Oxidizing Property)
Objective: To investigate the oxidizing property of nitric acid.

Apparatus: Round-bottomed borosilicate flask, clamp stand, delivery tube, test tube, tongs, glass wool, bowl or trough, safety screens.

Chemicals: 5 g copper turnings, 40 mL concentrated nitric acid.

Safety: Same as above — concentrated HNO₃ is corrosive and oxidizing.

Procedure:
  • Fill the test tube flask with water and add acid to the round-bottomed flask.
  • Clamp the flask into position with the delivery tube at the bottom.
  • Loosely plug the neck with glass wool; prepare delivery tube.
  • Add the copper and fit the delivery tube.
  • After ~80 seconds, the reaction slows — observe what happens.
Expected observation: Brown NO₂ gas is produced; copper dissolves to form blue Cu(NO₃)₂ solution.
Copper + Concentrated HNO₃
Cu + 4HNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂ + 2H₂O
📝 Why is nitric acid a strong oxidizing agent?

Nitric acid oxidizes metals not by releasing H⁺ (like typical acids), but by the oxidizing power of the nitrate ion (NO₃⁻). This is why copper — which doesn't react with HCl — readily dissolves in HNO₃, producing brown NO₂ gas.

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1Ostwald Process Identification
Problem: Identify each step of the Ostwald process and the catalyst used in Step 1.
Step 1
NH₃ oxidized by O₂ → NO. Catalyst = platinum (Pt) at ~850 °C
Step 2
NO oxidized by more O₂ → NO₂
Step 3
NO₂ absorbed in warm water → HNO₃ + NO (which is recycled)
✅ Step 1: 4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O (Pt catalyst). Step 2: 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂. Step 3: 3NO₂ + H₂O → 2HNO₃ + NO
Example 2Lab vs Industrial HNO₃
Problem: Compare how nitric acid is prepared in the laboratory vs how it's made industrially.
Lab method
2KNO₃ + H₂SO₄ → K₂SO₄ + 2HNO₃ (displacement of volatile acid by non-volatile acid)
Industrial method
Ostwald process: NH₃ + O₂ → NO → NO₂ → HNO₃ (3 steps, Pt catalyst)
Why different?
Lab: uses readily available reagents, small scale. Industrial: uses cheap NH₃, large scale, catalyst
✅ Lab uses KNO₃ + H₂SO₄; industry uses NH₃ + O₂ (Ostwald process)
Example 3Compare N Content
Problem: Which fertilizer has higher nitrogen content by mass — anhydrous ammonia (NH₃) or urea ((NH₂)₂CO)?
NH₃
M = 17 g/mol; N = 14 g/mol. % N = 14/17 × 100 = ≈ 82%
Urea
M = 60 g/mol; N = 28 g/mol (2 atoms). % N = 28/60 × 100 = ≈ 46%
Conclusion
Anhydrous NH₃ has the highest N content — but it requires special handling (pressure tank, injection)
✅ NH₃ (≈82%) > urea (≈46%). Higher N content ≠ always better — handling matters too.
Example 4Why Bind NH₄⁺ to Soil?
Problem: Explain why NH₄⁺ (ammonium) stays in the soil while NO₃⁻ (nitrate) can leach away.
Soil charge
Clay particles and organic matter are negatively charged
NH₄⁺ binding
NH₄⁺ is positively charged → attracted to negative soil particles → held in place
NO₃⁻ problem
NO₃⁻ is negatively charged → repelled by soil → moves freely with water → leaches away
Implication
Nitrification (NH₄⁺ → NO₃⁻) actually makes N more mobile in the soil — which is good for uptake by plant roots but bad for retention
✅ NH₄⁺ binds to negative soil particles; NO₃⁻ does not, so it can leach
Example 5DAP — Dual Nutrient Advantage
Problem: Why is DAP ((NH₄)₂HPO₄) considered more efficient than a single-nutrient fertilizer like urea?
DAP composition
Contains both nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) — two primary macronutrients
Single nutrient
Urea provides only N. Farmers growing crops need both N and P — they'd have to apply two separate fertilizers
Efficiency
One product supplies two nutrients → saves time, money, and application steps
Caveat
DAP acidifies soil over time, so pH management is needed
✅ DAP supplies both N and P in one product — more efficient than single-nutrient fertilizers

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 3.4
✅ Key Points — Nitric Acid & Nitrogen Fertilizers
  • Nitric acid (HNO₃) — colorless, pungent liquid; density 1.51 g/cm³; commercial form 65–68%
  • Turns brown on exposure to light due to decomposition producing NO₂: 4HNO₃ → 4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O
  • Strong acid + strong oxidizing agent, especially when hot and concentrated
  • Lab preparation: 2KNO₃ + H₂SO₄ → K₂SO₄ + 2HNO₃ (volatile acid displaced by non-volatile acid)
  • Ostwald process — 3 steps: NH₃ → NO (Pt catalyst, 850 °C) → NO₂ → HNO₃ (NO recycled)
  • Main use: making ammonium nitrate fertilizer (NH₃ + HNO₃ → NH₄NO₃)
  • Other uses: explosives (TNT), rocket fuel, dyes, pharmaceuticals, fungicides, metal etching
  • N-based fertilizers: anhydrous NH₃ (~82% N), urea (46% N), UAN solution, DAP
  • Urea synthesis: 2NH₃ + CO₂ → NH₂COONH₄ → (NH₂)₂CO + H₂O
  • Urea hydrolysis in soil: (NH₂)₂CO + H₂O → CO₂ + 2NH₃ (volatilization loss if not incorporated)
  • Nitrification: NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ (Nitrosomonas) → NO₃⁻ (Nitrobacter)
  • NH₄⁺ binds to negatively-charged soil particles; NO₃⁻ does not (leaches)
  • DAP = (NH₄)₂HPO₄ — supplies both N and P; also used as fire retardant and yeast nutrient
  • Reference: MoE Chemistry Grade 12 Student Textbook, §3.3.2 + §3.3.3 (pp. 160–170)
