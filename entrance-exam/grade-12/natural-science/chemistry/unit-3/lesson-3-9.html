3.9.1 Tannery (Leather Manufacturing)
MoE Textbook, pp. 195–196
📌 Definitions

Tanning is the process of converting raw animal hides and skin to leather, using tannin. Leather is a durable and flexible material created by the tanning of animal hides and skin. Tannin is an acidic chemical that permanently alters the protein structure of a skin so that it can never return to raw hide again.

The Three Phases of Leather Production

Leather production involves preparatory stages, tanning, and crusting:

Phase 1 — Preparatory Stages

📌 Six preparatory steps

1. Curing — salting or drying the hide once removed from the animal. Prevents putrefaction of collagen from bacterial infection. Brine curing is the simplest and fastest method.

2. Soaking — cured hides soaked in water for hours to days to remove salt, dirt, blood, and excess animal fat.

3. Flesh removal — hides moved through a machine that strips flesh from the hide surface.

4. Hair removal (liming) — hides immersed in a mixture of lime and water for 1–10 days. Loosens hair from the skin, then mechanically removed.

5. Scudding — removal of hair and fat missed by machines, using a plastic tool or dull knife.

6. Deliming — removal of lime from the skin in a vat of acid. After this, the hide is ready for tanning.

Phase 2 — Tanning (Two Types)

🌿 Vegetable (Natural) Tanning

Skin placed in a solution of tannin.

Tannins occur naturally in the barks and leaves of many plants — chestnut, oak, tanoak, hemlock, quebracho, mangrove, wattle (acacia), myrobalan.

Produces flexible leather — used for shoes, luggage, and furniture.

Slower process (weeks to months).

⚗️ Mineral Tanning

Skin placed in solutions of chromium sulfate and other chromium salts.

Chrome tanning is faster — requires only 24 hours.

Produces leather that is greenish-blue in color (from chromium).

Produces stretchable leather — used for garments and handbags.

Faster process — much shorter than vegetable tanning.

Phase 3 — Crusting

📌 The final stage

The final stage in leather manufacturing. Includes dyeing, rolling the leather to make it strong, stretching it in a heat-controlled room, and covering the grain surface with chemical compounds such as wax, oil, and glazes to make the leather attractive.

📝 The three tanning questions from the textbook

1. Why do people prefer imported leather products over Ethiopian ones despite higher quality and lower cost locally?
2. Why are leather products being replaced by synthetic (artificial) materials?
3. What is the traditional way of tanning in Ethiopia? How does it compare to modern methods?

3.9.2 Food Processing and Preservation
MoE Textbook, pp. 197–198
📌 Definition

Food preservation is the process of treating and handling food to stop or greatly reduce spoilage, loss of quality, edibility, or nutritive value caused or accelerated by microorganisms.

📌 What preservation prevents

• Growth of bacteria, fungi, and other microorganisms
• Oxidation of fats (which causes rancidity)
• Loss of color, flavor, and nutritive value

Modern Methods of Preservation

❄️
Freezing
One of the most common methods, used commercially and domestically for a wide range of foods — potatoes, prepared foodstuffs, meats.
🧊
Freeze-Drying
Water vapour escapes gently through the cell structure, leaving the product close to its original shape, taste, and colour with no aroma loss. Used for coffee, tea, juices, vegetables, fish, meat.
🎁
Vacuum-Packing
Stores food in a vacuum environment (air-tight bag or bottle). Strips bacteria of the oxygen needed for survival, slowing spoilage. Common for nuts (reduces oxidation).
🧪
Chemical Preservatives
Both inorganic (NaCl, nitrates, sulfites, SO₂) and organic (lactic acid, propionic acid, citric acid, benzoic acid) preservatives extend shelf life.

Inorganic Preservatives

PreservativeHow it works
Sodium chloride (NaCl)Lowers water activity; causes plasmolysis by withdrawing water from cells
Nitrates and nitritesCuring agents for meats (hams, bacon, sausages); inhibit Clostridium botulinum under vacuum packaging
Sulfur dioxide (SO₂), sulfites (SO₃²⁻), bisulfite (HSO₃⁻), metabisulfites (S₂O₅²⁻)Form sulfurous acid in aqueous solutions — the actual antimicrobial agent. Used in the wine industry, dried fruits, and fruit juices. Also prevents enzymatic browning in cut potatoes and vegetables.

Organic Preservatives

📌 Common organic acid preservatives

• Lactic acid and lactates
• Propionic acid and propionates (calcium most common) — active against molds at pH < 6. Used in baked products and cheeses.
• Citric acid
• Acetic acid — found in vinegar (4–5%). Used in mayonnaise, pickles, ketchup. Most active against bacteria.
• Sorbic acid and sorbates
• Benzoic acid and benzoates
• Methyl and propyl parabens (benzoic acid derivatives)

📝 Wine yeasts are sulfite-resistant

Sulfites are widely used in the wine industry to sanitize equipment and reduce competing microorganisms — but wine yeasts are resistant to sulfites. This allows the desired fermentation to continue while other microbes are suppressed.

3.9.3 Manufacturing of Ethanol
MoE Textbook, pp. 199–200
📌 What is ethanol?

Ethanol is one of the constituents of all alcoholic beverages. Tella, Tej, beer, wine, Katikalla, ouzo, gin, and whisky contain ethanol.

Two Industrial Methods

🍯 1. Fermentation of Carbohydrates

Slow decomposition of carbohydrates (sucrose, starch, cellulose) by enzymes, producing ethanol and CO₂.

Sucrose → Glucose + Fructose (by invertase)
C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2CH₃CH₂OH + 2CO₂ (by zymase)

Fermentation can produce ethanol up to 12–15%. Higher concentrations kill the yeast. To produce stronger beverages, distillation is required.

Most Ethiopian liquor factories use molasses (a by-product of sugar industries) as raw material. In brewing, malted barley ("Bikil") is the starting material.

💨 2. Catalytic Hydration of Ethene

Most ethanol is manufactured at present by this method.

Ethene + steam at 573 K and 60 atm over H₃PO₄ catalyst:

CH₂=CH₂(g) + H₂O(g) → CH₃CH₂OH(g)

Faster, higher-yield process — used for industrial ethanol (fuel, solvent, chemical feedstock).

Fermentation — Step 1 (invertase)
C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ (sucrose) → C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose) + C₆H₁₂O₆ (fructose)
Fermentation — Step 2 (zymase)
C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2CH₃CH₂OH + 2CO₂
Catalytic Hydration of Ethene
CH₂=CH₂(g) + H₂O(g) —[H₃PO₄, 573 K, 60 atm]→ CH₃CH₂OH(g)
📝 Beer, wine, liquor

• Beer — from barley and hops; average alcohol 2–6% by volume
• Wine — from grapes (or other fruits); alcohol 10–16% by volume
• Liquor — made by distillation of fermented beverages; alcohol 30–45% by volume (cognac from grape wine, rum from sugarcane, whisky from rye)

3.9.4 Local Ethiopian Beverages — Araki and Tella
MoE Textbook, pp. 200–205
📌 Araki — Local Ethiopian distilled alcohol

Araki is the local Ethiopian alcohol which is prepared almost everywhere with certain local differences. The differences are in the ingredients, not in the process.

Traditional Preparation of Araki

  1. Prepare Bikel (malt) — Barley is germinated as in beer production.
  2. Prepare Tinses (starter) — Bikel is mixed with water and Gesho (Rhamnus prinoides) powder to make a starter. Left to ferment for about 4 days (varies by locality depending on humidity and temperature).
  3. Bake bread — Bread is baked from Teff, Barley, Wheat, and Sorghum, depending on availability and local preferences.
  4. Mix and ferment — Bread broken into small pieces, mixed with the starter, and left to ferment for 5–10 days.
  5. Add water — A proportional amount of water is added to liquefy the thick dough-like mixture. Left for 1–2 days for further fermentation.
  6. Boil and distill — The liquid mixture is boiled and distilled in the traditional way. The distillate is Araki.
  7. By-product: Atela — The leftover residue (un-distilled component) is locally called Atela and is usually used to feed cattle.
📝 Tella — Non-distilled local alcohol

Tella is a traditional non-distilled Ethiopian beverage. It is prepared similarly to beer — through fermentation of grains (barley, wheat, sorghum) with Gesho, but without distillation. Its alcohol content is lower (typically 2–6%) than Araki.

📝 The role of Gesho

Gesho (Rhamnus prinoides) is a plant whose leaves and twigs are used as a bittering and fermenting agent — the Ethiopian equivalent of hops in beer. It also provides antimicrobial properties that help control the fermentation.

3.9.5 Soap Manufacturing
MoE Textbook, pp. 198–201
📌 What are soaps?

Soaps are substances used to remove dirt. They are also called surfactants or surface-active agents because they reduce the surface tension of water and change surface properties.

📌 Chemical nature

Soaps are either sodium or potassium salts of higher (long-chain) carboxylic acids.

• Sodium salts → hard soaps
• Potassium salts → soft soaps

Soaps are prepared by boiling animal fat or vegetable oil with a base. The reaction is called saponification.

The Saponification Reaction

Animal Fat + NaOH → Soap + Glycerol
Fat + 3NaOH → 3C₁₇H₃₅COONa (soap) + Glycerol (C₃H₅(OH)₃)
📝 The two ends of a soap molecule

A soap molecule (e.g., C₁₇H₃₅COONa) has two distinct ends:

• Hydrophilic (water-loving) end: –COONa — the ionic "head" that dissolves in water
• Hydrophobic (water-fearing) end: the C₁₇H₃₅ hydrocarbon chain — the fatty "tail" that dissolves in grease/oil

This dual nature is why soap can lift grease off surfaces and suspend it in water.

Soap Molecule Structure
The dual nature of soap: a hydrophobic tail that dissolves in grease, and a hydrophilic head that dissolves in water.

Industrial Soap Manufacturing — 4 Steps

  1. Saponification — A mixture of tallow (animal fat) and coconut oil is mixed with sodium hydroxide and heated. The soap is the salt of a long-chain carboxylic acid.
  2. Glycerine removal — Glycerine is more valuable than soap, so most is removed. Adding salt causes soap to separate from glycerine in salt water.
  3. Soap purification — Any remaining NaOH is neutralized with a weak acid (e.g., citric acid); two-thirds of remaining water is removed.
  4. Finishing — Additives (preservatives, colour, perfume) are mixed in; soap is shaped into bars for sale.
📌 Types of soap by lye

• Hard soap — made with NaOH (caustic soda)
• Soft soap — made with KOH (caustic potash) or K₂CO₃ (potassium carbonate)

In industry, tallow, lard, cotton seed oil, palm oil, castor oil, olive oil, whale oil, and soybean oil are used to prepare soap.

3.9.6 Detergents
Soapless soaps
📌 What are detergents?

Detergents are cleaning agents often called soapless soaps because they lather well but are chemically different from ordinary soaps. Long open-chain alcohols and alkyl benzene sulphonic acid can be used for the production of detergents.

📌 Chemical nature

Detergents are sodium salts of sulphonated long-chain organic alcohols:

R–C₆H₄–SO₃Na

Where:
• R = an alkyl group with a chain of 10 to 18 carbon atoms
• –SO₃Na = the water-soluble (hydrophilic) group
• –R–C₆H₄ = the fat-soluble (hydrophobic) group

Advantage of Detergents over Soap

📌 The key advantage

Detergents lather well with both soft AND hard water, and even with water that contains common salt or acids. They are more soluble than soap in water, form stable emulsions with grease, and do not form a scum with hard water because their calcium and magnesium salts are soluble.

📝 Why soap fails in hard water

Hard water contains Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions. When soap (RCOO⁻Na⁺) meets these ions, it forms insoluble precipitates (scum) — calcium and magnesium salts of the fatty acid:

2RCOO⁻Na⁺ + Ca²⁺ → (RCOO)₂Ca↓ + 2Na⁺

This scum wastes soap and leaves deposits on clothes and skin. Detergents solve this problem because their Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ salts are soluble.

Example — Sodium Lauryl Sulfate

Step 1 — Alcohol + H₂SO₄
C₁₂H₂₅OH + H₂SO₄ → C₁₂H₂₅OSO₂OH + H₂O
Step 2 — Neutralization with NaOH
C₁₂H₂₅OSO₂OH + NaOH → C₁₂H₂₅OSO₂ONa + H₂O
📝 Dry cleaning — when water is not an option

Some clothes (like silk) lose quality when washed with water and ordinary soap. Dry cleaning uses organic chemicals that dissolve grease and stains without water:

• Tetrachloromethane (CCl₄)
• Tetrachloroethylene (Cl₂C=CCl₂)
• Benzene
• Gasoline

Example 1Vegetable vs Mineral Tanning
Problem: Compare vegetable (natural) and mineral tanning in terms of (a) chemical agent, (b) time required, and (c) typical leather products.
(a) Agent
Vegetable: tannin (natural, from bark and leaves)
Mineral: chromium sulfate (chrome tanning)
(b) Time
Vegetable: weeks to months
Mineral: only 24 hours
(c) Products
Vegetable: flexible leather — shoes, luggage, furniture
Mineral: stretchable leather (greenish-blue) — garments, handbags
✅ Vegetable = tannin, slow, flexible leather; Mineral = Cr-sulfate, fast (24 h), stretchable leather
Example 2Why Freeze-Drying is Superior
Problem: Explain why freeze-drying is considered a superior preservation method for certain foods.
Mechanism
Water vapour escapes through the cell structure of the product — a gentle process
Comparison
Other drying methods evaporate water from the surface, causing shrinkage and flavor loss
Result
The product keeps its original shape, taste, and colour — and there is no loss of aroma or flavour
Applications
Coffee, tea, juices, vegetables, fish, meat products
✅ Freeze-drying preserves shape, taste, colour, and aroma by gentle water vapour escape through cell structure
Example 3Why Fermentation Stops at 12–15%
Problem: Explain why fermentation stops naturally at 12–15% ethanol concentration, and what must be done to obtain higher concentrations.
Cause
At ~12–15% ethanol, the alcohol concentration becomes toxic to the yeast
Effect
The yeast cells die, and fermentation stops by itself
Solution
To obtain beverages with higher alcohol content (30–45% in liquors), you must distill the fermented mixture
Examples
Cognac (from grape wine), rum (from sugarcane), whisky (from rye) — all made by distillation
✅ Yeast dies above 12–15% ethanol; distillation is required for higher concentrations
Example 4Why Soap Fails in Hard Water
Problem: Explain why ordinary soap does not clean well in hard water, using a chemical equation. Why is detergent preferred in hard water?
Hard water
Contains dissolved Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions (from dissolved carbonates and sulfates)
Reaction
Soap anions react with Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ to form insoluble scum:
2RCOO⁻Na⁺ + Ca²⁺ → (RCOO)₂Ca↓ + 2Na⁺
Soap problem
The scum wastes soap, deposits on clothes and skin, and reduces cleaning efficiency
Detergent solution
Detergent's Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ salts are soluble, so no scum forms → detergents lather well even in hard water
✅ Soap forms insoluble scum with Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺; detergents form soluble salts, so they work in hard water
Example 5Araki Process vs Industrial Ethanol
Problem: Compare the traditional Ethiopian preparation of Araki with industrial ethanol production. Identify one similarity and one difference.
Similarity
Both rely on fermentation of carbohydrates followed by distillation
Difference (raw material)
Traditional uses locally-available cereals (barley, teff, wheat, sorghum); industrial often uses molasses or ethene
Difference (scale)
Traditional is small-scale, household-level; industrial is large-scale with controlled conditions
By-product
Traditional residue is Atela — used to feed cattle; industrial residues have separate management
✅ Same chemistry (fermentation + distillation); different raw materials, scale, and by-product use
