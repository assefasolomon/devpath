🌍 Natural Resources & Industry

Every product of the chemical industry begins with a raw material taken from the environment — from the air, the ocean, the earth's crust, or living things. This lesson surveys Earth's four resource spheres and how industry classifies them.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources

Introduction
What you will learn

In Lesson 3.1 you learned what industrial chemistry is. Now we ask: where do the raw materials come from? The Earth provides resources from four major compartments — atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere, and biosphere. Understanding these sources is the first step in any chemical industry.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define natural resources and raw materials
  • List the four spheres of the environment and their resources
  • Classify resources as renewable or non-renewable
  • Define industry and manufacturing industry
  • Classify chemical industries based on raw materials and product type
  • Relate raw materials to industrial products
📌
What are Natural Resources?
The raw materials of industry
📌 Natural Resources (MoE Textbook, p. 147)

Natural resources are the raw materials for the chemical industry which are obtained from the natural environment — from the atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere, and biosphere.

📌 Two types of raw materials

• Natural raw materials — obtained directly from the environment (e.g., air, seawater, mineral ores)
• Synthetic / derived raw materials — processed in one industry to serve as feedstock for another (e.g., ethanol from sugar for plastics)

📝 Everything traces back to nature

Even plastics, medicines, and synthetic fibers eventually trace back to natural sources — fossil fuels from the lithosphere, minerals from ores, or plants from the biosphere. Industry is fundamentally about transforming what nature provides.

🌐
The Four Spheres of Resources
MoE Textbook, p. 147

Natural resources come from four major compartments of the environment:

🌬️
1. Atmosphere
Contains approximately 5 × 10¹⁵ tons of gases. Composed mainly of N₂, O₂, CO₂, plus trace Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe.

Source: Nitrogen for fertilizers, oxygen for combustion, argon for welding, neon for lighting

💧
2. Hydrosphere
Ocean water amounts to about 1.5 × 10²¹ liters, with about 3.5% by mass dissolved material.

Source: Sodium chloride, magnesium, bromine from seawater

🪨
3. Lithosphere
The vast majority of elements are obtained from the earth's crust in the form of mineral ores, carbon, and hydrocarbons.

Source: Coal, natural gas, crude petroleum, metal ores

🌱
4. Biosphere
Vegetation and animals contribute raw materials to agro-based industries.

Source: Oils, fats, waxes, resins, sugar, natural fibers, leather

The Four Spheres — Visual Overview
📝 The atmosphere's unlimited supply

The natural supply of atmospheric gases (N₂, O₂, Ar, etc.) is essentially unlimited. This is why nitrogen fertilizer production (Haber process — Lesson 3.3) can never run out of its key raw material.

♻️
Renewable vs Non-Renewable Resources
The critical distinction
♻️ Renewable Resources

Resources that can be replenished through rapid natural cycles. They can increase in abundance through reproduction and use of simple substances.

Examples:
• Plants (crops, forests)
• Animals
• Wood and wood products
• Natural rubber
• Fibers (cotton, jute, wool, silk)
• Pulp products
• Leather
• Water and soil
• Solar energy

⛔ Non-Renewable Resources

Resources that cannot be replenished through natural processes. Available in limited amounts — once consumed, gone forever.

Examples:
• Fossil fuels (petrol, coal)
• Metals (iron, copper, gold, silver, lead, zinc)
• Minerals and salts (carbonates, phosphates, nitrates)

A substitute is needed once these run out.

📌 Why this matters for industry

Industries built on renewable resources (e.g., sugar from sugarcane, ethanol from fermentation) can be sustainable long-term.
Industries built on non-renewable resources (e.g., plastics from petroleum) face eventual shortages and must develop substitutes.

📝 Activity 3.3 (Textbook p. 148)
Although renewable resources can be replenished by natural processes, they are practically getting depleted. Similarly, non-renewable resources are over-used and may be exhausted shortly.

Think: How can chemical knowledge be used to tackle such problems? Share your views with the class.

Industry & Manufacturing
MoE Textbook, p. 148
📌 Industry

Industry is a well-organized facility with a high degree of automation and specialization where large-scale manufacturing of goods takes place. It can also include commercial activities that provide goods and services — agriculture, transportation, hospitality, and many others.

📌 Manufacturing Industry

A manufacturing industry is a compartment of industry or economy which is concerned with the production or making of goods out of raw materials by means of a system of organized labor.

📌 Chemical Industry

A chemical industry is a facility where industrial chemicals are manufactured. The products result from:

a. Chemical reaction between organic materials, inorganic materials, or both
b. Extraction, separation, or purification of natural products, with or without chemical reactions
c. The preparation of specifically valuable materials

📂
Classifying Chemical Industries
Two different ways

Method 1 — Based on Raw Material

TypeDescriptionExample
Uses natural raw materialsDirectly from the environmentSugar industry uses sugarcane
Uses products from other industriesProcessed inputs from elsewhereSoap industry uses caustic soda (NaOH) from the chlor-alkali industry

Method 2 — Based on Product Type

CategoryExamples
Food processing industriesFlour mills, canning, dairies
Beverages industriesBeer, wine, soft drinks, bottled water
Textiles industriesCotton mills, weaving, dyeing
Wearing apparel industriesGarment manufacturing
Leather industriesTanning, shoe and bag manufacturing
Paper and chemical industriesPulp and paper, basic chemicals
📝 Two lenses on the same industry

A soap factory could be classified either:
• By raw materials — "uses products from other industries" (NaOH from chlor-alkali, oils from agriculture)
• By product type — "chemical industry" (produces a chemical product)

Both classifications are valid — they answer different questions.

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1Identify the Source Sphere
Problem: For each of these raw materials, identify which sphere (atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere, or biosphere) provides it: (a) bromine, (b) sugarcane, (c) nitrogen, (d) iron ore.
(a) Bromine
Hydrosphere — extracted from seawater (3.5% dissolved material)
(b) Sugarcane
Biosphere — plant-based (agro-based raw material)
(c) Nitrogen
Atmosphere — makes up 78% of the air
(d) Iron ore
Lithosphere — mined from Earth's crust
✅ Bromine = hydrosphere; Sugarcane = biosphere; Nitrogen = atmosphere; Iron ore = lithosphere
Example 2Renewable or Non-Renewable?
Problem: Classify each of the following as renewable or non-renewable: (a) natural rubber, (b) copper, (c) petroleum, (d) solar energy, (e) cotton.
(a) Natural rubber
Renewable — comes from rubber trees which regrow
(b) Copper
Non-renewable — a metal from finite ore deposits
(c) Petroleum
Non-renewable — fossil fuel, formed over millions of years
(d) Solar energy
Renewable — inexhaustible on the human timescale
(e) Cotton
Renewable — plant fiber, regrows each season
✅ Renewable: rubber, solar, cotton | Non-renewable: copper, petroleum
Example 3Classify an Industry Two Ways
Problem: A brewery makes beer from barley. Classify this industry (a) by raw material and (b) by product type.
(a) By raw material
The brewery uses natural raw materials — barley is grown directly from the biosphere
(b) By product type
It is a beverages industry — producing a drinkable alcohol product
✅ (a) Uses natural raw materials | (b) Beverages industry
Example 4From Sphere to Product
Problem: Trace the source of the following products back to their raw materials and spheres: (a) glass, (b) plastic bottle, (c) soap.
(a) Glass
Made from silica sand (SiO₂), sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃), and limestone (CaCO₃) — all from the lithosphere
(b) Plastic bottle
Made from petroleum-based polymers — fossil fuels from the lithosphere
(c) Soap
Made from animal fat or vegetable oil (biosphere) + NaOH from chlor-alkali (NaCl from lithosphere/hydrosphere)
✅ Every product ultimately traces back to one or more of the four spheres
Example 5Why Substitutes Matter
Problem: Petroleum is a non-renewable resource. Explain why the chemical industry is actively developing substitutes for petroleum-based plastics.
Finite supply
Petroleum reserves are limited — once extracted, they cannot be regenerated within human timescales
Rising cost
As reserves decline, extraction cost rises — eventually making petroleum-based plastics uneconomical
Sustainability
Bioplastics from corn starch, sugarcane (biosphere sources) offer a renewable alternative
Environment
Bioplastics are often biodegradable, reducing pollution
✅ Petroleum is finite → industry must develop renewable substitutes to remain sustainable

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 3.2
✅ Key Points — Natural Resources & Industry
  • Natural resources = raw materials from the environment for the chemical industry
  • The four spheres are atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere, and biosphere
  • Atmosphere — ~5 × 10¹⁵ tons of gases (N₂, O₂, CO₂, noble gases)
  • Hydrosphere — ~1.5 × 10²¹ L water, ~3.5% dissolved salts (NaCl, Mg, Br₂)
  • Lithosphere — mineral ores, coal, natural gas, crude petroleum
  • Biosphere — vegetation and animals for agro-based industries (oils, fats, waxes, resins, sugar, fibers, leather)
  • Renewable resources — plants, animals, wood, rubber, fibers, water, soil, solar energy
  • Non-renewable resources — fossil fuels, metals, minerals, salts (finite, exhaustible)
  • Industry — organized facility for large-scale manufacturing
  • Chemical industry — manufactures chemicals by reactions, extraction, purification, or preparation of valuable materials
  • Chemical industries can be classified by raw material source (natural vs derived) or by product type (food, beverages, textiles, leather, paper, etc.)
  • Reference: MoE Chemistry Grade 12 Student Textbook, §3.2 (pp. 146–149)
