🧂 Sodium Carbonate & Sodium Hydroxide

Two of the most important industrial bases. Sodium carbonate (washing soda) is made from salt and limestone via the Solvay process; sodium hydroxide (caustic soda) is made by electrolysis of brine in the Castner-Kellner cell.

Introduction
What you will learn

Two inorganic compounds drive a huge portion of the chemical industry: sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) — the "washing soda" used to make glass — and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) — the "caustic soda" used to make soap. Both come from cheap, abundant raw materials.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • List the properties and uses of Na₂CO₃
  • Describe the Solvay process step-by-step
  • Write the overall reaction of the Solvay process
  • List the properties and uses of NaOH
  • Describe the Castner-Kellner process for NaOH production
  • Explain safety hazards of both compounds
🧂
Sodium Carbonate (Na₂CO₃)
Properties and Uses
📌 Names to know

• Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) — the anhydrous form
• Washing soda — the decahydrate: Na₂CO₃·10H₂O
• Soda ash — the commercial anhydrous form
• Baking soda — actually NaHCO₃ (sodium hydrogen carbonate), a different compound!

📌 Physical Properties (MoE Textbook, p. 178)

• White crystalline solid powder
• Exists as the decahydrate: Na₂CO₃·10H₂O
• Density = 2.54 g/cm³
• Purity > 98% (commercial)
• Melting point = 851 °C
• Boiling point = 1,600 °C
• Hygroscopic — absorbs moisture from the air
• Two commercial forms: light soda (0.7 g/mL) and dense soda (0.9 g/mL)

📌 Chemical Properties

• Dissolves easily in water — forms a moderately alkaline solution
• Insoluble in alcohol
• Anhydrous Na₂CO₃ is unaffected by heat — melts without decomposing
• Aqueous solution is somewhat alkaline due to hydrolysis:
Na₂CO₃(s) + 2H₂O(l) → H₂CO₃(aq) + 2Na⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq)
• Absorbs CO₂ from air to form sodium hydrogen carbonate:
Na₂CO₃(aq) + H₂O + CO₂(g) → 2NaHCO₃(aq)

Hydrolysis in Water (Basic Solution)
Na₂CO₃(s) + 2H₂O(l) → H₂CO₃(aq) + 2Na⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq)
Reaction with CO₂ from Air
Na₂CO₃(aq) + H₂O + CO₂(g) → 2NaHCO₃(aq)

Uses of Sodium Carbonate

🪟
Glass Manufacturing
Biggest use — about half of all Na₂CO₃ goes to glassmaking. Acts as a flux in melting silica.
🧼
Soap & Detergent
Used to make soap and detergent products.
📰
Pulp & Paper
Used in the Kraft pulping process.
🧵
Textiles
Used in textile processing and dyeing.
💧
Water Treatment
Used in drinking water treatment to adjust pH.
🧱
Brick Industry
Used as an additive in brick manufacturing.
🧽
Cleaning Agent
Washing soda is a classic household cleaner (removes grease).
🍽️
Food Preparation
Used in food processing (as a food additive).
🏭
The Solvay Process
Industrial production of Na₂CO₃
📌 The Solvay Process

Sodium carbonate is manufactured synthetically by the Solvay process (also called the ammonia-soda process). It involves a series of partial reactions, using brine (NaCl), limestone (CaCO₃), and ammonia (NH₃).

📌 Raw materials

• NaCl (brine) — source of sodium
• CaCO₃ (limestone) — source of CO₂ and CaO
• NH₃ — recycled catalyst (not consumed in overall reaction)

The Five Steps

1
Calcination of CaCO₃
CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
2
Slaking of lime
CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂
3
Formation of NaHCO₃
2NaCl + 2CO₂ + 2NH₃ + 2H₂O → 2NaHCO₃ + 2NH₄Cl
4
Decomposition of NaHCO₃
2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂
5
Recovery of NH₃
Ca(OH)₂ + 2NH₄Cl → CaCl₂ + 2NH₃ + 2H₂O
Overall Reaction
CaCO₃ + 2NaCl → Na₂CO₃ + CaCl₂
Solvay Process — Simplified Flow
📝 The clever trick: ammonia is recycled

NH₃ is used in Step 3 but regenerated in Step 5 — so it acts as a catalyst, not consumed overall. This is why the Solvay process is so economical.

📝 Natural vs synthetic sources

Historically, all Na₂CO₃ came from natural deposits (like trona ore) or was made by the Solvay process. Today, natural production has surpassed synthetic production — large natural deposits in the USA, Kenya, and elsewhere are now mined directly.

🧴
Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH)
Properties and Uses
📌 The compound

Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) is also known as caustic soda. It does not occur in nature — it must be manufactured.

📌 Physical Properties (MoE Textbook, p. 180)

• White, translucent crystalline solid
• Melting point = 591 K (318 °C)
• Stable compound
• Dissolves readily in water and moderately in alcohol
• Its solution has a bitter taste and a soapy feeling
• Strongly alkaline in nature
• Very corrosive — decomposes proteins at room temperature
• Causes chemical burns on contact with human skin

📌 Why "caustic"?

"Caustic" comes from the Latin causticus — "burning". NaOH is named caustic soda because of its corrosive action on many substances, including human tissue.

Uses of Sodium Hydroxide

🧼
Soap & Detergent
Main use — saponification of fats and oils to make soap. Also in detergents.
📰
Pulp & Paper
Used in the Kraft pulping process to break down wood lignin.
🔋
Alumina Extraction
Used in the Bayer process to extract Al₂O₃ from bauxite for aluminum production.
🧵
Textiles
Used in textile processing (mercerization of cotton).
💧
Drinking Water
Used to adjust pH and remove heavy metals in water treatment.
🧽
Washing Soda Production
Used to manufacture Na₂CO₃ (in alternative processes).
⚗️
Chemical Catalyst
Used in base catalysis, esterification, etherification reactions.
🚰
Drain Cleaners
Main ingredient in household and industrial drain cleaners.
🔋
The Castner-Kellner Process
Industrial production of NaOH
📌 What it is

NaOH is manufactured at large scale from sodium chloride (NaCl) and water (H₂O) by electrolysis. There are several methods:

1. Castner-Kellner process
2. Nelson Diaphragm cell
3. Loewig's process

📌 The Castner-Kellner principle

In the Castner-Kellner method, electrolysis of brine solution is performed to obtain sodium hydroxide.

The Castner-Kellner Cell

📌 Cell construction

• A rectangular steel tank
• Lined inside with ebonite
• Titanium anode (positive electrode)
• A layer of mercury at the bottom acts as the cathode (negative electrode)

How it works

  1. Ionization of brine occurs: 2NaCl → 2Na⁺ + 2Cl⁻
  2. Sodium ions (Na⁺) migrate to the mercury cathode and form a sodium amalgam (Na-Hg).
  3. Chloride ions (Cl⁻) migrate to the titanium anode and are discharged as chlorine gas (which exits from the top).
  4. The sodium amalgam is transferred to a separate chamber called the denuder.
  5. In the denuder, the amalgam is treated with water to form a sodium hydroxide solution.
  6. The solution is evaporated to obtain solid NaOH.
Ionization of Brine
2NaCl → 2Na⁺ + 2Cl⁻
At the Anode (Oxidation)
2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
At the Cathode (Reduction)
2Na⁺ + 2e⁻ → 2Na (dissolves in Hg to form amalgam)
In the Denuder (with water)
2Na(Hg) + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂(g) + 2Hg
📝 Why mercury?

Mercury is used because sodium dissolves readily in it to form an amalgam. This keeps the sodium separated from the water, preventing it from reacting with water directly. The mercury is recycled back to the cell. However, mercury is toxic — strict precautions are needed to prevent mercury losses.

📝 The efficiency advantage

The Castner-Kellner process is a very efficient way to obtain pure caustic soda from brine. The chlorine gas produced at the anode is a valuable by-product.

⚠️
Safety and Health Effects
Handling caustic compounds
📌 NaOH Safety (MoE Textbook, p. 181)

• Strong corrosive qualities — exposure to solid NaOH or its solution can cause skin and eye irritation
• Decomposes proteins at room temperature
• Causes chemical burns on contact with human skin
• High affinity for water — may form hydrates depending on concentration
• Some hydrates have melting points > 0 °C, so insulation or heating during storage may be needed
• Wear goggles, gloves, and lab coat when handling

📌 First aid

• Skin contact: Flush with plenty of water for at least 15 minutes, then seek medical attention
• Eye contact: Flush with water for at least 15 minutes while holding eyelids open, seek medical attention immediately
• Ingestion: Do NOT induce vomiting. Rinse mouth, drink water or milk, seek medical attention

📝 Na₂CO₃ is milder but still alkaline

Sodium carbonate is less dangerous than NaOH, but its aqueous solution is still moderately alkaline. Prolonged contact can irritate skin. Always handle with basic lab safety.

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1Name the Compound
Problem: Give the chemical formula for (a) washing soda, (b) soda ash, and (c) baking soda.
(a) Washing soda
Na₂CO₃·10H₂O — the decahydrate of sodium carbonate
(b) Soda ash
Na₂CO₃ — anhydrous sodium carbonate (commercial form)
(c) Baking soda
NaHCO₃ — sodium hydrogen carbonate (DIFFERENT compound)
✅ Washing soda = Na₂CO₃·10H₂O; Soda ash = Na₂CO₃; Baking soda = NaHCO₃
Example 2Write the Solvay Overall Reaction
Problem: Write the overall reaction of the Solvay process. Why is NH₃ not shown in the overall reaction?
Overall
CaCO₃ + 2NaCl → Na₂CO₃ + CaCl₂
NH₃ role
NH₃ is used in Step 3 and regenerated in Step 5
Catalyst
Because it is not consumed overall, NH₃ acts as a catalyst, so it doesn't appear in the net equation
✅ CaCO₃ + 2NaCl → Na₂CO₃ + CaCl₂; NH₃ is a recycled catalyst
Example 3Identify the By-Product
Problem: What is the main by-product of the Solvay process, and where does it come from?
By-product
CaCl₂ (calcium chloride)
Source
Formed in Step 5 when Ca(OH)₂ reacts with NH₄Cl: Ca(OH)₂ + 2NH₄Cl → CaCl₂ + 2NH₃ + 2H₂O
Fate
The NH₃ is recycled; the CaCl₂ is a by-product that must be disposed of or sold
✅ Main by-product = CaCl₂ (calcium chloride)
Example 4Why Mercury in Castner-Kellner?
Problem: Why is mercury used as the cathode in the Castner-Kellner process instead of a solid metal cathode?
Amalgam
Sodium dissolves readily in mercury to form a sodium amalgam (Na-Hg)
Separates Na from H₂O
This keeps the sodium separated from the water in the brine, preventing it from reacting directly with water
Reaction control
The amalgam is transferred to a separate denuder, where water reacts with Na to form NaOH safely
Mercury recycled
Mercury is recovered and recycled back to the cell — very efficient, but needs care because mercury is toxic
✅ Mercury forms a sodium amalgam that separates Na from water; the amalgam is then safely decomposed in the denuder
Example 5Why Does Na₂CO₃ Solution Feel Soapy?
Problem: Explain why an aqueous solution of Na₂CO₃ is alkaline, using the appropriate reaction equation.
Hydrolysis
The carbonate ion (CO₃²⁻) is the conjugate base of a weak acid (H₂CO₃). It hydrolyzes water:
Reaction
Na₂CO₃(s) + 2H₂O(l) → H₂CO₃(aq) + 2Na⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq)
Result
OH⁻ ions are produced → the solution is basic (that's why it feels soapy — saponification of skin oils)
✅ CO₃²⁻ hydrolyzes water to give OH⁻, making the solution alkaline (soapy feel)

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 3.6
✅ Key Points — Na₂CO₃ & NaOH
  • Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) — also called soda ash (anhydrous) or washing soda (decahydrate Na₂CO₃·10H₂O)
  • Physical: white crystalline solid; density 2.54 g/cm³; mp 851 °C; bp 1,600 °C; hygroscopic; two forms — light (0.7 g/mL) and dense (0.9 g/mL)
  • Chemical: hydrolyzes in water to give alkaline solution; absorbs CO₂ from air to form NaHCO₃
  • Main use: glass manufacturing (~50%); also soaps, detergents, paper, textiles, water treatment, cleaning
  • Solvay process — 5 steps: calcination → slaking → main reaction → decomposition → ammonia recovery
  • Solvay overall reaction: CaCO₃ + 2NaCl → Na₂CO₃ + CaCl₂
  • NH₃ acts as a recycled catalyst in the Solvay process
  • Main by-product: CaCl₂
  • Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) — also called caustic soda; does not occur in nature
  • Physical: white translucent solid; mp 591 K; strongly alkaline; strongly corrosive; causes chemical burns
  • Main use: soap and detergent manufacturing; also paper, alumina extraction, textiles, water treatment, drain cleaners
  • Castner-Kellner process: electrolysis of brine with titanium anode + mercury cathode → Cl₂ (anode) + Na-Hg amalgam; amalgam treated with water in denuder → NaOH
  • Mercury is recycled but is toxic — requires careful handling
  • Reference: MoE Chemistry Grade 12 Student Textbook, §3.3.6 + §3.3.7 (pp. 178–182)
