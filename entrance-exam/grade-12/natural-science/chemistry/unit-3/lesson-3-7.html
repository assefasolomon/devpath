🐛 Pesticides & Herbicides

Pesticides protect crops from insects, diseases, and weeds — but the chemistry matters. This lesson covers the four main synthetic classes, Ethiopia's traditional botanical pesticides, and the herbicide groups that control weeds in modern agriculture.

📖 Reading: ~22 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Every year, insects, weeds, and plant diseases destroy a large fraction of the world's food crops. Pesticides are chemicals used to prevent or control these pests, decreasing yield losses and maintaining high product quality. But they must be used carefully — many are toxic to humans and the environment.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define pesticides and explain their role in agriculture
  • List and describe the four main classes of synthetic pesticides
  • Explain the properties, uses, and risks of each class
  • Describe Ethiopian botanical pesticides (especially Neem Leaf)
  • Define herbicides and distinguish selective from non-selective types
  • List the eight main herbicide groups with examples
🐛
What are Pesticides?
Definition and purpose
📌 Definition (MoE Textbook, p. 173)

Pesticides are chemicals used to prevent or control pests, diseases, weeds, and other plant pathogens. They decrease yield losses and help maintain high product quality.

📌 Why classify pesticides?

Chemical pesticides can be classified according to their chemical composition. This allows uniform and scientific grouping to establish a correlation between:

• Structure
• Activity
• Toxicity
• Degradation mechanisms

📝 Why the classification matters

Understanding the chemical class helps farmers and regulators predict: How long does the pesticide persist in the environment? How toxic is it to non-target organisms? Does it accumulate in fatty tissues? How quickly does it break down?

🧪
The Four Main Classes of Pesticides
MoE Textbook, Table 3.1
🔴 1. Organochlorines
Composed of C, H, Cl, O — nonpolar and lipophilic

• Soluble in lipids
• Accumulate in fatty tissue of animals
• Transferred through the food chain
• Toxic to a variety of animals
• Long-term persistence in the environment

Example: Endosulfan

🟡 2. Organophosphates
Central phosphorus atom — aliphatic, cyclic, or heterocyclic

• Soluble in organic solvents and water
• Infiltrate and reach groundwater
• Less persistent than chlorinated hydrocarbons
• Some affect the central nervous system
• Absorbed by plants, then transferred to leaves and stems
• More stable and less toxic in the environment than organochlorines

Example: Malathion

🟠 3. Carbamates
Based on plant alkaloid Physostigma venenosum

• Carbamic acid derivatives
• Kill a limited spectrum of insects
• Highly toxic to vertebrates
• Relatively low persistence

Example: Carbofuran

🟢 4. Pyrethroids
Synthetic pyrethrins — from Chrysanthemum cinereifolium petals

• Affect the nervous system — but less than other pesticides
• The safest in terms of their use
• Some used as household insecticides
• Derived from natural plant compounds

Example: Permethrin

🟣 5. Biological Pesticides
Viruses, microorganisms, or their metabolic products

• Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) and its subspecies are most commonly used
• Applied against forest pests and crops
• Particularly effective against butterflies and caterpillars
• Natural, targeted approach — minimal environmental impact

📝 The persistence trend

The general trend of environmental persistence:
Organochlorines (very long, years) > Organophosphates / Carbamates (weeks to months) > Pyrethroids (days) > Biological (very short).

🌿
Ethiopian Botanical Pesticides
Traditional, locally-produced solutions
📌 What are botanical pesticides?

Botanical pesticides are pesticides extracted from various plant parts — stems, seeds, roots, leaves, and flower heads — of different plant species. Traditionally produced by Ethiopian farmers in different districts and applied to fruits, vegetables, and other crops.

📌 Why botanicals are valued

• Broad spectrum of activity
• Easy to process and use
• Short residual activity — break down quickly
• Do not accumulate in the environment
• Do not accumulate in fatty tissues of warm-blooded animals
• Made from locally available resources

Common Ethiopian Botanical Pesticides

🌳 Neem Leaf (Azadirachta indica)
Neem has long been used for its medicinal and culinary properties. It is also known to be used as a deterrent to pests. This medicinal herb has a bitter taste and strong odor that may keep bugs away from plants — but is non-toxic to animals, birds, plants, and humans.

How to use:
• Spray Neem oil on young plants — effective for about 22 days
• Add Neem oil to a dash of liquid soap + warm water and stir slowly
• Use immediately in a spray bottle as an insect/pest repellent
• Alternatively, cut and collect Neem leaves and simply place them in your home or on a table as a pest/insect repellent

Other Ethiopian Botanical Pesticides

Herbicides (Chemical Weed Killers)
Controlling unwanted plants
📌 Definition (MoE Textbook, p. 177)

Herbicides, commonly known as weed killers, are substances used to control unwanted plants.

Two Types of Herbicides

🎯 Selective Herbicides
Target specific weeds only

• Control specific weed species
• Leave the desired crop relatively unharmed
• Allow farmers to spray directly on crops without damaging them

Examples: 2,4-D (kills broadleaf weeds in grass crops like wheat)

💥 Non-Selective Herbicides
Kill all plant material

• Also called total weed killers in commercial products
• Kill ALL plant material they contact
• Used on industrial sites, roadsides, or before planting

Examples: Glyphosate (kills everything it touches)

📝 Why herbicides matter

Herbicides have largely replaced mechanical methods of weed control in countries where intensive and highly mechanized agriculture is practiced. Instead of hand-weeding, farmers spray — reducing labor cost and increasing efficiency.

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1Classify a Pesticide
Problem: The pesticide DDT is highly soluble in lipids and accumulates in animal fatty tissue. To which class of pesticide does it most likely belong?
Key clue 1
Soluble in lipids — a hallmark of a specific class
Key clue 2
Accumulates in fatty tissue — bioaccumulation, another hallmark
Class
These two features define organochlorines — nonpolar, lipophilic, long persistence
✅ DDT is an organochlorine pesticide
Example 2Why Pyrethroids Are Safest
Problem: Explain why pyrethroids are considered the safest class of pesticides for general use.
Origin
Derived from natural pyrethrins found in chrysanthemum flowers
Mechanism
Affect the nervous system, but less severely than organophosphates or carbamates
Degradation
Break down quickly in the environment — short persistence
Uses
Some are safe enough for household insecticides
✅ Pyrethroids: natural origin, mild mechanism, rapid breakdown, safe for household use
Example 3Why Botanical Pesticides?
Problem: Give three reasons why Ethiopian farmers use botanical pesticides like Neem Leaf instead of synthetic ones.
Availability
Locally available — no cost to buy, easy to obtain from local plants
Safety
Non-toxic to animals, birds, plants, humans — safe for the farmer and family
Environment
Short residual activity — do not accumulate in the environment or in fatty tissues
✅ Local availability + safety to non-target organisms + environmental friendliness
Example 4Selective vs Non-Selective
Problem: A farmer has a field of wheat with broadleaf weeds growing among the wheat plants. Should the farmer use a selective or non-selective herbicide? Explain.
Identify problem
The weeds are broadleaf plants; the crop is wheat (a grass) — they are different plant types
Consider non-selective
Non-selective (e.g., glyphosate) would kill everything — including the wheat
Choose selective
A selective herbicide like 2,4-D kills broadleaf weeds while leaving grass crops (wheat) unharmed
✅ Use a selective herbicide (like 2,4-D) — it targets broadleaf weeds while sparing the wheat
Example 5Persistence Ranking
Problem: Rank the following pesticides from most persistent to least persistent in the environment: organochlorines, pyrethroids, organophosphates, biological pesticides.
Organochlorines
Most persistent — long-term persistence, accumulate in fatty tissue, transfer through food chain
Organophosphates
Moderate — less persistent than organochlorines but still reaches groundwater
Pyrethroids
Short — break down quickly in the environment
Biological
Least persistent — natural microorganisms, minimal environmental impact
✅ Persistence order: Organochlorines > Organophosphates > Pyrethroids > Biological

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 3.7
✅ Key Points — Pesticides & Herbicides
  • Pesticides = chemicals that prevent or control pests, diseases, weeds, and plant pathogens
  • Classified by chemical composition to correlate structure, activity, toxicity, and degradation
  • Organochlorines — C, H, Cl, O; soluble in lipids; accumulate in fatty tissue; long persistence (e.g., Endosulfan)
  • Organophosphates — central P atom; soluble in water and organics; reach groundwater; affect CNS (e.g., Malathion)
  • Carbamates — carbamic acid derivatives; limited spectrum; highly toxic to vertebrates (e.g., Carbofuran)
  • Pyrethroids — from chrysanthemum; safest class; used as household insecticides (e.g., Permethrin)
  • Biological pesticides — Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt); targeted at caterpillars and butterflies
  • Botanical pesticides — extracted from plants; Ethiopian farmers use Neem Leaf, Salt Spray, Onion and Garlic Spray
  • Neem Leaf (Azadirachta indica) — bitter taste, strong odor; non-toxic to humans and animals; effective ~22 days
  • Herbicides = weed killers; either selective (target specific weeds) or non-selective (kill all plants)
  • Selective herbicide example: 2,4-D (kills broadleaf weeds without harming grass crops like wheat)
  • Non-selective herbicide example: Glyphosate (total weed killer)
  • Eight herbicide groups: chlorophenoxy acids, triazines, organic phosphorus chemicals, amides, thiocarbamates, dinitroanilines, chloroaliphatics, inorganic chemicals
  • Reference: MoE Chemistry Grade 12 Student Textbook, §3.3.5 (pp. 173–177)
