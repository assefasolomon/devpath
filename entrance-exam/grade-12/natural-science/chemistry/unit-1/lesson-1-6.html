🛡️ Buffer Solutions

Add a drop of strong acid to pure water — pH plunges. Add it to a buffer — pH barely moves. Buffers are pH guardians that keep biological systems alive and chemical reactions under control.

📖 Reading: ~22 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources

Introduction
What you will learn

Your blood stays at pH 7.4 even when you eat acidic or basic foods. Your stomach stays at pH 1.5 while digesting. How? Buffers. A buffer is a solution that resists pH changes when a small amount of acid or base is added.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define a buffer and identify its components
  • Explain how buffers neutralize added acid or base
  • Apply the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation
  • Calculate the pH of a buffer
  • Understand buffer capacity and its limits
  • Recognize biological buffers
📌
6.1 What is a Buffer?
Definition and composition
📌 Buffer Definition

A buffer is a solution that resists changes in pH when a small amount of acid or base is added, or when the solution is diluted with pure solvent.

📌 Buffer Composition

A buffer is made from an acid-base conjugate pair:

• A weak acid + its conjugate base (salt) — e.g., CH₃COOH + CH₃COONa
OR
• A weak base + its conjugate acid (salt) — e.g., NH₃ + NH₄Cl

📝 Why conjugate pairs?

A buffer must have: (1) a large concentration of acid to react with added base, and (2) a large concentration of base to react with added acid. These requirements are perfectly satisfied by a conjugate acid-base pair.

Example Buffer — Acetic Acid + Sodium Acetate

When CH₃COOH and CH₃COONa are dissolved together in water:

  • CH₃COONa is a strong electrolyte → dissociates 100% into CH₃COO⁻ and Na⁺
  • CH₃COOH is a weak acid → partially ionizes
  • The CH₃COO⁻ from the salt suppresses the ionization of CH₃COOH (common ion effect)

The result: significant concentrations of both CH₃COOH and CH₃COO⁻ in the same solution.

⚙️
6.2 How Buffers Resist pH Changes
Buffer action

The acetic acid/acetate buffer system (CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻) works by consuming added H⁺ or OH⁻ ions:

When H⁺ is Added

📌 Added acid reacts with conjugate base

CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq) → CH₃COOH(aq)

The acetate ion (conjugate base) neutralizes the added H⁺, forming more acetic acid. pH stays roughly constant.

When OH⁻ is Added

📌 Added base reacts with weak acid

CH₃COOH(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)

The acetic acid neutralizes the added OH⁻, forming more acetate. pH stays roughly constant.

Buffer Action — Both Directions

Comparing Buffered vs Unbuffered

SolutionInitial pHAfter 0.010 mol NaOHAfter 0.010 mol HCl
Unbuffered (dilute HCl)4.812.02.0
Buffered (CH₃COOH/CH₃COONa)4.84.84.7
📝 Conclusion

The buffered solution's pH barely changes (±0.1 unit), while the unbuffered solution's pH shifts by 7+ units!

🔢
6.3 The Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation
The key formula for buffer pH

The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation lets us calculate the pH of a buffer directly from the concentrations of the acid and conjugate base:

📌 Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation (acidic buffer)

pH = pKa + log([conjugate base] / [weak acid])

Where pKa = −log(Ka).

📌 For a Basic Buffer (weak base + conjugate acid)

pOH = pKb + log([conjugate acid] / [weak base])

Then pH = 14 − pOH.

📝 Two useful shortcuts

• When [acid] = [conjugate base], then pH = pKa
• When [base] = [conjugate acid], then pOH = pKb

A buffer has its maximum capacity when the acid and base concentrations are equal — because it can resist both added acid AND added base equally well.

Buffer Capacity and pH Range

📌 Buffer Capacity

The amount of acid or base a buffer can neutralize before the pH begins to change appreciably. A buffer has maximum capacity when:

pH = pKa (i.e., [A⁻] = [HA])

📝 Effective pH range

A buffer is effective in the range pKa ± 1. Outside this range, one component dominates and buffering action is poor.

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1pH of an Ammonia/Ammonium Buffer
Problem: What is the pH of a buffer solution consisting of 0.035 M NH₃ and 0.050 M NH₄⁺? Ka(NH₄⁺) = 5.6 × 10⁻¹⁰.
Identify
NH₄⁺ is the weak acid, NH₃ is the conjugate base. Use Henderson-Hasselbalch for acids.
pKa
pKa = −log(5.6×10⁻¹⁰) = 9.25
HHE
pH = pKa + log([NH₃]/[NH₄⁺])
Substitute
pH = 9.25 + log(0.035/0.050) = 9.25 + log(0.7)
Solve
pH = 9.25 + (−0.155) = 9.10
✅ pH = 9.10
Example 2Buffer from NH₃ and NH₄Cl
Problem: A buffer is made by mixing 0.060 M NH₃ with 0.040 M NH₄Cl. What is the pH? Kb(NH₃) = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵.
Approach
Use HHE for bases. NH₄⁺ is the conjugate acid (from NH₄Cl).
pKb
pKb = −log(1.8×10⁻⁵) = 4.74
pOH HHE
pOH = pKb + log([NH₄⁺]/[NH₃])
Substitute
pOH = 4.74 + log(0.040/0.060) = 4.74 + (−0.176)
pOH and pH
pOH = 4.56
pH = 14 − 4.56 = 9.44
✅ pH = 9.44
Example 3Acetic Acid/Acetate Buffer pH
Problem: Calculate the pH of a buffer containing 0.40 M CH₃COOH and 0.20 M CH₃COO⁻. Ka(CH₃COOH) = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵.
pKa
pKa = −log(1.8×10⁻⁵) = 4.74
HHE
pH = pKa + log([CH₃COO⁻]/[CH₃COOH])
Substitute
pH = 4.74 + log(0.20/0.40) = 4.74 + log(0.50)
Solve
pH = 4.74 + (−0.30) = 4.44
✅ pH = 4.44
Example 4Which Buffer is Best?
Problem: You need a buffer at pH 5.0. Which acid would you choose? (a) HF (Ka = 6.8×10⁻⁴), (b) CH₃COOH (Ka = 1.8×10⁻⁵), (c) HCN (Ka = 4.9×10⁻¹⁰).
Rule
Choose the acid whose pKa is closest to the target pH. The buffer's effective range is pKa ± 1.
Compare pKa
(a) HF: pKa = 3.17
(b) CH₃COOH: pKa = 4.74
(c) HCN: pKa = 9.31
Match
CH₃COOH (pKa = 4.74) is closest to pH 5.0
✅ Use acetic acid / sodium acetate (CH₃COOH/CH₃COONa)
Example 5Buffer pH After Adding Acid
Problem: A buffer contains 0.10 M CH₃COOH and 0.10 M CH₃COONa (pH = 4.74). What is the pH after adding 0.010 mol HCl to 1.0 L of this buffer? (Assume volume doesn't change significantly.)
Reaction
The added H⁺ reacts with the conjugate base:
CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃COOH
New concentrations
[CH₃COO⁻] = 0.10 − 0.010 = 0.090 M
[CH₃COOH] = 0.10 + 0.010 = 0.110 M
HHE
pH = 4.74 + log(0.090/0.110)
Solve
pH = 4.74 + log(0.818) = 4.74 + (−0.087) = 4.65
✅ pH changed from 4.74 to 4.65 — only 0.09 units! (Unbuffered would change by 7 units.)

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 1.6
✅ Key Points — Buffer Solutions
  • A buffer resists pH changes when small amounts of acid or base are added
  • Buffer = weak acid + its conjugate base OR weak base + its conjugate acid
  • Added acid is neutralized by the conjugate base: A⁻ + H⁺ → HA
  • Added base is neutralized by the weak acid: HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O
  • Henderson-Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]) for acidic buffers
  • For basic buffers: pOH = pKb + log([HB⁺]/[B])
  • Maximum buffer capacity when pH = pKa ([acid] = [base])
  • Effective pH range: pKa ± 1
  • Blood buffer: H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻ maintains pH 7.4
  • Buffers are essential in biology, medicine, and industrial chemistry
