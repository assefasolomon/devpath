💪 Strengths of Acids and Bases

Not all acids are created equal. HCl ionizes 100% in water — acetic acid only about 1%. Learn how to quantify the strength of acids and bases using three powerful tools: concentration, pH, and percent ionization.

📖 Reading: ~18 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Why is HCl a "strong" acid but acetic acid "weak"? The answer lies in how much each acid ionizes in water. A strong acid completely dissociates into ions; a weak acid only partially ionizes.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Distinguish between strong and weak acids and bases
  • Compare strengths using H₃O⁺ concentration
  • Use pH/pOH to compare strengths
  • Calculate percent ionization of weak acids and bases
  • Apply the equilibrium direction rule for acid-base reactions
⚡
3.1 Strong vs Weak Acids and Bases
The ionization difference
📌 Strong Acid

An acid that completely ionizes in water (essentially 100%). For example:

HCl(aq) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)

Common strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, HClO₄, H₂SO₄ (first ionization)

📌 Weak Acid

An acid that partially ionizes in water (usually 1–5%). It reaches equilibrium. Example:

CH₃COOH(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)

Common weak acids: acetic acid, formic acid, HF, HCN, H₂CO₃

📌 Strong vs Weak Bases

• Strong base: completely ionizes (all Group IA hydroxides + Ba(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ca(OH)₂)
• Weak base: partially ionizes (NH₃, amines, most other hydroxides)

📝 Important distinction

Strength ≠ Concentration!
• Strength = how completely it ionizes (a property of the substance)
• Concentration = how much is dissolved in a given volume (a property of the solution)

You can have a dilute solution of a strong acid, or a concentrated solution of a weak acid.

Visual Comparison — 0.1 M Solutions

Ionization at the Same Concentration
Both solutions have the same concentration (0.1 M) but HCl produces 75× more H₃O⁺ than acetic acid.

3.2 Three Measures of Acid Strength
Comparing acids and bases quantitatively

Measure 1 — Concentration of H₃O⁺ (or OH⁻)

The most direct measure. If two acids have the same initial concentration, the one that produces more H₃O⁺ is stronger.

📌 Rule

For solutions of the same concentration:
• Stronger acid → higher [H₃O⁺]
• Stronger base → higher [OH⁻]

Measure 2 — pH and pOH

Since pH = −log[H₃O⁺], higher [H₃O⁺] means lower pH.

📌 Rule

For solutions of the same concentration:
• Stronger acid → lower pH
• Stronger base → lower pOH (and higher pH)

Measure 3 — Percent Ionization

The most informative measure — it tells us what fraction of the acid has actually ionized.

📌 Percent Ionization

% Ionization = ([ionized acid] / [initial acid]) × 100%

Same formula for bases using [ionized base] / [initial base].

📝 Typical values

• Strong acids: ~100% ionization
• Weak acids: 1–5% ionization
• The lower the percent ionization, the weaker the acid

📉
3.3 How to Calculate Percent Ionization
Step-by-step

Consider a weak acid HA with initial concentration C:

ICE Table for Weak Acid HA
HAH₂O⇌ H₃O⁺A⁻
InitialC—00
Change−x—+x+x
EquilibriumC − x—xx

At equilibrium, the amount of ionized acid is x, which equals [H₃O⁺]. So:

📌 Percent Ionization Formula

% ionization = (x / C) × 100% = ([H₃O⁺] / C) × 100%

Where C is the initial acid concentration.

📝 The 5% rule

When solving weak acid problems, if percent ionization is ≤ 5%, you can use the approximation C − x ≈ C to simplify the math. If more than 5%, you must use the quadratic formula.

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Compare Two Acids at Same Concentration
Problem: 0.10 M HCl has pH = 1.00. 0.10 M CH₃COOH has pH = 2.89. Which is the stronger acid, and by what factor is [H₃O⁺] different?
Find [H₃O⁺]
[H₃O⁺] of HCl = 10⁻¹·⁰⁰ = 0.10 M
[H₃O⁺] of CH₃COOH = 10⁻²·⁸⁹ = 1.3 × 10⁻³ M
Compare
HCl gives more H₃O⁺ → HCl is stronger
Factor
0.10 / 1.3×10⁻³ ≈ 77
✅ HCl is ~77× stronger than CH₃COOH at the same concentration
Example 2Percent Ionization of Acetic Acid
Problem: A 0.100 M solution of acetic acid has [H₃O⁺] = 1.34 × 10⁻³ M at equilibrium. Calculate the percent ionization.
Formula
% ionization = ([H₃O⁺] / C) × 100%
Substitute
% = (1.34×10⁻³ / 0.100) × 100%
Compute
% = 0.0134 × 100 = 1.34%
Check rule
1.34% ≤ 5% → approximation valid
✅ Percent ionization = 1.34%
Example 3Percent Ionization of Nitrous Acid
Problem: A 0.125 M solution of nitrous acid (HNO₂) has pH = 2.09. Calculate the percent ionization.
Find [H₃O⁺]
[H₃O⁺] = 10⁻²·⁰⁹ = 8.1 × 10⁻³ M
Formula
% ionization = ([H₃O⁺] / C) × 100%
Substitute
% = (8.1×10⁻³ / 0.125) × 100%
Compute
% ≈ 6.5%
Check rule
6.5% > 5% → approximation NOT valid; must use quadratic. (Here just reporting % ionization.)
✅ Percent ionization ≈ 6.5%
Example 4Identify Strongest Base
Problem: Rank these bases from strongest to weakest: NH₃ (Kb = 1.8×10⁻⁵), CH₃NH₂ (Kb = 4.4×10⁻⁴), C₅H₅N (Kb = 1.7×10⁻⁹).
Rule
Higher Kb → stronger base
Compare
CH₃NH₂ (4.4×10⁻⁴) > NH₃ (1.8×10⁻⁵) > C₅H₅N (1.7×10⁻⁹)
✅ Strongest → weakest: CH₃NH₂ > NH₃ > C₅H₅N
Example 5Direction of Acid-Base Reaction
Problem: Predict the direction of the reaction: HCN + HCOO⁻ ⇌ CN⁻ + HCOOH. Given: Ka(HCN) = 4.9×10⁻¹⁰, Ka(HCOOH) = 1.7×10⁻⁴.
Rule
Reaction favors formation of the weaker acid and weaker base.
Compare Ka
HCOOH (1.7×10⁻⁴) is stronger than HCN (4.9×10⁻¹⁰)
Which side
Products contain weaker acid (HCN) and weaker base (CN⁻)... wait — check carefully.
Analysis
Reactants: HCN (weak acid) + HCOO⁻ (strong base from weak acid). Products: CN⁻ (strong base) + HCOOH (strong acid). Reactants are more stable.
✅ Equilibrium lies to the left (favors HCN + HCOO⁻)

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 1.3
✅ Key Points — Strengths of Acids & Bases
  • Strong acids ionize 100%; weak acids ionize partially (1–5%)
  • Common strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, HClO₄, H₂SO₄
  • Common strong bases: Group IA hydroxides + Ba(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ca(OH)₂
  • Strength ≠ concentration — a dilute strong acid is still strong
  • Stronger acid → higher [H₃O⁺] → lower pH (at same concentration)
  • Percent ionization = ([H₃O⁺] / C) × 100%
  • The 5% rule: use approximation C − x ≈ C only if % ionization ≤ 5%
  • Higher Ka → stronger acid; higher Kb → stronger base
  • Reactions proceed toward the weaker acid and base
