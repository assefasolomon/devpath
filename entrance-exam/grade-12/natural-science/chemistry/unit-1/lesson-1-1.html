Unit 1 · Acid-Base Equilibria · Natural Science

⚗️ Acid-Base Concepts

Chemistry has three major ways of defining acids and bases — Arrhenius, Brønsted-Lowry, and Lewis. Each one extends the last, covering more reactions. Master all three and you'll understand acids and bases from any angle.

📖 Reading: ~22 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Acids and bases are among the most familiar chemistry concepts — from vinegar (acetic acid) to drain cleaner (sodium hydroxide) and ammonia glass cleaner. But defining what makes something an acid has taken chemists over a century, with three successful theories built on top of each other.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe the Arrhenius definition and its limitations
  • State the Brønsted-Lowry definition with conjugate acid-base pairs
  • State the Lewis definition with electron-pair donors and acceptors
  • Identify conjugate pairs in acid-base reactions
  • Explain amphiprotic species and autoionization
  • Rank the relative strengths of conjugate pairs
🧪
1.1 Arrhenius Concept
The first successful theory (1884)

The Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius framed the first successful concept of acids and bases. He defined them in terms of their effect on water:

📌 Arrhenius Definition

• Arrhenius acid — a substance that increases the concentration of H⁺ (proton) in aqueous solution
• Arrhenius base — a substance that increases the concentration of OH⁻ (hydroxide ion) in aqueous solution

Examples

Arrhenius Acid — Perchloric Acid

HClO₄(aq) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + ClO₄⁻(aq)

Strong acids (completely ionize in water): HClO₄, H₂SO₄, HI, HBr, HCl, HNO₃

Arrhenius Base — Sodium Hydroxide

NaOH(s) —(H₂O)→ Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

Strong bases: hydroxides of Group IA (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs) and Group IIA (except Be)

📝 Limitations of Arrhenius Theory

✗ Limited to aqueous solutions only
✗ Requires the base to contain OH⁻ — can't explain why NH₃ is a base
✗ Doesn't explain reactions in non-aqueous solvents

⚛️
1.2 Brønsted-Lowry Concept
Proton transfer (1923)

In 1923, J. N. Brønsted (Denmark) and T. M. Lowry (Great Britain) independently proposed a broader theory. They described acid-base reactions as proton-transfer reactions.

📌 Brønsted-Lowry Definition

• Acid — a proton (H⁺) donor
• Base — a proton (H⁺) acceptor

Ionization of Ammonia in Water

NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

Water donates a proton (acts as acid) to ammonia, which accepts it (acts as base).

📝 Advantage over Arrhenius

✓ Works in any solvent, not just water
✓ Bases do not need to contain OH⁻
✓ Explains NH₃'s basic behavior

Conjugate Acid-Base Pairs

When a Brønsted-Lowry acid donates a proton, it becomes a conjugate base. When a base accepts a proton, it becomes a conjugate acid. Together they form a conjugate acid-base pair.

📌 Conjugate Pair Rules

• The conjugate base has one fewer H and one more negative charge than the acid
• The conjugate acid has one more H and one fewer negative charge than the base

Conjugate Pairs — Acetic Acid
CH₃COOH donates H⁺ → becomes CH₃COO⁻
H₂O accepts H⁺ → becomes H₃O⁺

Strengths of Conjugate Pairs

📌 The Inverse Rule

• The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base
• The stronger the base, the weaker its conjugate acid

A reaction proceeds in the direction that forms the weaker acid and weaker base.

📝 Example

HCl is a strong acid → Cl⁻ is a very weak base.
CH₃COOH is a weak acid → CH₃COO⁻ is a moderately strong base.

Autoionization of Water

📌 Self-Ionization

Water can act as both an acid and a base — it autoionizes:

2 H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

This is the equilibrium reaction that gives water its very small electrical conductivity and its ion-product constant Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C.

⚡
1.3 Lewis Concept
Electron-pair theory (1923)

G. N. Lewis proposed the most general definition — one based on electron-pair transfer, not just protons.

📌 Lewis Definition

• Lewis base — any species that donates an electron pair to form a covalent bond
• Lewis acid — any species that accepts an electron pair to form a covalent bond

BF₃ + NH₃ → Lewis Acid-Base Reaction
BF₃ accepts a lone electron pair from NH₃ — forming a coordinate covalent bond.
📝 Note

Any molecule or negative ion with an excess of electrons (lone pairs) is a Lewis base. Any electron-deficient molecule or positive ion is a Lewis acid.

Comparing the Three Definitions

🔴 Arrhenius

Acid → H⁺ in water
Base → OH⁻ in water
Limited to aqueous solutions

🔵 Brønsted-Lowry

Acid → proton donor
Base → proton acceptor
Any solvent

🟢 Lewis

Acid → electron-pair acceptor
Base → electron-pair donor
Most general

🔄
1.4 Amphiprotic Species
Molecules that can act as both acids and bases
📌 Amphiprotic Species

Molecules or ions that can either donate or accept a proton, depending on the other reactant, are called amphiprotic species.

The Most Important Amphiprotic Species — Water

Water acts as a base with strong acids:

HCl + H₂O → Cl⁻ + H₃O⁺

Water acts as an acid with strong bases:

NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Identify Brønsted-Lowry Acid and Base
Problem: Identify the Brønsted-Lowry acids, bases, and their conjugates in:
NH₃ + H₂PO₄⁻ ⇌ NH₄⁺ + HPO₄²⁻
Find donors/acceptors
Look for who gains/loses a proton (H⁺).
Analyze
H₂PO₄⁻ loses H⁺ → HPO₄²⁻ (H₂PO₄⁻ is the acid, HPO₄²⁻ is its conjugate base)
Analyze
NH₃ gains H⁺ → NH₄⁺ (NH₃ is the base, NH₄⁺ is its conjugate acid)
✅ Acid: H₂PO₄⁻ | Base: NH₃ | Conjugate base: HPO₄²⁻ | Conjugate acid: NH₄⁺
Example 2Same Species, Both Roles
Problem: In the reaction HCl + H₂PO₄⁻ ⇌ Cl⁻ + H₃PO₄, what role does H₂PO₄⁻ play?
Analyze
HCl loses H⁺ → Cl⁻ (HCl is the acid).
Compare
H₂PO₄⁻ gains H⁺ → H₃PO₄. It's the base here.
Compare to Example 1
In the previous problem, H₂PO₄⁻ acted as an acid. So H₂PO₄⁻ is amphiprotic!
✅ H₂PO₄⁻ acts as a base in this reaction (but it can act as an acid elsewhere)
Example 3Identify Lewis Acid and Base
Problem: Identify the Lewis acid and base in: BH₃ + (CH₃)₂S → H₃BS(CH₃)₂
Find electron-deficient
B in BH₃ has only 6 valence electrons → can accept an electron pair.
Find electron-rich
S in (CH₃)₂S has two lone pairs → can donate one.
✅ Lewis acid: BH₃   Lewis base: (CH₃)₂S
Example 4Write the Conjugate Base
Problem: Write the conjugate base of HSO₄⁻.
Rule
Conjugate base = acid with one fewer H⁺ (and one more negative charge).
Remove H⁺
HSO₄⁻ → SO₄²⁻ + H⁺
✅ Conjugate base of HSO₄⁻ is SO₄²⁻
Example 5Rank Conjugate Base Strengths
Problem: Rank the following conjugate bases from strongest to weakest: F⁻, NO₂⁻, CN⁻. Given Ka values: HF = 7.2 × 10⁻⁴, HNO₂ = 4.5 × 10⁻⁴, HCN = 6.2 × 10⁻¹⁰.
Rule
Stronger acid → weaker conjugate base.
Rank acids
HF (7.2×10⁻⁴) > HNO₂ (4.5×10⁻⁴) > HCN (6.2×10⁻¹⁰)
Reverse for bases
So conjugate bases rank opposite: CN⁻ > NO₂⁻ > F⁻
✅ Strongest → weakest: CN⁻ > NO₂⁻ > F⁻

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 1.1
✅ Key Points — Acid-Base Concepts
  • Arrhenius: acid → H⁺ in water; base → OH⁻ in water. Limited to aqueous.
  • Brønsted-Lowry: acid → proton donor; base → proton acceptor. Works in any solvent.
  • Lewis: acid → electron-pair acceptor; base → electron-pair donor. Most general.
  • A conjugate acid-base pair differs by one H⁺. Acid ⇌ Conjugate base + H⁺.
  • Stronger acid → weaker conjugate base.
  • Water is amphiprotic — acts as both acid and base.
  • Water autoionizes: 2H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ (Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C).
  • Amphiprotic species: H₂O, HCO₃⁻, H₂PO₄⁻, HSO₄⁻
