📈 Dissociation Constants (Ka and Kb)

The dissociation constant is a quantitative fingerprint of an acid or base. Learn how Ka and Kb reveal strength at a glance — and how they relate through Kw to give you power over any pH calculation.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Numbers beat adjectives. Saying an acid is "weak" doesn't tell us much. Saying it has Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ tells us everything — we can calculate pH, percent ionization, and predict reaction directions. That's the power of dissociation constants.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Write the Ka expression for any weak acid
  • Write the Kb expression for any weak base
  • Use Ka or Kb to determine acid/base strength
  • Calculate pH or [H₃O⁺] from Ka and initial concentration
  • Use the relationship Ka × Kb = Kw
  • Calculate Ka from pH or pH from Ka
🔢
4.1 The Acid Dissociation Constant Ka
Quantifying weak acid strength

Consider a weak monoprotic acid HA ionizing in water:

Weak Acid Ionization

HA(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + A⁻(aq)

The equilibrium constant for this reaction is:

📌 Ka Expression

Kc = [H₃O⁺][A⁻] / ([HA][H₂O])

Since [H₂O] is essentially constant (≈ 55.5 M), we multiply both sides by [H₂O]:

Ka = [H₃O⁺][A⁻] / [HA]

📝 Reading Ka

• Higher Ka → stronger acid
• Ka = 10⁰ to 10⁻² → moderately strong
• Ka = 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁷ → moderately weak
• Ka < 10⁻⁸ → extremely weak

4.2 The Base Dissociation Constant Kb
Quantifying weak base strength

Weak bases also ionize partially in water. For a general weak base B:

Weak Base Ionization

B(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ HB⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

📌 Kb Expression

Kb = [HB⁺][OH⁻] / [B]

For ammonia specifically:

NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻

Kb = [NH₄⁺][OH⁻] / [NH₃]

📝 Reading Kb

• Higher Kb → stronger base
• Values are typically very small (10⁻⁴ to 10⁻¹⁰)
• Strong bases (Group IA hydroxides) don't have Kb values — they ionize 100%

4.3 The Ka × Kb = Kw Relationship
Connecting conjugate pairs

For a conjugate acid-base pair (like NH₄⁺ and NH₃, or CH₃COOH and CH₃COO⁻), the Ka of the acid and Kb of its conjugate base are related:

📌 Key Relationship

Ka × Kb = Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴

At 25°C. This works for any conjugate acid-base pair.

📝 What it tells us

If you know Ka of an acid, you can find Kb of its conjugate base:
Kb = Kw / Ka

And if you know Kb of a base, you can find Ka of its conjugate acid:
Ka = Kw / Kb

Example — NH₄⁺ / NH₃ Conjugate Pair

Kb(NH₃) = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵
Ka(NH₄⁺) = Kw / Kb = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ / 1.8×10⁻⁵ = 5.6 × 10⁻¹⁰

The 5-Step Method for Weak Acid pH Problems

  1. Write the balanced ionization equation
  2. Set up an ICE table (Initial, Change, Equilibrium)
  3. Write the Ka expression and substitute equilibrium values
  4. Approximate C − x ≈ C (if % ionization ≤ 5%)
  5. Solve for x, then calculate [H₃O⁺], pH, or % ionization

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Find Ka from pH — Butyric Acid
Problem: A 0.250 M aqueous solution of butyric acid (CH₃(CH₂)₂COOH) is found to have pH = 2.72. Determine Ka for butyric acid.
Write equation
CH₃(CH₂)₂COOH + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + CH₃(CH₂)₂COO⁻
Find x
[H₃O⁺] = 10⁻²·⁷² = 1.9 × 10⁻³ M = x
ICE table
Initial: 0.250 M acid; Change: −x; Equilibrium: (0.250 − x) M
Ka expression
Ka = x² / (0.250 − x)
Substitute
Ka = (1.9×10⁻³)² / (0.250 − 1.9×10⁻³) = 1.5 × 10⁻⁵
✅ Ka = 1.5 × 10⁻⁵
Example 2Find pH from Ka — HF
Problem: Calculate the pH of a 0.50 M HF solution at 25°C. Ka(HF) = 6.8 × 10⁻⁴.
Equation
HF + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + F⁻
ICE table
Initial: [HF] = 0.50
Change: −x, +x, +x
Equilibrium: 0.50 − x, x, x
Ka expression
Ka = x² / (0.50 − x) = 6.8 × 10⁻⁴
Approximate
Assume x ≪ 0.50: x² = (0.50)(6.8×10⁻⁴) = 3.4 × 10⁻⁴
Solve
x = 1.8 × 10⁻² M = [H₃O⁺]
Check 5% rule
(0.018 / 0.50) × 100% = 3.6% ≤ 5% ✓ valid
pH
pH = −log(0.018) = 1.74
✅ pH = 1.74
Example 3Find [OH⁻] from Kb — Ammonia
Problem: Find the [OH⁻] of a 0.20 M aqueous solution of ammonia. Kb(NH₃) = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵.
Equation
NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻
ICE table
Initial: [NH₃] = 0.20
Change: −x, +x, +x
Equilibrium: 0.20 − x, x, x
Kb expression
Kb = x² / (0.20 − x) = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵
Approximate
x² = (0.20)(1.8×10⁻⁵) = 3.6 × 10⁻⁶
Solve
x = √(3.6×10⁻⁶) = 1.9 × 10⁻³ M = [OH⁻]
✅ [OH⁻] = 1.9 × 10⁻³ M
Example 4Find Kb from Ka of Conjugate Acid
Problem: The Ka of the ammonium ion (NH₄⁺) is 5.6 × 10⁻¹⁰. Find Kb for ammonia (NH₃).
Relation
Ka × Kb = Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴
Solve
Kb = Kw / Ka = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ / 5.6×10⁻¹⁰
Compute
Kb = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵
✅ Kb(NH₃) = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵
Example 5Percent Ionization from Ka — Acetic Acid
Problem: Calculate the percent ionization of a 0.100 M solution of acetic acid. Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵.
Equation
CH₃COOH ⇌ H₃O⁺ + CH₃COO⁻
ICE table
Initial 0.100; change −x, +x, +x; equilibrium 0.100 − x, x, x
Ka expression
1.8×10⁻⁵ = x² / (0.100 − x)
Approximate
x² = (1.8×10⁻⁵)(0.100) = 1.8×10⁻⁶
Solve
x = 1.342 × 10⁻³ M
% ionization
% = (1.342×10⁻³ / 0.100) × 100% = 1.34%
✅ Percent ionization = 1.34%

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 1.4
✅ Key Points — Dissociation Constants (Ka and Kb)
  • Ka = [H₃O⁺][A⁻] / [HA] — the acid dissociation constant
  • Kb = [HB⁺][OH⁻] / [B] — the base dissociation constant
  • Larger Ka → stronger acid; larger Kb → stronger base
  • Ka × Kb = Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C for any conjugate pair
  • Use ICE tables to organize equilibrium values
  • 5% rule: approximate C − x ≈ C if % ionization ≤ 5%
  • x = √(Ka × C) gives [H₃O⁺] for weak acids
  • Percent ionization = (x / C) × 100%
  • Common Ka: HF 6.8×10⁻⁴, CH₃COOH 1.8×10⁻⁵, HCN 4.9×10⁻¹⁰
  • Common Kb: CH₃NH₂ 4.4×10⁻⁴, NH₃ 1.8×10⁻⁵, C₅H₅N 1.7×10⁻⁹
