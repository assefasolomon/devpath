💧 Ionization of Water and the pH Scale

Water isn't just a solvent — it autoionizes. Learn how the tiny concentration of H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ in water defines the pH scale — the universal measure of acidity and basicity.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Pure water seems like a non-conductor, but precise measurements show it conducts just a tiny bit of electricity. Why? Because water molecules react with each other — some acting as acids, some as bases — producing H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ ions. This is autoionization.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Explain the autoionization of water
  • Define and use Kw (ion-product constant)
  • Classify solutions as acidic, basic, or neutral
  • Calculate pH and pOH from concentrations
  • Find [H₃O⁺] and [OH⁻] from pH and pOH
  • Use the relation pH + pOH = 14
🔄
2.1 Autoionization of Water
Water reacting with itself

Because water is amphiprotic, one water molecule can donate a proton to another:

Autoionization Reaction
One water donates H⁺ (acts as acid) → becomes OH⁻. Another accepts H⁺ (acts as base) → becomes H₃O⁺.
📌 Autoionization (Self-Ionization)

A reaction in which two identical neutral molecules react to produce a cation and an anion. For water:

2 H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

🔢
2.2 The Ion-Product Constant Kw
Quantifying autoionization

Since autoionization is an equilibrium, we can write an equilibrium constant:

📌 Kw Derivation

Kc = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] / [H₂O]²

But [H₂O] is essentially constant (≈ 56 M at 25°C). So we multiply both sides by [H₂O]² and get a new constant:

Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ (at 25°C)

📝 Temperature dependence

Kw is temperature-dependent. At body temperature (37°C), Kw = 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁴. The value 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ is defined at 25°C.

Pure Water at 25°C

In pure water, H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ are produced in equal numbers, so [H₃O⁺] = [OH⁻]. Let x = [H₃O⁺] = [OH⁻]:

x² = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴
x = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M

So in pure water at 25°C: [H₃O⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M

⚖️
2.3 Acidic, Basic, and Neutral Solutions
Using Kw to classify solutions

In any aqueous solution at 25°C, Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ is always true. We classify solutions by comparing [H₃O⁺] and [OH⁻]:

📌 Solution Classification

• Neutral: [H₃O⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M
• Acidic: [H₃O⁺] > [OH⁻], so [H₃O⁺] > 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M
• Basic: [OH⁻] > [H₃O⁺], so [H₃O⁺] < 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M

📝 Key insight

Adding acid to water increases [H₃O⁺] and decreases [OH⁻] (Le Chatelier's principle). Their product always stays 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴.

📊
2.4 The pH Scale
A convenient way to express acidity

Because [H₃O⁺] values are very small, chemists use a logarithmic scale — pH:

📌 pH Definition

pH = −log[H₃O⁺]

Conversely, to find [H₃O⁺] from pH:

[H₃O⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ

📌 pOH Definition

pOH = −log[OH⁻]

[OH⁻] = 10⁻ᵖᴼᴴ

The pH + pOH Relationship

Taking negative log of both sides of Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴:

KEY RELATIONSHIP

pH + pOH = 14

The pH Scale at a Glance

pH Scale
pH < 7 → acidic | pH = 7 → neutral | pH > 7 → basic
Lower pH = higher [H₃O⁺] | Higher pH = lower [H₃O⁺]

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Find [OH⁻] from [H₃O⁺]
Problem: HCl gas is added to pure water at 25°C. The solution has [H₃O⁺] = 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ M. Calculate [OH⁻]. Is the solution acidic, basic, or neutral?
Given
[H₃O⁺] = 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ M, Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴
Use Kw
[OH⁻] = Kw / [H₃O⁺]
Solve
[OH⁻] = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ / 3.0×10⁻⁴ = 3.3 × 10⁻¹¹ M
Classify
[H₃O⁺] (3.0×10⁻⁴) > [OH⁻] (3.3×10⁻¹¹) → acidic
✅ [OH⁻] = 3.3 × 10⁻¹¹ M — solution is acidic
Example 2pH and pOH from Concentration
Problem: Find the pH and pOH of a juice solution with [H₃O⁺] = 5.0 × 10⁻³ M.
pH
pH = −log(5.0×10⁻³) = 3 − log(5.0)
Compute
log(5.0) = 0.7, so pH = 3 − 0.7 = 2.3
pOH
pOH = 14 − pH = 14 − 2.3 = 11.7
✅ pH = 2.3   pOH = 11.7 (acidic solution)
Example 3Concentrations from pH — Human Blood
Problem: The pH of human blood is 7.40. Find [H₃O⁺] and [OH⁻].
[H₃O⁺]
[H₃O⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ = 10⁻⁷·⁴⁰ = 4.0 × 10⁻⁸ M
pOH
pOH = 14 − 7.40 = 6.60
[OH⁻]
[OH⁻] = 10⁻⁶·⁶⁰ = 2.51 × 10⁻⁷ M
Classify
[OH⁻] > [H₃O⁺] → basic (as expected for blood)
✅ [H₃O⁺] = 4.0 × 10⁻⁸ M   [OH⁻] = 2.5 × 10⁻⁷ M
Example 4Antacid Solution
Problem: A solution formed by dissolving an antacid tablet has a pH of 9.18 at 25°C. Calculate [H⁺], [OH⁻], and pOH.
[H⁺]
[H⁺] = 10⁻⁹·¹⁸ = 6.6 × 10⁻¹⁰ M
pOH
pOH = 14 − 9.18 = 4.82
[OH⁻]
[OH⁻] = 10⁻⁴·⁸² = 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ M
✅ [H⁺] = 6.6 × 10⁻¹⁰ M   [OH⁻] = 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ M   pOH = 4.82
Example 5Determine [H₃O⁺] from [OH⁻]
Problem: A solution at 25°C has [OH⁻] = 6.7 × 10⁻² M. Calculate [H₃O⁺]. Is it acidic, basic, or neutral?
Use Kw
[H₃O⁺] = Kw / [OH⁻]
Solve
[H₃O⁺] = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ / 6.7×10⁻² = 1.5 × 10⁻¹³ M
Classify
[H₃O⁺] (1.5×10⁻¹³) < [OH⁻] (6.7×10⁻²) → basic
✅ [H₃O⁺] = 1.5 × 10⁻¹³ M — solution is basic
