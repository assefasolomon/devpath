↔️ The Common Ion Effect

Adding a salt that shares an ion with a weak acid or base suppresses the acid's ionization. This is Le Chatelier's principle in action — and it's the secret behind buffer solutions.

📖 Reading: ~18 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

What happens if you add sodium acetate (CH₃COONa) to a solution of acetic acid? The solution contains two sources of the acetate ion. By Le Chatelier's principle, the equilibrium shifts to reduce the disturbance. This is the common ion effect — and it's the foundation of buffers.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define the common ion effect
  • Explain it in terms of Le Chatelier's principle
  • Predict how adding a common ion shifts equilibrium
  • Calculate pH of a weak acid + its salt
  • Calculate pH of a weak base + its salt
  • Explain solubility effects from common ions
📌
5.1 What is the Common Ion Effect?
Definition and examples
📌 The Common Ion Effect

The shift in an ionic equilibrium caused by the addition of a solute that provides an ion that takes part in the equilibrium.

When a given ion is added to an equilibrium mixture that already contains that ion, the equilibrium shifts to reduce the added ion's concentration.

Example — Acetic Acid + HCl

Consider a solution of acetic acid:

Acetic Acid Ionization Equilibrium

CH₃COOH(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)

Now add HCl, a strong acid that completely ionizes:

HCl Ionization

HCl(aq) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)

The H₃O⁺ from HCl is a common ion with the H₃O⁺ from acetic acid. By Le Chatelier's principle, the acetic acid equilibrium shifts left, reducing its ionization.

📝 Result

The degree of ionization of acetic acid is decreased by the addition of the strong acid HCl. This is the common ion effect in action.

⚙️
5.2 Mechanism — Le Chatelier's Principle
Why the equilibrium shifts

Another common case: adding sodium acetate to acetic acid. Sodium acetate is a strong electrolyte that dissociates completely:

Sodium Acetate Dissociation

CH₃COONa(s) → CH₃COO⁻(aq) + Na⁺(aq)

Now the solution has two sources of CH₃COO⁻:

  • From the weak acid CH₃COOH (partial ionization)
  • From the salt CH₃COONa (complete dissociation)

The large concentration of CH₃COO⁻ drives the acetic acid equilibrium to the left:

Equilibrium Shift
📝 Consequence for pH

Adding CH₃COO⁻ (from a salt) to a solution of CH₃COOH makes the solution less acidic — the pH increases because [H₃O⁺] decreases.

Common Ion Effects Beyond Acids and Bases

The same principle applies to solubility equilibria. Adding a common ion to a saturated solution of a sparingly soluble salt reduces its solubility:

Common Ion Effect on Solubility

NaCl(s) ⇌ Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
Adding more Cl⁻ (from HCl or KCl) shifts equilibrium left → less NaCl dissolves

🔢
5.3 pH Calculations with Common Ions
The simplified method

When a weak acid HA and its salt (containing the common ion A⁻) are both present, the calculation simplifies enormously because:

  • [HA] at equilibrium ≈ [HA] initial (from the acid)
  • [A⁻] at equilibrium ≈ [A⁻] initial (from the salt)
  • Ionization of HA is suppressed — we can ignore x
📌 Simplified Ka Equation

Ka = [H₃O⁺] × [salt] / [acid]

So:

[H₃O⁺] = Ka × [acid] / [salt]

📌 Simplified Kb Equation (for weak base + salt)

[OH⁻] = Kb × [base] / [salt]

📝 Compare with pure acid

For the same concentration of a weak acid:
• Pure weak acid solution: [H₃O⁺] = √(Ka × C)
• With common ion (from salt): [H₃O⁺] = Ka × Cacid / Csalt

The common ion case typically produces much lower [H₃O⁺] — the ionization is suppressed.

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Acetic Acid + HCl
Problem: Determine [H₃O⁺] and [CH₃COO⁻] in a solution that is 0.10 M in both CH₃COOH and HCl. Ka(CH₃COOH) = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵.
Note
HCl is a strong acid — 0.10 M HCl gives [H₃O⁺] = 0.10 M and 0.10 M Cl⁻ (a spectator).
Equilibrium
CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺
Initial: 0.10 M CH₃COOH, 0.10 M H₃O⁺
Change: −x, +x, +x
Equilibrium: 0.10−x, x, 0.10+x
Approximate
Since x is very small, (0.10−x) ≈ 0.10 and (0.10+x) ≈ 0.10
Ka
1.8×10⁻⁵ = (x)(0.10) / 0.10 = x
Solve
x = [CH₃COO⁻] = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ M
Final
[H₃O⁺] = 0.10 + 1.8×10⁻⁵ ≈ 0.10 M
✅ [H₃O⁺] ≈ 0.10 M   [CH₃COO⁻] = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ M
Example 2Acetic Acid + Sodium Acetate — pH
Problem: Find the pH of a solution containing 0.20 M CH₃COOH and 0.30 M CH₃COONa. Ka(CH₃COOH) = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵.
Salt
CH₃COONa fully dissociates → [CH₃COO⁻] from salt = 0.30 M
Approximate
[CH₃COOH] ≈ 0.20 M (from acid)
[CH₃COO⁻] ≈ 0.30 M (from salt)
Formula
[H₃O⁺] = Ka × [acid] / [salt]
Substitute
[H₃O⁺] = (1.8×10⁻⁵)(0.20)/(0.30) = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ M
pH
pH = −log(1.2×10⁻⁵) = 4.92
✅ pH = 4.92
Example 3Compare pH With and Without Salt
Problem: What would the pH of a 0.20 M CH₃COOH solution be if no salt were present?
Formula
[H₃O⁺] = √(Ka × C)
Substitute
[H₃O⁺] = √(1.8×10⁻⁵ × 0.20) = √(3.6×10⁻⁶) = 1.9 × 10⁻³ M
pH
pH = −log(1.9×10⁻³) = 2.72
Compare
Without salt: pH = 2.72
With salt (Ex 2): pH = 4.92
The salt raised the pH by ~2.2 units
✅ Without salt, pH = 2.72 — the common ion dramatically reduced the acidity
Example 4Ammonia + Ammonium Chloride — pH
Problem: A buffer is made by mixing 0.060 M NH₃ with 0.040 M NH₄Cl. Find the pH. Kb(NH₃) = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵.
Equation
NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻
Approximate
[NH₃] ≈ 0.060 M (from base)
[NH₄⁺] ≈ 0.040 M (from salt)
Formula
[OH⁻] = Kb × [base] / [salt]
Substitute
[OH⁻] = (1.8×10⁻⁵)(0.060)/(0.040) = 2.7 × 10⁻⁵ M
pOH and pH
pOH = −log(2.7×10⁻⁵) = 4.57
pH = 14 − 4.57 = 9.43
✅ pH = 9.43
Example 5Common Ion on Solubility
Problem: Predict what happens to the solubility of AgCl when NaCl is added to a saturated AgCl solution.
Equilibrium
AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
Common ion
NaCl adds Cl⁻ to the solution. Cl⁻ is the common ion.
Le Chatelier
Higher [Cl⁻] shifts equilibrium to the left — precipitating more AgCl.
✅ The solubility of AgCl decreases — adding a common ion reduces solubility

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 1.5
✅ Key Points — The Common Ion Effect
  • The common ion effect is the shift in an ionic equilibrium when a solute shares an ion with the equilibrium species
  • Direct consequence of Le Chatelier's principle
  • Adding HCl to CH₃COOH suppresses acetic acid ionization
  • Adding CH₃COONa to CH₃COOH raises the pH (less acidic)
  • [H₃O⁺] = Ka × [acid] / [salt] — simplified equation
  • [OH⁻] = Kb × [base] / [salt] — for weak bases
  • Salts are strong electrolytes — they dissociate 100%
  • Common ion effects also reduce solubility of sparingly soluble salts
  • Adding NaCl to AgCl solution precipitates more AgCl → solubility decreases
  • Sets up the theory for buffer solutions (next lesson!)
