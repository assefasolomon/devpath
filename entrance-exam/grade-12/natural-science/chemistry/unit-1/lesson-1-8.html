🎨 Acid-Base Indicators

Indicators are chemical spies — they change color to reveal the pH of a solution. From litmus paper to phenolphthalein, these weak acids and bases are essential tools in every chemistry lab.

📖 Reading: ~18 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

How do you know if a solution is acidic or basic without a pH meter? Use an indicator — a compound that changes color depending on the pH of the solution. In this lesson, we'll see how they work, examine the common ones, and learn how to choose the right one for a titration.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define what an acid-base indicator is
  • Explain how indicators work as weak acids or bases
  • Know the color ranges of common indicators
  • Choose the right indicator for a titration
  • Interpret indicator color changes in terms of equilibrium
⚙️
8.1 How Indicators Work
Weak acids that change color
📌 Definition

An acid-base indicator is a weak organic acid (HIn) or weak organic base (In⁻) that changes color depending on the pH of the solution. The color of the indicator depends on whether it is in its ionized or non-ionized form.

The Equilibrium Behind Indicator Color

Indicator Equilibrium

HIn + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + In⁻

Acid color    ⇌    Base color

📌 Le Chatelier Applied to Indicators

• In acidic solution ([H₃O⁺] high) → equilibrium shifts left → more HIn → acid color dominates

• In basic solution ([H₃O⁺] low) → equilibrium shifts right → more In⁻ → base color dominates

Acid-Base Indicator — Visual Diagram
📝 Two-color system

Most indicators have two distinct colors — one for the acid form (HIn) and one for the base form (In⁻). The transition range is the narrow pH interval where the color is changing from one to the other.

The pH Color Spectrum

Color Ranges on the pH Scale
Each indicator has a unique pH range where its color changes. Choose the one whose range matches the pH of your titration's equivalence point.

Indicator Types

📌 pH Test Paper

A porous paper is impregnated with an indicator solution and dried. When moistened with the solution being tested, the paper acquires a color determined by the pH.

📌 Universal Indicator

A mixture of several indicators that gives a continuous range of colors across the pH scale. Colors range from red (acidic) to violet (basic), passing through orange, yellow, green, blue.

📝 Why indicators are added in small amounts

Only a few drops of indicator solution are added to the solution being titrated. Since indicators are weak acids or bases themselves, adding too much could alter the pH of the solution. Small amounts don't measurably affect the equilibrium.

🎯
8.3 Choosing the Right Indicator
Matching indicator to titration

During a titration, the equivalence point is the moment when the acid and base have completely neutralized each other. The end point is when the indicator actually changes color. For accurate results, we need the indicator's color change range to overlap with the equivalence-point pH.

📌 The Rule

Choose an indicator whose pH range is centered near the pH of the equivalence point.

Recommended Indicators for Different Titrations

Titration TypeEquivalence pHBest Indicator
Strong acid + Strong base~7Bromothymol blue (or universal)
Weak acid + Strong base> 7 (basic)Phenolphthalein
Strong acid + Weak base< 7 (acidic)Methyl orange or methyl red
Weak acid + Weak baseNear 7 (varies)Difficult — often no good indicator
📝 Practical tip

For weak acid–weak base titrations, the pH change around the equivalence point is too gradual for any indicator to give a sharp color change. Chemists use a pH meter instead.

Worked Decision — Example

Titrating CH₃COOH with NaOH

Equivalence point pH ≈ 8.7 (basic)
Phenolphthalein range: 8.2 – 10.0
Phenolphthalein is the best choice ✓

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Predict Indicator Color
Problem: A solution of pH = 3.0 has methyl orange added. What color will it turn?
Range
Methyl orange changes color between pH 3.2 – 4.4
Compare
pH 3.0 is below the range (more acidic)
Apply
Below the range → indicator is in its acid form (red)
✅ Methyl orange will be red
Example 2Phenolphthalein Color Prediction
Problem: A solution of pH = 9.5 has phenolphthalein added. What color will it turn?
Range
Phenolphthalein changes from pH 8.2 – 10.0 (colorless → pink)
Compare
pH 9.5 is within the range
Apply
At pH 9.5, phenolphthalein is predominantly in its base form (pink)
✅ Phenolphthalein will be pink
Example 3Best Indicator for NaOH/HCl Titration
Problem: Which indicator best fits a strong acid–strong base titration? (Methyl orange, bromothymol blue, phenolphthalein.)
Equivalence pH
For strong acid + strong base, equivalence pH ≈ 7
Check options
• Methyl orange: 3.2 – 4.4 → too acidic
• Bromothymol blue: 6.0 – 7.6 → matches pH 7
• Phenolphthalein: 8.2 – 10.0 → too basic
✅ Best choice: bromothymol blue (or universal indicator)
Example 4Titrating Acetic Acid with NaOH
Problem: During titration of 0.10 M CH₃COOH with 0.10 M NaOH, the equivalence point pH is ~8.7. Which indicator is best?
Equivalence pH
pH ≈ 8.7 (basic, due to acetate hydrolysis)
Match
Look for an indicator whose range covers 8.7
• Phenolphthalein: 8.2 – 10.0 → perfect match
✅ Use phenolphthalein — it changes just above pH 8
Example 5Explain Indicator Color Change
Problem: Using Le Chatelier's principle, explain why phenolphthalein is colorless in acid and pink in base.
Equilibrium
HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻ (HIn is colorless; In⁻ is pink)
In acid
High [H⁺] → equilibrium shifts left → mostly HIn → colorless
In base
OH⁻ consumes H⁺ → equilibrium shifts right → more In⁻ → pink
✅ Le Chatelier's principle explains the color change: HIn is colorless, In⁻ is pink

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 1.8
✅ Key Points — Acid-Base Indicators
  • Acid-base indicators are weak organic acids or bases that change color with pH
  • Indicator equilibrium: HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻
  • In acidic solution → equilibrium shifts left → acid color
  • In basic solution → equilibrium shifts right → base color
  • Each indicator has a specific pH range for color change
  • Methyl orange: red → yellow, pH 3.2–4.4
  • Methyl red: red → yellow, pH 4.8–6.0
  • Bromothymol blue: yellow → blue, pH 6.0–7.6
  • Phenolphthalein: colorless → pink, pH 8.2–10.0
  • Universal indicator: continuous color range across full pH scale
  • Choose indicator whose range includes the equivalence point pH
  • Strong–strong → bromothymol blue | Weak acid–strong base → phenolphthalein | Strong acid–weak base → methyl orange
