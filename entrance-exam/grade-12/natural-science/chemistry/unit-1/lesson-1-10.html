🧪 Acid-Base Titrations

Titration is one of the most precise techniques in analytical chemistry — it lets you determine the exact concentration of an unknown solution by carefully adding a known one until neutralization is complete.

📖 Reading: ~22 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

How do you find the concentration of acid in a sample of vinegar? Or test the purity of a medicine? You use titration — a procedure where you add a solution of known concentration (the titrant) to a solution of unknown concentration (the analyte) until neutralization is complete.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define titration, titrant, and analyte
  • Distinguish equivalence point from end point
  • Describe the procedure and equipment
  • Perform titration calculations
  • Choose the right indicator for the titration
  • Understand sources of error in titrations
📌
10.1 What is Titration?
Definition and key terms
📌 Titration

Titration is a procedure for determining the amount of acid (or base) in a solution by measuring the volume of base (or acid) of known concentration that completely reacts with it.

Key Terms

📌 Titrant

The solution of known concentration that is added from a burette. In acid-base titration, this is the acid or base whose concentration you know precisely.

📌 Analyte (Titrand)

The solution of unknown concentration that is placed in the flask. This is the sample you want to analyze.

📌 Equivalence Point

The point at which the amount of titrant added is exactly enough to completely neutralize the analyte. At this point:
moles of acid = moles of base (accounting for stoichiometry)

📌 End Point

The point where the indicator changes color. Ideally, the end point should match the equivalence point exactly. In practice, they are very close.

📝 Equivalence vs End Point

Equivalence point = theoretical (the actual stoichiometric neutralization)
End point = observed (when indicator changes color)

A good indicator ensures these two are as close as possible.

🔬
10.2 Equipment and Procedure
How a titration is performed

Equipment

Titration Setup
The burette holds the titrant; the flask holds the analyte with a few drops of indicator.

Step-by-Step Procedure

  1. Clean and rinse: Rinse the burette with distilled water, then with a small amount of titrant. Rinse the pipette with the analyte solution.
  2. Fill the burette: Pour titrant into the burette using a funnel. Remove the funnel before starting. Make sure there are no air bubbles in the tip.
  3. Record initial reading: Read the meniscus at eye level. Record to the nearest 0.1 mL.
  4. Prepare the analyte: Pipette a known volume of analyte into a clean Erlenmeyer flask. Add 2–3 drops of indicator.
  5. Titrate: Slowly add titrant from the burette while swirling the flask. Initially add quickly, then dropwise as you approach the end point.
  6. Detect the end point: Stop when the indicator shows a permanent color change (lasts ~30 seconds with swirling).
  7. Record final reading: Read the burette at eye level. Calculate the volume of titrant used.
  8. Repeat: Perform at least 2–3 trials for accuracy. Average the concordant values.
📝 Safety notes

• Never pipette by mouth — always use a suction bulb
• Wear goggles and gloves
• Add acid to water (not water to acid) if diluting
• Read the meniscus at eye level to avoid parallax error

10.3 Titration Calculations
Finding the unknown concentration

There are two common approaches to titration calculations:

Method 1 — Using Molarity and Stoichiometry

📌 General Formula

Macid × Vacid / nacid = Mbase × Vbase / nbase

Where n is the number of H⁺ (acid) or OH⁻ (base) per molecule.

At the equivalence point:

Moles Equality

moles of H⁺ = moles of OH⁻

Method 2 — Using Normality (Simpler)

📌 N₁V₁ = N₂V₂

N₁ V₁ = N₂ V₂

When both sides use normality, no stoichiometric factor is needed.

Reaction Ratios and Their Effects

AcidBaseStoichiometric Ratio (H⁺:OH⁻)
HCl (monoprotic)NaOH (monobasic)1 : 1
H₂SO₄ (diprotic)NaOH (monobasic)1 : 2
HCl (monoprotic)Ba(OH)₂ (dibasic)2 : 1
H₃PO₄ (triprotic)NaOH (monobasic)1 : 3
📝 Why this matters

If you use moles without adjusting for stoichiometry, you'll get the wrong answer. Using normality automatically handles this — that's why it's often preferred for titration calculations.

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Simple 1:1 Titration
Problem: 25.0 mL of HCl solution is titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. If 20.0 mL of NaOH is required, find the molarity of the HCl solution.
Reaction
HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O   (1:1 ratio)
Moles NaOH
n(NaOH) = 0.100 × 0.0200 = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol
Moles HCl
1:1 ratio → n(HCl) = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol
Molarity HCl
M = 2.00×10⁻³ / 0.0250 = 0.0800 M
✅ [HCl] = 0.0800 M
Example 22:1 Ratio — H₂SO₄ with NaOH
Problem: 20.0 mL of H₂SO₄ is neutralized by 30.0 mL of 0.150 M NaOH. Find the molarity of the sulfuric acid.
Reaction
H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O   (1:2 ratio)
Moles NaOH
n(NaOH) = 0.150 × 0.0300 = 4.50 × 10⁻³ mol
Moles H₂SO₄
2 NaOH per H₂SO₄ → n(H₂SO₄) = 4.50×10⁻³ / 2 = 2.25 × 10⁻³ mol
Molarity
M = 2.25×10⁻³ / 0.0200 = 0.1125 M
✅ [H₂SO₄] = 0.1125 M
Example 3Using Normality
Problem: What volume of 0.50 N NaOH is needed to neutralize 25.0 mL of 0.80 N HCl?
Formula
N₁ V₁ = N₂ V₂
Identify
Acid: N₁ = 0.80 N, V₁ = 25.0 mL
Base: N₂ = 0.50 N, V₂ = ?
Solve
V₂ = (0.80 × 25.0) / 0.50 = 40.0 mL
✅ Volume of NaOH = 40.0 mL
Example 4Find Concentration of Vinegar
Problem: A 10.0 mL sample of vinegar (acetic acid) is titrated with 0.500 M NaOH. The end point is reached at 16.5 mL of NaOH. What is the molarity of the acetic acid?
Reaction
CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O   (1:1 ratio)
Moles NaOH
n = 0.500 × 0.0165 = 8.25 × 10⁻³ mol
Moles CH₃COOH
1:1 → n = 8.25 × 10⁻³ mol
Molarity
M = 8.25×10⁻³ / 0.0100 = 0.825 M
✅ Acetic acid concentration = 0.825 M
Example 5Titration of a Base with an Acid
Problem: 25.0 mL of Ca(OH)₂ solution is titrated with 0.200 M HCl. The end point is at 22.5 mL of HCl. Find the molarity of the Ca(OH)₂.
Reaction
Ca(OH)₂ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + 2H₂O   (1:2 ratio)
Moles HCl
n(HCl) = 0.200 × 0.0225 = 4.50 × 10⁻³ mol
Moles Ca(OH)₂
2 HCl per Ca(OH)₂ → n = 4.50×10⁻³ / 2 = 2.25 × 10⁻³ mol
Molarity
M = 2.25×10⁻³ / 0.0250 = 0.0900 M
✅ [Ca(OH)₂] = 0.0900 M

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 1.10
✅ Key Points — Acid-Base Titrations
  • Titration determines unknown concentration by adding a known solution until neutralization
  • Titrant = known solution in the burette; analyte = unknown in the flask
  • Equivalence point: moles of H⁺ = moles of OH⁻
  • End point: when indicator changes color
  • Choose an indicator whose range matches the equivalence point pH
  • N₁V₁ = N₂V₂ for normality-based calculations
  • M₁V₁/n₁ = M₂V₂/n₂ for molarity-based calculations
  • Account for stoichiometry: H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → 1:2 ratio
  • Perform multiple trials to improve accuracy
  • Sources of error: parallax, air bubbles, overshooting the end point, indicator choice
