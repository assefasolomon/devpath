📉 Titration Curves

A titration curve plots pH against the volume of titrant added. It reveals the story of the reaction — where the equivalence point is, how sharp the pH change is, and which indicator to use.

📖 Reading: ~22 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

During a titration, the pH doesn't change at a constant rate. It starts slowly, changes drastically near the equivalence point, then slows down. Plotting pH vs. titrant volume gives a titration curve — a visual map of the reaction.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define a titration curve
  • Identify the four main types of titration curves
  • Locate the equivalence point on a curve
  • Explain why the pH changes sharply near the equivalence point
  • Choose the right indicator for each curve type
  • Interpret buffer regions on a curve
📌
11.1 What is a Titration Curve?
The pH story of a titration
📌 Definition

A titration curve is a plot of the pH of the solution being titrated (y-axis) against the volume of titrant added (x-axis). It provides insight into the titration process.

Anatomy of a Titration Curve

Generic Titration Curve (Acid → Base)
Titration curves have three phases: slow start (often buffered), sharp change at equivalence, and asymptotic approach to the excess-titrant pH.
📌 Key Features

• Initial pH: the pH of the analyte before titration begins
• Buffer region: pH changes slowly (only for weak acid/base)
• Equivalence point: the steepest part of the curve
• Excess titrant: final flat region approaching the titrant's own pH

📝 Why the pH changes sharply

Near the equivalence point, the amounts of acid and base are nearly equal. A tiny addition of titrant shifts the balance dramatically — the solution goes from slightly acidic to slightly basic almost instantly, causing the steep pH jump.

11.2 The Four Types of Titration Curves
Strong/weak combinations
📌 Type 1 — Strong Acid + Strong Base

Equivalence pH = 7.0
Very sharp vertical jump around pH 4 → 10
Initial pH very low (strong acid), final pH very high (strong base)

SA + SB Curve — Sharp Jump at pH 7
📌 Type 2 — Weak Acid + Strong Base

Equivalence pH > 7 (basic, because of conjugate base hydrolysis)
Has a buffer region before equivalence
Smaller vertical jump than SA + SB

WA + SB Curve — Equivalence in Basic Region
📌 Type 3 — Strong Acid + Weak Base

Equivalence pH < 7 (acidic, because of conjugate acid hydrolysis)
Has a buffer region
Smaller vertical jump on the acidic side

SA + WB Curve — Equivalence in Acidic Region
📌 Type 4 — Weak Acid + Weak Base

Equivalence pH depends on Ka vs Kb
No sharp vertical jump — the pH change is very gradual
No suitable indicator — must use a pH meter

WA + WB Curve — No Sharp Jump
📝 Summary table

SA + SB → equivalence pH = 7, sharp jump
WA + SB → equivalence pH > 7, buffer region before
SA + WB → equivalence pH < 7, buffer region before
WA + WB → equivalence pH varies, no sharp jump

11.3 Matching Indicator to Curve
Using the curve to pick your indicator
📌 The Rule

The indicator's pH range must fall within the sharp vertical portion of the titration curve (near the equivalence point). Then the color change happens at the right time.

Titration TypeEquivalence pHBest Indicator
Strong acid + Strong base7.0Bromothymol blue (pH 6.0 – 7.6)
Weak acid + Strong base~8.7 (basic)Phenolphthalein (pH 8.2 – 10.0)
Strong acid + Weak base~5.3 (acidic)Methyl orange (pH 3.2 – 4.4) or methyl red (pH 4.8 – 6.0)
Weak acid + Weak baseVaries, no sharp jumpNone — use pH meter
🔴 Strong Acid Titrations

Sharp jump spans pH 4 → 10
Many indicators work
Methyl orange, methyl red, or bromothymol blue

🔵 Weak Acid Titrations

Sharp jump is narrower and higher
Need indicator that changes in basic range
Use phenolphthalein

📝 Methyl orange vs phenolphthalein

Methyl orange (red → yellow, pH 3.2–4.4) — best for titrations where the equivalence point is acidic.
Phenolphthalein (colorless → pink, pH 8.2–10.0) — best for titrations where the equivalence point is basic.

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Identify Curve Type
Problem: A titration curve starts at pH 2.9, has a sharp jump from pH 6 to pH 10, and ends at pH 11.5. What type of titration is it?
Initial pH
Starts at pH 2.9 — strongly acidic. This could be a strong acid (pH ~1) or weak acid (pH ~3) as analyte.
Jump range
Sharp jump from 6 to 10 — centers around pH 8.
Equivalence pH
Equivalence at pH 8 → basic → indicates a weak acid + strong base titration
✅ Weak acid titrated with strong base (e.g., CH₃COOH + NaOH)
Example 2Choose Indicator for SA + SB
Problem: Which indicator would work best for a strong acid + strong base titration? Options: (a) methyl orange (3.2–4.4), (b) bromothymol blue (6.0–7.6), (c) phenolphthalein (8.2–10.0).
Equivalence pH
For SA + SB, equivalence point = pH 7
Match
• Methyl orange: 3.2 – 4.4 — too acidic
• Bromothymol blue: 6.0 – 7.6 — matches pH 7 ✓
• Phenolphthalein: 8.2 – 10.0 — too basic
✅ Use bromothymol blue
Example 3Estimate pKa from a Curve
Problem: A weak acid titration curve shows a buffer region at pH 4.7 at half-neutralization (halfway to equivalence). What is the Ka of the acid?
Key relation
At half-equivalence, [HA] = [A⁻], so pH = pKa
Given
pH at half-eq = 4.7 → pKa = 4.7
Find Ka
Ka = 10⁻ᵖᴷᵃ = 10⁻⁴·⁷ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵
✅ Ka ≈ 2.0 × 10⁻⁵
Example 4Why WA + WB Has No Sharp Jump
Problem: Explain why a weak acid + weak base titration has no sharp vertical jump in its titration curve.
Reason 1
Both weak acid and weak base establish equilibria in solution.
Reason 2
Near equivalence, both the conjugate acid and conjugate base buffer the solution — pH changes very gradually.
Result
No sharp color change for any indicator → must use a pH meter to detect equivalence.
✅ Mutual buffering by both conjugate species prevents a sharp pH jump
Example 5Read the Curve
Problem: A titration curve of 25.0 mL of HCl with 0.10 M NaOH shows the equivalence point at 20.0 mL of NaOH and pH = 7.0. What is the concentration of the HCl?
Reaction
HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O (1:1)
Moles NaOH
n = 0.10 × 0.0200 = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol
Moles HCl
1:1 → n(HCl) = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol
Molarity
M = 2.00×10⁻³ / 0.0250 = 0.0800 M
✅ [HCl] = 0.0800 M

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 1.11
✅ Key Points — Titration Curves
  • A titration curve plots pH vs. volume of titrant added
  • Three phases: initial (slow change) → equivalence (sharp jump) → excess (flattens)
  • SA + SB: sharp jump at pH 7; any appropriate indicator works
  • WA + SB: equivalence pH > 7; use phenolphthalein
  • SA + WB: equivalence pH < 7; use methyl orange or methyl red
  • WA + WB: no sharp jump; use a pH meter instead
  • At half-equivalence: pH = pKa (for weak acid titrations)
  • The buffer region appears at the half-equivalence point
  • The indicator's pH range must overlap the sharp vertical portion of the curve
  • Titration curves are powerful tools for choosing indicators and analyzing unknowns
