📊 Classification of Polymers

There are many ways to classify polymers: by monomer type (homo vs co), by origin (natural vs synthetic), by reaction type (addition vs condensation), and by thermal behavior (thermoplastic vs thermoset). This final Unit 4 lesson brings them all together.

Introduction
What you will learn

Polymers can be classified in several different ways, depending on which property you focus on. In this lesson we cover four classification systems plus a detailed treatment of the most important synthetic and natural polymers.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Distinguish homopolymers from copolymers
  • Classify polymers as natural or synthetic
  • Classify polymers as addition or condensation types
  • Distinguish thermoplastics from thermosets
  • Recognize common synthetic polymers and their properties
  • Describe the structure and role of natural polymers
🔬
Classification 1 — Homo vs Copolymers
By number of monomer types
🟢 Homopolymers

Made from only one type of monomer.

Example: Polyethylene is synthesized by the polymerization of one type of monomer — ethene (ethylene).

n CH₂=CH₂ → –(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ–

🟡 Copolymers

Prepared by polymerizing more than one kind of monomer unit.

Example: Ethene (H₂C=CH₂) and propene (H₂C=CH–CH₃) can be copolymerized to produce a polymer with two kinds of repeating units.

Types of Copolymers

📌 Random copolymer

Repeating units arranged in a random fashion: A-B-B-A-A-A-B-A-A

📌 Regular (alternating) copolymer

Monomers arranged in a regular alternating sequence: A-B-A-B-A-B-A-B-A

Classification 2 — Natural vs Synthetic
By origin
📌 Natural polymers (biopolymers)

Occur in nature and can be extracted. Common examples:

• Carbohydrates — monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose), disaccharides (sucrose, lactose, maltose)
• Polysaccharides — starch, cellulose, gums
• Proteins — enzymes, structural proteins
• Fibers — wool, silk, cotton
• Polyisoprenes — natural rubber
• Nucleic acids — RNA, DNA

📌 Synthetic polymers (plastics)

Human-made polymers. One well-known example is polyethylene — it has no taste or odour, and is lightweight, nontoxic, and relatively cheap.

📝 Key difference

Natural polymers are produced by living organisms through biochemical processes.
Synthetic polymers are produced industrially from petrochemicals or other chemical feedstocks.

⚗️
Classification 3 — Addition vs Condensation
By reaction type
🔴 Addition Polymers

Formed by the addition reaction of an unsaturated monomer — no by-product formed.

n CH₂=CHCl → –(CH₂–CHCl)ₙ–
(Vinyl chloride → PVC)

Examples: Polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC, polystyrene, Teflon, natural rubber

🔵 Condensation Polymers

Formed from monomers with two functional groups, with the loss of a small molecule (usually water).

HO–R–OH + HOOC–R'–COOH → polyester + H₂O

Examples: Nylons, polyesters (Dacron), Bakelite, carbohydrates, proteins

🌡️
Classification 4 — Thermoplastics vs Thermosets
By response to heat
🔴 Thermoplastic Polymers

• Melt when heated and resolidify when cooled
• Comprised of essentially linear or lightly branched polymer molecules
• Can be remelted or reprocessed (recycled)

Examples: Polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC, Teflon, PMMA, nylon, polyester (Dacron), polystyrene, polyamide

🔵 Thermoset Polymers

• Do NOT melt when heated; decompose irreversibly at high temperatures
• Substantially cross-linked — extensive 3-D network of covalent bonds
• Exhibit resistance to heat, corrosion, and mechanical stress
• Non-recyclable

Examples: Bakelite, cyanate esters, epoxy resin, fiberglass, polyester resin, polyurethane, silicone resin, vinyl esters, vulcanized rubber

📝 Why thermosets can't be recycled

Thermosets are held together by a covalent 3-D network. When heated, the covalent bonds don't break cleanly for reshaping — they just decompose. Because the network is permanent, thermosets can't be melted and reshaped like thermoplastics can.

Common Synthetic Polymers — Detailed
Structure, properties, and uses
🛍️
Polyethylene
–(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ–
No taste or odour; lightweight, nontoxic, relatively cheap. Used in squeeze bottles, plastic wrapping, garment bags, trash bags, and electrical insulation.
🪣
Polypropylene
–(CH₂–CH(CH₃))ₙ–
Stronger than polyethylene. Used for food containers that can be washed in a dishwasher; ropes; fishing nets; carpets; bottles.
🚰
PVC (Polyvinyl chloride)
–(CH₂–CHCl)ₙ–
Third most widely produced plastic, after polyethylene and polypropylene. Used for pipes, leather-like materials, shoes, raincoats, wallpaper, floor tile, and phonograph records.
✈️
PMMA (Perspex / Plexiglass)
–(CH₂–C(CH₃)(COOCH₃))ₙ–
Polymethyl methacrylate — sold as Lucite or Plexiglass. A lightweight glass-like polymer used as a glass substitute (airplane windows, streetlights).
🍳
PTFE (Teflon)
–(CF₂–CF₂)ₙ–
Polytetrafluoroethylene. Good resistance to chemical attack; works from −73 °C to 260 °C; low coefficient of friction (waxy/slippery). Used for non-stick coatings and electrical insulation.
📝 Teflon's unique property

Teflon's very low coefficient of friction makes it feel waxy or slippery — that's why bread dough doesn't stick to a Teflon-coated surface. It's also why Teflon is used on non-stick cookware.

2. Macromolecules

📌 Examples of biological macromolecules

• Polysaccharides — starches (300–600 glucose units), cellulose, gums
• Proteins — enzymes, structural proteins (skin, hair, muscles)
• Fibers — wool, silk, cotton
• Polyisoprenes — natural rubber
• Nucleic acids — RNA, DNA (genetic material)

Natural Polymers — Key Examples
📝 Protein structure

Proteins are condensation polymers of amino acids. Each amino acid has an amine group (–NH₂) and a carboxylic acid group (–COOH). The peptide bond (–CO–NH–) links them into long chains.

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1Homo vs Copolymer
Problem: Classify each polymer as homopolymer or copolymer: (a) polyethylene, (b) Nylon 66, (c) PVC, (d) SBR (styrene-butadiene rubber).
(a) Polyethylene
Homopolymer — only ethylene monomers
(b) Nylon 66
Copolymer — two different monomers (diamine + diacid)
(c) PVC
Homopolymer — only vinyl chloride monomers
(d) SBR
Copolymer — styrene + butadiene
✅ Homo: polyethylene, PVC | Co: Nylon 66, SBR
Example 2Thermoplastic or Thermoset?
Problem: Classify each as thermoplastic or thermoset: (a) polyethylene, (b) Bakelite, (c) PVC, (d) vulcanized rubber.
(a) Polyethylene
Thermoplastic — linear chains, melts on heating, recyclable
(b) Bakelite
Thermoset — cross-linked network, decomposes on heating
(c) PVC
Thermoplastic — melts and can be reshaped
(d) Vulcanized rubber
Thermoset — sulfur cross-links make it rigid
✅ Thermoplastic: polyethylene, PVC | Thermoset: Bakelite, vulcanized rubber
Example 3Natural or Synthetic?
Problem: Classify each polymer as natural or synthetic: cellulose, nylon, DNA, Teflon, silk, PVC, natural rubber, polystyrene.
Natural
Cellulose, DNA, silk, natural rubber — from plants or animals
Synthetic
Nylon, Teflon, PVC, polystyrene — made industrially
✅ Natural: cellulose, DNA, silk, natural rubber | Synthetic: nylon, Teflon, PVC, polystyrene
Example 4Why Teflon is Non-Stick
Problem: Explain why Teflon (PTFE) is used for non-stick cookware, and list two other uses.
Structure
Teflon has a very low coefficient of friction — it feels waxy or slippery to touch
Why non-stick works
Because of the low friction, food (like bread dough) doesn't stick to a Teflon-coated surface
Other properties
Also resists chemical attack and works from −73 °C to 260 °C with no effect on properties
Other uses
Electrical insulation, and coatings for laboratory equipment
✅ Teflon's low friction makes it non-stick; it's also used for electrical insulation and chemical-resistant coatings
Example 5Protein Peptide Bonds
Problem: Proteins are natural condensation polymers. Explain what monomer they use and what bond links the monomers.
Monomer
Amino acids — each has an amine (–NH₂) and a carboxyl (–COOH) group
Bond formation
The amine of one amino acid reacts with the carboxyl of another, releasing water (H₂O)
Bond type
Forms a peptide bond (–CO–NH–) — an amide linkage
Polymer type
Condensation polymerization — because water is released
✅ Monomer: amino acids. Bond: peptide (–CO–NH–). Type: condensation polymerization

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 4.3
✅ Key Points — Classification of Polymers
  • Polymers can be classified in four main ways: by monomer type, by origin, by reaction type, and by thermal behavior
  • Homopolymers — one monomer type (e.g., polyethylene, PVC)
  • Copolymers — two or more monomer types; can be random or regular (alternating)
  • Natural polymers (biopolymers) — carbohydrates, polysaccharides, proteins, fibers, natural rubber, DNA/RNA
  • Synthetic polymers (plastics) — human-made; polyethylene is the classic example
  • Addition polymers — from unsaturated monomers; no by-product (e.g., PE, PP, PVC, PS, Teflon)
  • Condensation polymers — polyfunctional monomers with small-molecule by-product (e.g., nylon, Dacron, Bakelite, carbohydrates, proteins)
  • Thermoplastics — melt when heated, can be remelted and recycled (linear/branched); e.g., PE, PP, PVC, Teflon, PMMA, nylon
  • Thermosets — do NOT melt, decompose irreversibly; are cross-linked 3-D networks; non-recyclable; e.g., Bakelite, epoxy, vulcanized rubber
  • Common synthetic polymers and uses: PE (bags, bottles), PP (food containers, ropes), PVC (pipes, raincoats), PMMA (airplane windows), PTFE/Teflon (non-stick, insulation)
  • Common natural polymers: starch (300–600 glucose units), cellulose (glucose, β-1,4), proteins (amino acids, peptide bonds), natural rubber (isoprene), DNA/RNA (nucleotides)
  • Proteins are natural condensation polymers of amino acids joined by peptide (–CO–NH–) bonds
  • Reference: MoE Chemistry Grade 12 Student Textbook, §4.3 (pp. 224–239)
