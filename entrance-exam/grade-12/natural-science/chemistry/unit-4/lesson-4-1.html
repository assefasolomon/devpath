🧬 Introduction to Polymers

The chair you're sitting on, the clothes you're wearing, the DNA in your cells — all are polymers. This lesson introduces the building blocks (monomers), the giant molecules they form (polymers), and how to describe their size and shape.

📖 Reading: ~18 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Look around the room you're in. The plastic in your pen, the fibers in your clothes, the wood of your desk, the proteins in your body — all of these are polymers. The word comes from Greek: poly (many) + meros (parts). Understanding polymers is understanding the materials that build the modern world.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define polymer and monomer
  • Identify polymers in everyday materials
  • Explain degree of polymerization (DP)
  • Distinguish polymers from oligomers
  • Describe linear, branched, and network polymer structures
  • Give examples of natural and synthetic polymers
📌
What is a Polymer?
The definition
📌 Polymer (MoE Textbook, p. 214)

A polymer is a large chainlike molecule built up from many repeating smaller molecules. The word comes from the Greek poly (many) and meros (part) — literally "many parts."

📌 Key numbers

• A polymer generally comprises more than 100 monomers
• Structures with lower numbers of monomers are called oligomers
• The repeat unit in a polymer usually corresponds to the monomer from which the polymer was made (few exceptions exist)

📝 Activity 4.1 (Textbook p. 214)
With your friends at your bench, look at the following materials. Then set criteria and categorize them:

• The textile fibre of your clothes
• Your hair and skin
• The wood from which your desk is made
• The glass of the window
📝 Which of these are polymers?

• Textile fiber (cotton, wool, polyester) → polymer ✓
• Hair and skin (keratin protein) → polymer ✓
• Wood (cellulose + lignin) → polymer ✓
• Glass (SiO₂ network) → not a polymer in the organic sense (though it is a network solid)

Monomers
The building blocks
📌 Monomer (MoE Textbook, p. 215)

The small molecules used as the basic building blocks for polymers are known as monomers. Mono means one.

Monomers → Polymer

📝 The pattern

In most polymers, the repeat unit corresponds exactly to the monomer. For example, polyethylene is just (–CH₂–CH₂–)ₙ, where each –CH₂–CH₂– comes from one ethylene molecule.

📏
Degree of Polymerization (DP)
How big is the polymer?
📌 Definition (MoE Textbook, p. 214)

The size of a polymer molecule may be defined either by its mass or by the number of repeat units in the molecule. This latter indicator of size is called the degree of polymerization, DP.

📌 Relationship

Relative molar mass of polymer = (Relative molar mass of repeat unit) × DP

M(polymer) = M(repeat unit) × n    (where n = DP)

📝 Worked example

A polyethylene molecule has a molar mass of 140,000 g/mol. What is its degree of polymerization?

Repeat unit: –CH₂–CH₂– → M = 2(12) + 4(1) = 28 g/mol
DP = 140,000 / 28 = 5,000

🌐
Polymer Structures
Linear, branched, or network

Polymer molecules may be linear, slightly branched, or highly interconnected. In the branched case, the structure can develop into a large three-dimensional network.

Linear

Straight chains. Example: HDPE (high-density polyethylene)

Branched

Chains with side branches. Example: LDPE (low-density polyethylene)

Network (Cross-linked)

Three-dimensional network. Example: Bakelite, vulcanized rubber

📝 Why structure matters

The structure determines properties:
• Linear → flexible, can be melted and reshaped
• Branched → lower density, weaker
• Network → rigid, cannot be melted (decomposes instead)

Natural Polymers
Made by nature

Natural polymers occur in nature and can be extracted. They are often referred to as biopolymers.

🌳
Cellulose
(C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ
Monomer = glucose. Found in wood, cotton, and plant fibers.
🧬
Proteins
–(NH–CHR–CO)ₙ–
Monomer = amino acids. Found in skin, hair, muscles, enzymes.
🧪
Starch
(C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ
Monomer = glucose. Found in potatoes, corn, rice.
🪵
Natural Rubber
(C₅H₈)ₙ
Monomer = isoprene. Produced by rubber trees.
🧬
DNA & RNA
Nucleotide chains
Monomer = nucleotides. Carry the genetic code.
🕸️
Silk & Wool
Protein chains
Monomer = amino acids. Natural fibers from animals.
🏭
Synthetic Polymers
Made by humans

Synthetic polymers are human-made polymers. They are sometimes referred to as plastics. One well-known example is polyethylene — it has no taste or odour, and is lightweight, nontoxic, and relatively cheap.

🛍️
Polyethylene
–(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ–
From ethylene. Used in squeeze bottles, plastic wrapping, garment bags, trash bags, electrical insulation.
🪣
Polypropylene
–(CH₂–CH(CH₃))ₙ–
From propylene. Stronger than polyethylene. Used in food containers, ropes, fishing nets, carpets, bottles.
🚰
PVC
–(CH₂–CHCl)ₙ–
Polyvinyl chloride. From vinyl chloride. Used for pipes, leather-like materials, shoes, raincoats, wallpaper, floor tiles.
🍳
Teflon (PTFE)
–(CF₂–CF₂)ₙ–
Polytetrafluoroethylene. From tetrafluoroethylene. Used for non-stick coatings, electrical insulation.
✈️
Plexiglass (PMMA)
–(CH₂–C(CH₃)(COOCH₃))ₙ–
Polymethyl methacrylate. Lightweight glass substitute — airplane windows, streetlights.
☕
Polystyrene
–(CH₂–CH(C₆H₅))ₙ–
From styrene. Used in disposable cups, packaging, insulation foam.

Worked Examples
5 practice problems
Example 1Identify the Monomer
Problem: Identify the monomer for each of the following polymers: (a) polyethylene, (b) PVC, (c) Teflon, (d) natural rubber.
(a) Polyethylene
Monomer = ethylene (CH₂=CH₂)
(b) PVC
Monomer = vinyl chloride (CH₂=CHCl)
(c) Teflon
Monomer = tetrafluoroethylene (CF₂=CF₂)
(d) Natural rubber
Monomer = isoprene (C₅H₈)
✅ Ethylene, vinyl chloride, tetrafluoroethylene, isoprene
Example 2Calculate DP
Problem: A PVC sample has a molar mass of 125,000 g/mol. What is its degree of polymerization? (Repeat unit = CH₂–CHCl; M = 62.5 g/mol.)
Formula
DP = M(polymer) / M(unit)
Substitute
DP = 125,000 / 62.5
Compute
DP = 2000
✅ DP = 2,000 repeat units
Example 3Natural vs Synthetic
Problem: Classify each of the following as a natural or synthetic polymer: cellulose, nylon, DNA, Teflon, silk, PVC.
Natural
Cellulose (plant), DNA (biological), silk (animal)
Synthetic
Nylon (polyamide), Teflon (PTFE), PVC (polyvinyl chloride)
✅ Natural: cellulose, DNA, silk | Synthetic: nylon, Teflon, PVC
Example 4Why Glass isn't a Polymer
Problem: Explain why glass — despite being a network of SiO₂ units — is not usually classified as a polymer in the organic chemistry sense.
Definition review
Polymers are large chainlike molecules built from many repeating monomer units
Glass structure
Glass is a non-crystalline (amorphous) network solid with SiO₂ units
Key difference
Glass is not built from discrete monomer molecules that connect — it's a continuous network without distinguishable repeating molecular units that "came from a monomer"
✅ Glass is a network solid, not a polymer in the organic sense — no discrete monomer
Example 5Match Structure to Property
Problem: Match each property to the correct polymer structure: (a) can be melted and reshaped, (b) rigid and cannot be melted, (c) lower density and weaker than linear form.
(a) Meltable
Linear structure — chains can slide past each other when heated
(b) Rigid
Network (cross-linked) structure — covalent bonds lock the chains together
(c) Lower density
Branched structure — side branches prevent tight packing
✅ (a) Linear → meltable; (b) Network → rigid; (c) Branched → lower density

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 4.1
✅ Key Points — Introduction to Polymers
  • Polymer = large chainlike molecule built from many repeating smaller molecules (Greek: poly = many, meros = part)
  • Monomer = small molecule used as the basic building block (Greek: mono = one)
  • A polymer generally contains more than 100 monomers; shorter chains are called oligomers
  • Degree of polymerization (DP) = number of repeat units in the polymer molecule
  • Relationship: M(polymer) = M(repeat unit) × DP
  • Polymers can be linear, branched, or network (cross-linked)
  • Structure determines properties: linear = flexible/meltable; branched = lower density; network = rigid/non-meltable
  • Natural polymers (biopolymers): cellulose, proteins, starch, natural rubber, DNA, RNA, silk, wool
  • Common monomers: glucose (→ cellulose, starch), ethylene (→ polyethylene), amino acids (→ proteins), isoprene (→ natural rubber), vinyl chloride (→ PVC)
  • Synthetic polymers (plastics): polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC, Teflon (PTFE), Plexiglass (PMMA), polystyrene
  • Polyethylene: no taste/odor, lightweight, nontoxic, relatively cheap
  • Reference: MoE Chemistry Grade 12 Student Textbook, §4.1 (pp. 213–215)
