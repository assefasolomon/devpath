⚗️ Polymerization Reactions

How do small molecules become giant polymers? There are two main routes: addition polymerization (chain-growth) and condensation polymerization (step-growth). This lesson covers both, plus the industrial polymers nylon and Dacron.

📖 Reading: ~28 min

Introduction
What you will learn

In Lesson 4.1 you learned what polymers and monomers are. Now we answer: how do monomers actually join together? The chemical change by which monomer units combine to form a polymer is called a polymerization reaction. There are two main types — and each produces very different materials.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define polymerization reaction
  • Describe addition polymerization — its three steps
  • Describe condensation polymerization and its by-products
  • Write the synthesis of Nylon 66 and Dacron (PET)
  • Distinguish addition from condensation polymers
  • Recognize the uses of polyamides and polyesters
📌
What is Polymerization?
The basic definition
📌 Polymerization (MoE Textbook, p. 216)

The chemical change by which monomer units combine to form a polymer is known as a polymerization reaction.

📌 Requirement for monomers

The essential feature of a monomer is that it must have two or more functional groups (polyfunctionality). This gives each monomer the capacity to form chemical bonds to at least two other monomer molecules.

• Bifunctional monomers → form linear, chainlike polymers
• Higher-functionality monomers → yield cross-linked, network polymeric products

📝 Two main types of polymerization

1. Addition polymerization (chain-growth) — unsaturated monomers join without losing any atoms
2. Condensation polymerization (step-growth) — monomers join with the loss of a small molecule (like water or methanol)

🔗
Addition Polymerization
Chain-growth polymerization
📌 Definition (MoE Textbook, p. 216)

Addition polymerization is a reaction in which a chain reaction adds new monomer units to the growing polymer molecule one at a time. Also known as chain-growth polymerization.

📌 Which monomers undergo addition?

Addition polymerization occurs with unsaturated monomers — monomers that contain a double or triple bond (usually C=C).

Classic Example — Ethylene → Polyethylene

Addition polymerization of ethylene
n CH₂=CH₂ —[catalyst]→ –(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ–
Ethylene → Polyethylene
The double bond of ethylene opens up and joins with other ethylene units. No atoms are lost.

Other Addition Polymers

📝 Note on double bonds

During addition polymerization, the double bond is converted to a single bond. When monomers with triple bonds are used, the triple bond is converted to a double bond.

📝
Three Steps of Addition Polymerization
MoE Textbook, p. 218

Addition polymerization takes place in three distinct steps:

  1. Chain initiation — The polymerization process is started by means of a reactive initiator molecule. The initiator can be a free radical, a cation, or an anion. These reactive centers react readily with other monomers without eliminating the active center.
  2. Chain propagation — A monomer adds to the growing chain, with the free radical, cation, or anion transferring to the chain end. High molar mass polymers are formed right from the beginning of the reaction.
  3. Chain termination — The polymerization is stopped — chain reactions do not continue indefinitely. The radical, cation, or anion is "neutralized," stopping chain propagation.

Three Types of Addition Polymerization (by Initiator)

TypeInitiator
Free radical polymerizationFree radical — most important industrial route
Cationic polymerizationCation (positive ion)
Anionic polymerizationAnion (negative ion)
📝 The free-radical route

The free-radical route to chain polymerization is the most important step in industrial polymerization. It's how polyethylene, PVC, polystyrene, and many other common plastics are made.

Free Radical Polymerization — Detailed Steps

📌 Step 1 — Chain initiation

The free radical on the initiator fragment (Ra·) attacks the covalent π bond in the monomer, leaving a free radical on the monomer:

Ra· + CH₂=CH₂ → Ra–CH₂–CH₂·

📌 Step 2 — Chain propagation

The radical on the end of the growing chain attacks the next monomer, regenerating the radical at the new chain end. This repeats thousands of times:

Ra–CH₂–CH₂· + n CH₂=CH₂ → Ra–(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ–CH₂–CH₂·

📌 Step 3 — Chain termination

Two growing chain radicals combine, destroying both radicals and stopping growth:

Chain₁· + Chain₂· → Chain₁–Chain₂ (terminated)

📝 Polymers made by addition polymerization

Polyethylene, polyvinyl chloride (PVC), acrylics, polystyrene, and polyoxymethylene (acetal).

💧
Condensation Polymerization
Step-growth polymerization
📌 Definition (MoE Textbook, p. 220)

Condensation polymerization involves the combination of monomer molecules with the release of simple molecules like water or methanol as a by-product. These products are normally referred to as condensate. Also known as step-growth polymerization.

📌 Requirement for monomers

To produce a condensation polymer, the monomers involved must have two or more functional groups. Typical reactions:

• Dibasic acids + diamines → polyamides (e.g., nylon)
• Dibasic acids + diols → polyesters (e.g., Dacron)

🔴 Addition Polymerization

• Monomers are unsaturated (contain C=C)
• No by-product produced
• All atoms of monomer end up in polymer
• Chain-growth mechanism
• Examples: polyethylene, PVC, Teflon, polystyrene

🔵 Condensation Polymerization

• Monomers have two functional groups
• Produces a small molecule by-product (H₂O, methanol)
• Not all atoms of monomers end up in polymer
• Step-growth mechanism
• Examples: nylon, Dacron, proteins, starch, cellulose

📝 Natural condensation polymers

Proteins, starch, and cellulose are all examples of natural condensation polymers. Synthetic condensation polymers include polyester (Dacron/PET) and polyamide (Nylon 66).

🧵
Nylon 66 — A Polyamide
The classic condensation polymer
📌 What is Nylon 66?

Nylon 66 is one of the most common polyamides. It is a copolymer — prepared by the condensation of 1,6-diaminohexane (hexamethylene diamine) and 1,6-hexanedioic acid (adipic acid).

📌 Why "66"?

The numbers indicate there are 6 carbon atoms in each of the two monomers — hexamethylene diamine and adipic acid.

The Reaction

The basic amine reacts with the acid to form a salt. Heating removes water and forms the amide bonds:

Nylon 66 Synthesis
n H₂N–(CH₂)₆–NH₂ + n HOOC–(CH₂)₄–COOH → –[HN–(CH₂)₆–NH–CO–(CH₂)₄–CO]ₙ– + (2n−1) H₂O
📌 The amide linkage

The reaction forms amide bonds (–CO–NH–) between the amine nitrogen and the carbonyl carbon. These strong hydrogen-bonding amide groups give nylon its strength.

Uses of Nylon

📌 Common uses

• Parachutes
• Ropes
• Clothes and stockings
• Hair combs
• Rugs
• Reinforcing automobile tires

👕
Dacron (PET) — A Polyester
The other big condensation polymer
📌 What is Dacron?

Dacron is a copolymer — a polyester formed when equimolar amounts of 1,4-benzenedicarboxylic acid (terephthalic acid) and 1,2-ethanediol (ethylene glycol) react.

Also known as Polyethylene Terephthalate or PET.

The Reaction

Dacron (PET) Synthesis
n HOOC–C₆H₄–COOH + n HO–CH₂CH₂–OH → –[CO–C₆H₄–CO–O–CH₂CH₂–O]ₙ– + (2n−1) H₂O
📌 The ester linkage

The reaction forms ester bonds (–CO–O–) between the acid carbonyl and the alcohol oxygen.

Properties and Uses of Dacron

📌 Properties

• Strong and impact resistant
• Colourless with high transparency

📌 Uses

• Synthetic fibers — the main use
• Mixed with cotton to give durable, easily dyed, crease-resistant fabrics
• Bottles and packaging materials

📝 The general polyester structure

The repeating unit of a polyester is:
–[–CO–R'–CO–O–R–O–]ₙ–

Where R and R' are the carbon backbones from the diol and diacid respectively.

🧪
Experiment 4.1 — Synthesis of Nylon
MoE Textbook, p. 222
🧪 Synthesis of Nylon
Objective: To prepare nylon and study its properties.

Apparatus & Chemicals: 250 mL beakers, dropping funnel, glass rod, forceps or copper wire hooks, 0.5 M basic solution of hexamethylenediamine, 0.5 M NaOH solution, adipoyl chloride, 0.5 M HNO₃.

Caution: Wear gloves while doing this experiment.

Procedure:
  • Place 5 mL of Solution A in a small beaker. (Solution A = 0.5 M basic solution of hexamethylenediamine: 6 g hexamethylene diamine + 100 mL of 0.5 M NaOH)
  • Place 5 mL of Solution B in a second beaker. (Solution B = 5 g adipoyl chloride dissolved in 100 mL cyclohexane)
  • Slowly add Solution B to Solution A down the side of the slanted beaker. Do not stir the mixture.
  • Nylon will form at the interface of the two solutions.
  • Draw the nylon thread out of the interface using forceps or a copper wire hook and slowly wind it up.
  • Wash the nylon with water, dry superficially with a towel, then let it dry.
  • Test its strength (stretch it), its melting behavior (heat a piece), and its reactivity with acids (add a drop of nitric acid).
Key Questions:
  • Why are the two solutions not able to mix?
  • Why is adipoyl chloride used instead of adipic acid?
  • What did you feel of the strength when you stretched the prepared nylon?
  • What happened to the nylon when it was heated?
  • Was nylon resistant to acids?
📝 Why adipoyl chloride (not adipic acid)?

In the lab, the reaction rate matters. Using adipoyl chloride (instead of adipic acid) makes the reaction happen quickly at the interface — so nylon forms immediately as a visible film. In industry, adipic acid is used because it's cheaper and safer (no toxic HCl by-product), but the reaction needs heat and longer time.

Example 1Identify the Polymerization Type
Problem: Classify each of the following polymerizations as addition or condensation: (a) ethylene → polyethylene, (b) hexamethylene diamine + adipic acid → nylon, (c) vinyl chloride → PVC, (d) terephthalic acid + ethylene glycol → Dacron.
(a) Ethylene → PE
Addition — unsaturated monomer, no by-product
(b) Diamine + diacid
Condensation — two functional groups, H₂O released
(c) Vinyl chloride → PVC
Addition — unsaturated monomer (C=C)
(d) Diacid + diol
Condensation — two functional groups, H₂O released
✅ (a) Addition | (b) Condensation | (c) Addition | (d) Condensation
Example 2The Three Steps Explained
Problem: Briefly describe the three steps of addition polymerization.
1. Initiation
A reactive initiator (free radical, cation, or anion) attacks the monomer's double bond, creating a reactive center at the end of the growing chain
2. Propagation
Monomers add one at a time to the growing chain; the reactive center transfers to the chain end. High molar mass forms quickly
3. Termination
The reactive center is neutralized — the chain reaction stops and the polymer chain is complete
✅ Initiation → Propagation → Termination

Example 5Addition vs Condensation
Problem: Compare addition and condensation polymerization in terms of (a) monomer type, (b) by-product, and (c) example.
(a) Monomer
Addition: unsaturated (C=C or C≡C)
Condensation: two or more functional groups (–COOH, –OH, –NH₂)
(b) By-product
Addition: none
Condensation: small molecule (H₂O, methanol)
(c) Example
Addition: polyethylene, PVC, Teflon
Condensation: nylon, Dacron, proteins, cellulose
✅ Addition: unsaturated monomers, no by-product. Condensation: polyfunctional monomers, small-molecule by-product

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 4.2
✅ Key Points — Polymerization Reactions
  • Polymerization = chemical change by which monomers combine to form a polymer
  • Monomers must have two or more functional groups (polyfunctionality) to form polymers
  • Bifunctional monomers → linear polymers; higher functionality → cross-linked networks
  • Addition polymerization (chain-growth): unsaturated monomers; no by-product; double bond → single bond
  • Addition polymerization has 3 steps: initiation → propagation → termination
  • Three types by initiator: free-radical (most important), cationic, anionic
  • Addition polymers: polyethylene, PVC, Teflon (PTFE), polystyrene, Plexiglass (PMMA)
  • Condensation polymerization (step-growth): monomers with 2+ functional groups; small molecule by-product (H₂O, methanol)
  • Typical reactions: diamine + diacid → polyamide; diol + diacid → polyester
  • Nylon 66 = hexamethylene diamine + adipic acid (both 6-carbon); forms amide bonds
  • Dacron (PET) = terephthalic acid + ethylene glycol; forms ester bonds
  • Uses of nylon: parachutes, ropes, clothes, combs, rugs, tire reinforcement
  • Uses of Dacron: synthetic fibers, blended fabrics, bottles, packaging
  • Natural condensation polymers: proteins, starch, cellulose
  • Reference: MoE Chemistry Grade 12 Student Textbook, §4.2 (pp. 215–223)
