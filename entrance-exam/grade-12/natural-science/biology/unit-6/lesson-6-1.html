🌡️ Climate Change: Causes & Greenhouse Gases

Climate change is one of the greatest challenges facing life on Earth. In this lesson, you'll learn what climate change is, how it differs from weather, what drives it, and the role that greenhouse gases — especially carbon dioxide — play in warming our planet.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Climate change is the global phenomenon of climate transformation characterized by changes in the usual climate of the planet — temperature, precipitation, and wind — especially caused by human activities. It's a systematic change in the long-term state of the atmosphere over multiple decades or longer.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define climate change and distinguish it from weather
  • Explain the difference between climate variability and climate change
  • Describe the Earth's energy balance and how it drives climate
  • Identify the main greenhouse gases and their sources
  • Explain why carbon dioxide (CO₂) is the most important greenhouse gas
  • Describe the natural greenhouse effect and how human activity has intensified it
📌
What is Climate Change?
MoE Textbook §6.1, p. 305
📌 Climate Change (MoE Textbook, p. 305)

Climate change is a long-term change in the Earth's climate, especially a change due to an increase in the average atmospheric temperature. It is the global phenomenon of climate transformation characterized by changes in the usual climate of the planet (temperature, precipitation, and wind) that are especially caused by human activities.

Key Points About Climate Change

  • It is a long-term change — measured over decades or longer
  • It affects the whole planet — a global phenomenon
  • It changes three main things: temperature, precipitation (rainfall), and wind patterns
  • It is primarily caused by human activities (anthropogenic) — burning fossil fuels, deforestation, and livestock farming
  • It is different from global warming — global warming is only one aspect (the temperature rise) of climate change
📝 Climate Change vs Global Warming

• Global warming — the increase in Earth's average temperature (one specific aspect)
• Climate change — the broader set of changes including temperature, rainfall, wind, sea level, and extreme weather

🌤️
Weather vs Climate
Understanding the key distinction

These terms are often confused. Knowing the difference is essential to understanding climate change.

TermMeaningTimeframe
WeatherThe day-to-day state of the atmosphere at a particular place and timeHours to days
ClimateThe average weather pattern of a place over a long period30 years or more
Climate variabilityShort-term changes in climate patterns that occur naturally (e.g., El Niño years)Years to decades
Climate changeA long-term, sustained change in the climate system, mainly driven by human activityDecades and beyond
📝 Remember

"Climate is what you expect, weather is what you get." A single cold winter day doesn't disprove climate change — just as a single hot summer day doesn't prove it. Climate change is about the long-term trend.

⚡
Causes & the Earth's Energy Balance
How human activity drives climate change

At its most basic, climate change is caused by a change in the Earth's energy balance — how much of the energy from the Sun that enters the Earth (and its atmosphere) is released back into space. Natural and anthropogenic (human-made) substances and processes that alter this energy budget are called drivers of climate change.

Main Human Activities Driving Climate Change

  • Burning fossil fuels — coal, oil, and gas for electricity, transport, and industry
  • Deforestation — cutting down forests removes the trees that absorb CO₂
  • Farming livestock — cattle and other animals produce methane (CH₄)
  • Industrial processes — cement production, manufacturing, and refrigeration
  • Fertilizer use — releases nitrous oxide (N₂O)
📝 The Bottom Line

These human activities increase the concentration of atmospheric CO₂ by more than 40% compared to pre-industrial times, with over half the increase occurring since 1970. This adds enormous amounts of greenhouse gases to the atmosphere, strengthening the greenhouse effect and causing global warming.

🏭
Greenhouse Gases
MoE Textbook §6.1.1, p. 307
📌 Greenhouse Gases (GHGs)

Greenhouse gases are gases released into the atmosphere that trap heat by preventing a certain amount of heat radiation from escaping back to space, making the Earth warmer. They act like a blanket or car windshield that traps the Sun's energy and heat instead of letting it reflect back into space.

The Natural Greenhouse Effect

Without the natural greenhouse effect, the Earth's average annual temperature would be below freezing instead of close to 60°F. This natural effect is essential for life. However, increases in greenhouse gases have tipped the Earth's energy budget out of balance by trapping additional heat and raising Earth's average temperature.

Main Greenhouse Gases & Their Sources

Carbon dioxide (CO₂)Fossil fuel combustion, deforestation76%
Methane (CH₄)Biomass combustion, agricultural wastes, livestock13%
Sulfur dioxide (SO₂)Combustion of coal, oil, and diesel7%
Nitrous oxide (N₂O)Fertilizer use3%
Fluorinated gases (CFCs, HCFs)Refrigeration1%
📝 Climate Active Pollutants

Greenhouse gases are sometimes called "climate active pollutants" because most of them have additional notable effects on human health — not just on the climate.

💨
Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)
The most important greenhouse gas

Carbon dioxide (CO₂) is the greenhouse gas responsible for the greatest amount of warming to date. The majority of CO₂ is released from the incomplete combustion of fossil fuels — coal, oil, and gas used for electricity production, transportation, and industrial processes.

Why CO₂ is So Important

  • CO₂ absorbs and radiates heat warmed by sunlight and thermal infrared energy (heat)
  • Unlike oxygen or nitrogen, GHGs absorb that heat and release it gradually over time
  • CO₂ absorbs less heat per molecule than methane or nitrous oxide — but it's more abundant and stays in the atmosphere much longer
  • Increases in atmospheric CO₂ are responsible for about two-thirds of the total energy imbalance causing Earth's temperature to rise

The Natural CO₂ Cycle

In nature, CO₂ is exchanged continually between the atmosphere, plants, and animals through photosynthesis, respiration, and decomposition, and between the atmosphere and the ocean through gas exchange. A very small amount of CO₂ (roughly 1%) is emitted from volcanic eruptions. This is balanced by an equivalent amount removed by chemical weathering of rocks.

📝 The Disruption

The additional CO₂ emitted from fossil fuel burning and deforestation has disturbed the balance of the carbon cycle because the natural processes that could restore the balance are too slow compared to the rates at which human activities are adding CO₂ to the atmosphere.

Current CO₂ Levels

  • In 2019, CO₂ levels were more than 40% higher than in the 19th century
  • Most of this increase has taken place since 1970 when global energy consumption accelerated
  • Current concentrations are substantially higher than they have been in at least 800,000 years (based on ice core evidence)
  • Once emitted, some CO₂ will remain in the atmosphere for decades or centuries — and some for thousands of years

Other Greenhouse Gases
Methane, N₂O, SO₂, and fluorinated gases
🐄
Methane (CH₄)

Sources: biomass combustion, agricultural wastes, livestock. Traps more heat per molecule than CO₂ but has a shorter atmospheric lifetime.

🌱
Nitrous Oxide (N₂O)

Source: fertilizer use. A potent greenhouse gas from agricultural activity.

🔥
Sulfur Dioxide (SO₂)

Source: combustion of coal, oil, and diesel. Also a major cause of acid rain.

❄️
Fluorinated Gases

CFCs and HCFs from refrigeration. Small share of emissions but very high global warming potential.

📝 Greenhouse Gas Properties

Each greenhouse gas has a different ability to trap heat and a different half-life in the atmosphere. This is why some gases (like methane) have a stronger short-term effect, while others (like CO₂) have a longer-lasting effect.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 6.1
What you should remember
  • Climate change = long-term change in Earth's climate — temperature, precipitation, wind — mainly driven by human activity
  • Weather is short-term (hours to days); climate is long-term (30+ years)
  • Climate change is caused by a shift in the Earth's energy balance — we're trapping more heat than we release
  • Main human activities: burning fossil fuels, deforestation, farming livestock
  • The greenhouse effect is natural and essential — but human activity is intensifying it
  • Main greenhouse gases: CO₂ (76%), CH₄ (13%), SO₂ (7%), N₂O (3%), fluorinated gases (1%)
  • CO₂ is responsible for ~two-thirds of the energy imbalance causing warming
  • CO₂ levels are more than 40% higher than in the 19th century — highest in at least 800,000 years
  • Some CO₂ remains in the atmosphere for decades, centuries, or even thousands of years
