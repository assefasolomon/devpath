🍞 Chemical Structure of Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are the most abundant organic molecules on Earth — and the main source of energy for most living things. From the sugar in your tea to the starch in teff, from the cellulose in plant cell walls to the glycogen in your muscles, carbohydrates are everywhere. This lesson explores their structure and classification.

Introduction
What you will learn

Carbohydrates are the most abundant biological molecules in the world. They're made of just three elements — carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen — but they form an astonishing variety of structures. They fuel our cells, build plant cell walls, and store energy in our liver and muscles.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define carbohydrates and explain their general formula
  • Distinguish between monosaccharides, oligosaccharides, and polysaccharides
  • Compare aldoses and ketoses
  • Identify the common disaccharides — sucrose, lactose, maltose
  • Describe the structure and function of the main polysaccharides
  • Distinguish between α-1,4 and β-1,4 glycosidic bonds

What are Carbohydrates?
MoE Textbook §3.1, p. 90
📌 Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are organic compounds made of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen, usually with hydrogen and oxygen in the ratio 2:1 (as in water). They are generally classified into three groups: monosaccharides, oligosaccharides, and polysaccharides.

📝 The general formula

Simple sugars (monosaccharides) have the formula [C(H₂O)]ₙ, where n = 3–7. For example, glucose (n = 6) has the formula C₆H₁₂O₆.

Why Carbohydrates Matter

  • They are the main source of energy for most living things
  • They are essential to mankind as food and fuel
  • They provide structural support (cellulose in plants, chitin in insects and fungi)
  • They store energy (starch in plants, glycogen in animals)
🍬
Monosaccharides
The simplest sugars
📌 Monosaccharides

Monosaccharides are carbohydrate molecules that cannot be broken down into simpler carbohydrate molecules by hydrolysis. They are the simplest (smallest) carbohydrates and are also called "simple sugars."

The Two Families of Monosaccharides

All carbohydrates have a carbonyl functional group (C=O) and multiple hydroxyl groups (C–OH). Based on the location of the carbonyl group, monosaccharides are classified as aldoses or ketoses.

FamilyCarbonyl positionExamples
Aldoses (aldehyde sugars)At the end of the carbon chainGlucose, Ribose, Galactose, Glyceraldehyde
Ketoses (ketone sugars)At any other position (usually carbon 2)Fructose, Ribulose, Dihydroxyacetone
📝 Glucose — the most common monosaccharide

Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) is the most common monosaccharide in living things. It's an aldose and is the primary fuel for cellular respiration. Every cell in your body uses glucose for energy.

🔬
Aldoses vs Ketoses
Two families of simple sugars

Aldoses (Aldehyde Sugars)

  • Have the carbonyl group (C=O) at the end of the molecule
  • Carbon atoms are numbered starting at the aldehyde functional group
  • Common examples: glyceraldehyde (triose), ribose (pentose), glucose and galactose (hexoses)

Ketoses (Ketone Sugars)

  • Have the carbonyl group not at the end of the molecule
  • Common examples: dihydroxyacetone (triose), ribulose (pentose), fructose (hexose)
  • Fructose is one of the most common monosaccharide ketone sugars

Disaccharides
Two sugars joined together
📌 Disaccharides

Disaccharides (two sugars) consist of two monosaccharide units joined by a glycosidic linkage — a central oxygen covalently bonded to two carbons, one in each ring. They have 12 carbon atoms with the general formula C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁.

The Three Common Disaccharides

DisaccharideMade ofFound in
Maltose (malt sugar)Two α-glucose unitsGerminating seeds, malted barley
Lactose (milk sugar)Glucose + GalactoseMilk and dairy products
Sucrose (table sugar)Glucose + FructoseSugarcane, sugar beet, plants

Formation & Breakdown

  • Dehydration synthesis (condensation reaction): Two monosaccharides join with the release of a water molecule (H₂O) to form a disaccharide
  • Hydrolysis ("breaking with water"): A disaccharide + water react to form two monosaccharides
  • Example: Sucrose + H₂O → Glucose + Fructose
📝 Reducing sugars

A reducing sugar is a sugar that can donate electrons to another compound (i.e., reduce it). All monosaccharides are reducing sugars; most disaccharides are reducing sugars except sucrose. This is why sucrose doesn't react with Benedict's solution — the test for reducing sugars.

🧱
Polysaccharides
Complex carbohydrates
📌 Polysaccharides

Polysaccharides ("many sugars") are long polymers of monosaccharides linked by dehydration synthesis. They may be linear or branched polymers and may contain hundreds or even thousands of monosaccharide units. They are also called complex carbohydrates.

The Four Key Polysaccharides

PolysaccharideMonomerFunctionFound in
StarchGlucose (α-1,4)Energy storage in plantsSeeds, bulbs, tubers (potato, teff)
GlycogenGlucose (α-1,4 + α-1,6 branches)Energy storage in animalsLiver and muscles
CelluloseGlucose (β-1,4)Structural supportPlant cell walls (wood)
ChitinModified glucose (β-1,4)Structural supportExoskeletons of insects, fungal cell walls

Starch — The Plant Energy Store

  • Made of two components: amylose (10–30%) — linear, and amylopectin (70–90%) — branched
  • Found especially in seeds, bulbs, and tubers
  • Stored in plants for later use as energy

Glycogen — The Animal Energy Store

  • A highly branched polymer of glucose
  • Most glucose residues are linked by α-1,4 glycosidic bonds
  • Branches (about every tenth residue) are created by α-1,6 glycosidic bonds
  • Readily mobilized — broken down to release glucose when energy is needed
  • Stored in the liver and muscles

Cellulose & Chitin — The Structural Polysaccharides

  • Cellulose — forms wood and parts of plant cell walls; long chains of glucose connected by β-acetal linkages; one of the most common organic compounds in nature
  • Chitin — found in the exoskeletons of insects, the cell walls of fungi, and certain hard structures in invertebrates and fish; made from chains of modified glucose; the second most common organic compound in nature
  • Pectin — a polysaccharide found in the cell walls of fruits and vegetables
🔗
Alpha vs Beta Glycosidic Bonds
Why starch and cellulose are so different

The D-glucose that can be used by cells is biologically active. It can exist in two forms — α-D-glucose and β-D-glucose — which differ only in the direction that the –H and –OH groups point on carbon 1.

The Key Difference

  • When α-glucose molecules are joined, they form starch and glycogen
  • When β-glucose molecules are joined, they form cellulose and chitin
  • 1,4 glycosidic bonds form between carbon-1 of one monosaccharide and carbon-4 of the other
  • α-1,4 bond: The OH on carbon-1 is below the glucose ring
  • β-1,4 bond: The OH on carbon-1 is above the plane
📝 Why this matters

This tiny difference in bond orientation has enormous consequences:

• Starch and glycogen (α-bonds) — easy for animals to digest, provide quick energy
• Cellulose and chitin (β-bonds) — very difficult for animals to digest, provide structural strength
• Humans can digest starch but cannot digest cellulose — because we don't produce the enzyme (cellulase) needed to break β-1,4 bonds. That's why fibre (cellulose) passes through our digestive system undigested.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 3.2
What you should remember
  • Carbohydrates contain C, H, and O; simple sugars have the formula [C(H₂O)]ₙ where n = 3–7
  • Three main groups: monosaccharides, oligosaccharides, polysaccharides
  • Monosaccharides — simplest sugars; classified by carbon number and by whether they are aldoses or ketoses
  • Disaccharides — two monosaccharides joined by a glycosidic linkage; common ones are maltose, lactose, and sucrose
  • Polysaccharides — long polymers of monosaccharides; the four key ones are starch, glycogen, cellulose, chitin
  • α-1,4 bonds (α-glucose) form starch and glycogen; β-1,4 bonds (β-glucose) form cellulose and chitin
  • Humans can digest starch but not cellulose because we lack the enzyme to break β-1,4 bonds
