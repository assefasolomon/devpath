🔥 Cellular Respiration

Cellular respiration is the process that extracts energy from food and converts it into a form all living things can use: ATP. Every cell in your body is doing it right now. This lesson explores how glucose is broken down in a series of stages to release energy.

📖 Reading: ~28 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

In photosynthesis, plants create glucose, which stores energy in its chemical bonds. Both plants and consumers (like animals) then undergo a series of metabolic pathways — collectively called cellular respiration — to extract that energy. Cellular respiration converts the energy in glucose into a form all living things can use: ATP.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe the structure of ATP and its role in cellular metabolism
  • Write the overall equation for aerobic respiration
  • Describe each stage of aerobic respiration: glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, Krebs cycle, electron transport
  • Explain how ATP is produced in a cell
  • Calculate the ATP yield from one glucose molecule
  • Explain how respirometers work
⚡
ATP — The Energy Currency
How cells store and transfer energy
📌 ATP — Adenosine Triphosphate

ATP is the principal molecule for storing and transferring energy in cells. It is often referred to as the "energy currency of the cell." ATP can be used to store energy for future reactions or be withdrawn to pay for reactions when energy is required.

Structure of ATP

ATP is a nucleotide consisting of three parts:

  • Adenine — a nitrogenous base
  • Ribose — a five-carbon sugar
  • Three phosphate groups — linked by two high-energy bonds called phosphoanhydride bonds

How ATP Releases Energy

  • When one phosphate group is removed by breaking a phosphoanhydride bond (hydrolysis), energy is released
  • ATP is converted to ADP (adenosine diphosphate)
  • Removing another phosphate converts ADP to AMP (adenosine monophosphate)
  • AMP can be recycled into ADP or ATP by forming new phosphoanhydride bonds to store energy again
📝 The bank account analogy

ATP is like a bank account for energy. You can "deposit" energy by making ATP from ADP + P, and "withdraw" energy by breaking ATP back down to ADP + P. In the cell, AMP, ADP, and ATP are constantly interconverted.

📐
Overall Equation
The summary of cellular respiration

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ~36 ATP

Cellular respiration involves three main stages:

  • Glycolysis (stage 1) — occurs in the cytosol
  • Krebs cycle / Citric acid cycle (stage 2) — occurs in the mitochondrial matrix
  • Electron transport (stage 3) — occurs in the inner mitochondrial membrane
📝 Where does it happen?

Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol and does not require oxygen. The Krebs cycle and electron transport occur in the mitochondria and require oxygen. In prokaryotes, all stages happen in the cytoplasm.

1️⃣
Stage 1 — Glycolysis
Splitting glucose

Glycolysis ("sugar splitting") occurs in the cytosol of the cell. It breaks glucose into two molecules of a three-carbon compound called pyruvate.

Key Steps

  1. Phosphorylation of glucose by ATP — glucose 6-phosphate
  2. Rearrangement — fructose 6-phosphate
  3. Second ATP phosphorylation
  4. The six-carbon molecule is split into two three-carbon molecules — G3P (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate)
  5. Oxidation followed by phosphorylation — produces 2 NADH and two molecules of BPG
  6. Removal of high-energy phosphate by 2 ADP — produces 2 ATP and leaves 2 molecules of 3PG
  7. Removal of water yields 2 molecules of PEP
  8. Removal of high-energy phosphate by 2 ADP — produces 2 ATP and 2 pyruvate molecules

Net Reaction of Glycolysis

Glucose + 2 ADP + 2 NAD⁺ + 2 Pᵢ → 2 Pyruvate + 2 ATP + 2 NADH + 2 H⁺ + 2 H₂O

📝 Net gain of glycolysis

Glycolysis "invests" 2 ATP to make glucose reactive, then produces 4 ATP — a net gain of 2 ATP. It also produces 2 NADH (which will be used later to make more ATP).

2️⃣
Stage 2 — Pyruvate Oxidation
The link reaction

Before pyruvate can enter the next pathway, it must undergo a series of changes to become acetyl CoA. This three-step process occurs in the mitochondrial matrix.

Three Steps

Step 1: A carboxyl group is removed from pyruvate, releasing a molecule of CO₂.
Step 2: The hydroxyethyl group is oxidized to an acetyl group, and the electrons are picked up by NAD⁺, forming NADH.
Step 3: The enzyme-bound acetyl group is transferred to CoA, producing a molecule of acetyl CoA.

Overall Reaction

2 Pyruvate + 2 NAD⁺ + 2 CoA → 2 acetyl-CoA + 2 NADH + 2 H⁺ + 2 CO₂

  • Acetyl-CoA enters the Krebs cycle
  • NADH goes to the electron transport chain to help produce ATP
  • Carbon dioxide diffuses out of the cell as a waste product
  • The protons (2H⁺) stay in the matrix
3️⃣
Stage 3 — Krebs Cycle
The citric acid cycle

The Krebs cycle (also called the citric acid cycle or tricarboxylic acid cycle) begins when acetyl-CoA combines with a four-carbon molecule called OAA (oxaloacetate).

Key Steps

  • Acetyl-CoA (2C) + OAA (4C) → citric acid (6C)
  • Citric acid goes through a series of reactions that release energy
  • Energy is captured in NADH, ATP, and FADH₂
  • Carbon dioxide is released as a waste product
  • The final step regenerates OAA, allowing the cycle to continue
  • Two turns are needed because glycolysis produces two pyruvate molecules per glucose

Net Reaction (per turn)

3 NAD⁺ + FAD + GDP + Pᵢ + acetyl-CoA + 2H₂O → 3 NADH + FADH₂ + GTP + 2CO₂ + 4H⁺

📝 Remember the name

The Krebs cycle is named after Hans Krebs, the German-British scientist who discovered it. It's also called the "citric acid cycle" because citric acid is the first product formed.

4️⃣
Stage 4 — Electron Transport Chain
Oxidative phosphorylation
📌 Oxidative Phosphorylation

Oxidative phosphorylation is the process in which ATP is formed as a result of the transfer of electrons from NADH or FADH₂ to O₂ by a series of electron carriers. It takes place in the mitochondria and is the major source of ATP in aerobic organisms.

Three Major Steps

  • (a) Oxidation-reduction reactions — electron transfers between specialized proteins embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane
  • (b) Proton gradient generation — protons (H⁺) are pumped across the inner mitochondrial membrane
  • (c) ATP synthesis — ATP synthase uses the energy from the proton gradient to make ATP

The Three Sites of ATP Production

SiteLocation
Site IBetween FMN and Coenzyme Q at complex I
Site IIBetween cyt b and cyt C1 at complex III
Site IIIBetween cyt a and cyt a3 at complex IV

Chemiosmosis

  • The transfer of electrons through the ETC causes protons to be pumped from the matrix to the intermembrane space
  • This creates an electrochemical potential difference across the membrane
  • The electrical potential is due to the accumulation of positively charged H⁺ outside the membrane
  • The chemical potential is due to the difference in pH (more acidic outside)
  • This potential drives ATP synthase to generate ATP from ADP and phosphate
📝 The terminal electron acceptor

The final molecule at the end of the electron transport chain is O₂ (oxygen). It accepts electrons and combines with H⁺ to form water (H₂O). Without oxygen, the whole chain backs up — which is why we need to breathe.

📊
ATP Yield
How much energy do we get?

From one glucose molecule, the ATP yield breaks down as follows:

StageATP / NADH / FADH₂ producedConverted to ATP
Glycolysis2 ATP + 2 NADH2 + 2×3 = 8 ATP
Pyruvate oxidation2 NADH2×3 = 6 ATP
Krebs cycle6 NADH + 2 FADH₂ + 2 GTP(6×3) + (2×2) + 2 = 24 ATP
Total—~38 ATP
📝 The conversion factors

• 1 NADH → ~3 ATP
• 1 FADH₂ → ~2 ATP
• 1 GTP → 1 ATP

Modern textbooks often use ~30–32 ATP per glucose — the difference comes from how the NADH from glycolysis is shuttled into the mitochondria.

🧪
Respirometers
Measuring respiration rate
📌 Respirometer

A respirometer is a device that measures the rate of respiration by measuring oxygen consumption or carbon dioxide production.

How They Work

The overall equation for aerobic respiration predicts that the volume of oxygen used (6O₂) equals the volume of carbon dioxide produced (6CO₂). Respirometers exploit this balance:

  • For every molecule of oxygen the organism uses, a molecule of carbon dioxide is produced
  • The carbon dioxide is absorbed by potassium hydroxide (KOH)
  • This leaves a net reduction in volume inside the respirometer
  • The change in volume over time is used to calculate the rate of respiration
📝 Why KOH is used

By absorbing the CO₂ produced, the respirometer isolates the change in volume due to oxygen consumption. This makes it a simple but effective way of measuring aerobic respiration rate.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 3.6
What you should remember
  • ATP is the energy currency of the cell — a nucleotide made of adenine, ribose, and three phosphate groups
  • Overall equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ~36 ATP
  • Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol; produces 2 pyruvate, 2 ATP (net), and 2 NADH per glucose
  • Pyruvate oxidation occurs in the mitochondrial matrix; produces acetyl-CoA, NADH, and CO₂
  • Krebs cycle occurs in the matrix; each turn produces 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 GTP, and 2 CO₂
  • Electron transport chain occurs in the inner mitochondrial membrane; uses chemiosmosis to make ATP
  • Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor — it combines with H⁺ to form water
  • ATP yield per glucose ≈ 36–38 ATP (in older textbooks); modern estimates ~30–32
  • Respirometers measure the rate of respiration by measuring oxygen consumption
