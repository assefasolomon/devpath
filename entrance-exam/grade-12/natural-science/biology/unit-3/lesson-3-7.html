🍺 Fermentation — Life Without Oxygen

What happens when oxygen runs out? Cells can still extract energy from glucose — just less efficiently. Fermentation is the ancient, oxygen-free pathway that powers yeast in bread and beer, and our own muscles during intense exercise.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Glycolysis produces a net profit of 2 ATP, converts 2 NAD⁺ to 2 NADH + H⁺, and splits glucose into 2 pyruvate molecules. But without oxygen, the electron transport chain can't operate — and the cell's supply of NAD⁺ would quickly run out. Fermentation solves this problem: it recycles NADH back to NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Explain why fermentation happens (and why it doesn't need oxygen)
  • Describe alcohol fermentation and its products
  • Describe lactic acid fermentation and its products
  • Compare fermentation to aerobic respiration
  • Identify the applications of fermentation in industry and daily life
❓
Why Fermentation?
The problem and the solution
📌 The Problem

During glycolysis, NAD⁺ is reduced to form NADH + H⁺. If NAD⁺ is not replenished, glycolysis cannot continue. During aerobic respiration, the NADH formed in glycolysis is oxidized back to NAD⁺ in the electron transport chain. But when oxygen is not present, this pathway is blocked — and NAD⁺ would run out.

📌 The Solution — Fermentation

Fermentation is an anaerobic pathway (does not require oxygen) in which pyruvate is converted into another molecule, using the NADH + H⁺ produced during glycolysis to regenerate NAD⁺. This allows glycolysis to continue producing ATP.

Key Points About Fermentation

  • Does not require oxygen — it's anaerobic
  • Replenishes NAD⁺ from the NADH + H⁺ produced in glycolysis
  • Allows glycolysis to keep producing 2 ATP per glucose
  • Produces only 2 ATP per glucose — much less than aerobic respiration (~36)
  • Occurs in the cytoplasm (not in mitochondria)
  • Produces various end products depending on the organism (ethanol, lactate)
📝 Fermentation ≠ Anaerobic Respiration

These are not the same thing:

• Fermentation — an anaerobic pathway where organic molecules (like pyruvate) serve as the final electron acceptor
• Anaerobic respiration — uses an inorganic molecule (like nitrate or sulfate) as the final electron acceptor instead of O₂
• Aerobic respiration — uses oxygen as the final electron acceptor

🍷
Alcohol Fermentation
The pathway in yeast

In alcohol fermentation, pyruvate is converted into ethanol and CO₂ in two steps.

Step 1: Decarboxylation. Pyruvate loses a CO₂ molecule and becomes acetaldehyde (a 2-carbon molecule).
Step 2: Reduction. Hydrogen atoms from NADH + H⁺ are used to convert acetaldehyde into ethanol. NAD⁺ is regenerated in the process.

Overall Reaction

Glucose → 2 Ethanol + 2 CO₂ + 2 ATP

Who Does It?

  • Yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) — the classic alcohol fermenter
  • Facultative anaerobes — organisms that can undergo fermentation when deprived of oxygen, but prefer aerobic respiration when O₂ is available
  • Yeast is a facultative anaerobe — it uses oxygen when available, but ferments when it's not
📝 The bubbles in bread and beer

The CO₂ produced during alcohol fermentation is what makes:

• Bread rise — the bubbles of CO₂ get trapped in the dough
• Beer and champagne fizzy — the CO₂ stays dissolved in the drink
• Ethanol — the alcohol that gives beer, wine, and spirits their intoxicating effect

💪
Lactic Acid Fermentation
The pathway in muscle and bacteria

In lactic acid fermentation, pyruvate is converted directly into lactic acid (which ionizes to form lactate) in a single step. There is no intermediate and no CO₂ released.

Single step: Two pyruvate molecules each accept electrons from NADH + H⁺ to become 2 lactate. Two NADH + H⁺ are converted back to 2 NAD⁺.

Overall Reaction

Glucose → 2 Lactate + 2 ATP

Who Does It?

  • Some bacteria — used in making yoghurt, cheese, and fermented milk (ergo)
  • Our muscle cells — during intense exercise when oxygen can't reach muscles fast enough ("oxygen debt")
  • Other cells that lack oxygen — such as red blood cells (which have no mitochondria)
📝 Why muscles burn during exercise

When you sprint or lift heavy weights, your muscles use oxygen faster than your blood can deliver it. The muscles switch to lactic acid fermentation to keep producing ATP. The lactate that builds up contributes to the burning sensation and fatigue you feel. When you rest and catch your breath, oxygen becomes available again — and the lactate is transported to the liver and converted back to glucose or used for energy.

📝 Why fermentation still matters

Even though fermentation produces much less ATP, it's still essential:

• It allows cells to keep producing ATP when oxygen is unavailable
• It regenerates NAD⁺, allowing glycolysis to continue
• It's the basis for the food and beverage industry (bread, beer, wine, yoghurt, cheese)
• It supports muscle function during short bursts of intense activity

🌍
Applications of Fermentation
From food to fuel

Fermentation has been used by humans for thousands of years and remains essential to modern industry.

🍞
Bread

Yeast ferments sugars in dough, producing CO₂ that makes bread rise and ethanol that evaporates during baking.

🍺
Beer & Wine

Yeast ferments sugars from grains or grapes into ethanol — the alcohol in alcoholic beverages.

🧀
Cheese & Yoghurt

Lactic acid bacteria ferment milk sugar (lactose) into lactic acid, thickening the milk and creating flavour.

🥖
Injera

Ethiopian teff dough is fermented by wild yeasts and bacteria, producing CO₂ that makes the flatbread spongy.

🍯
Tej

Ethiopian honey wine fermented by yeasts — a traditional alcoholic beverage.

🍶
Tella

Ethiopian barley beer fermented by yeasts and bacteria.

⛽
Bioethanol

Yeast ferments plant sugars (from corn, sugarcane) to produce ethanol — a renewable fuel for cars.

💊
Pharmaceuticals

Fermentation is used to produce antibiotics, vitamins, and other medicines on an industrial scale.

📝 Activity 3.14 — Investigating fermentation in yeast
Simple investigation: Put yeast and glucose in a test tube with warm water. Attach a balloon to the top. Watch the balloon inflate as CO₂ is produced.
  • Independent variable — temperature, sugar type, or sugar concentration
  • Dependent variable — rate of fermentation (measured by bubbles or gas volume)
  • Control variables — yeast amount, water volume, time
Questions to explore:
  • How does temperature affect the rate of fermentation?
  • Which sugar (glucose, sucrose, lactose) ferments fastest?
  • How does sugar concentration affect the rate?

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 3.7
What you should remember
  • Fermentation = an anaerobic pathway that regenerates NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue
  • It does not require oxygen and produces only 2 ATP per glucose
  • Alcohol fermentation: pyruvate → acetaldehyde → ethanol + CO₂; done by yeast
  • Lactic acid fermentation: pyruvate → lactate; done by some bacteria and muscle cells
  • Yeast is a facultative anaerobe — it uses oxygen when available but ferments when it's not
  • Fermentation vs aerobic respiration: fermentation is much less efficient (~2 vs ~36 ATP)
  • Fermentation is used in making bread, beer, wine, cheese, yoghurt, injera, tej, tella, bioethanol, and pharmaceuticals
