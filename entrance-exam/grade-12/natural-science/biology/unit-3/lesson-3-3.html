🧈 Lipids — Fats, Oils, and Beyond

Lipids are a diverse group of molecules that don't dissolve in water — but they're essential to life. They form cell membranes, store energy, insulate our bodies, and carry hormones. This lesson explores the structure, classification, and functions of lipids.

📖 Reading: ~22 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Lipids are one of the four major classes of biological macromolecules (along with carbohydrates, proteins, and nucleic acids). Unlike the others, lipids are defined not by their structure but by their insolubility in water. They contain the same elements as carbohydrates — carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen — but with much less oxygen.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define lipids and describe their chemical composition
  • Explain the functions of lipids in the body
  • Classify lipids into simple, compound, and derived lipids
  • Describe the structure of triglycerides, phospholipids, and sterols
  • Distinguish between saturated, unsaturated, and polyunsaturated fats
  • Explain the health effects of different types of fats
📌
What are Lipids?
MoE Textbook §3.2, p. 99
📌 Lipids (MoE Textbook, p. 99)

Lipids contain the same elements as carbohydrates. However, they are made of proportionately less oxygen. Lipids are diverse molecules that dissolve in organic solvents but not in water.

Key Characteristics

  • Contain carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen — but much less oxygen than carbohydrates
  • Hydrophobic — insoluble in water (they don't dissolve)
  • Lipophilic — soluble in organic solvents (ether, chloroform, benzene)
  • Excellent energy source — provide more than double the energy per gram than carbohydrates or proteins
📝 Lipids are chemically diverse

Unlike carbohydrates (which share a similar structure), lipids are defined by their physical property — they don't dissolve in water. This means they include a wide variety of molecules, from fats and oils to cholesterol and vitamins.

💡
Functions of Lipids
Why we need them

Lipids perform many essential functions in the human body:

🧱
Cell Membranes

Phospholipids form the structural basis of all cell membranes.

⚡
Energy Storage

Triglycerides store energy — providing more than twice the energy per gram of carbohydrates.

🧊
Insulation

Fat under the skin reduces heat loss and protects against cold.

🛡️
Organ Protection

Fat cushions vital organs, protecting them from injury.

🧬
Hormones

Steroid hormones (testosterone, estrogen) are lipids.

⚡
Nerve Transmission

Lipids help transmit nerve impulses and form myelin sheaths.

🚚
Nutrient Transport

Transport fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K).

💧
Waterproofing

Waxes on leaves and feathers prevent water loss.

🧈
Simple Lipids
Fats, oils, and waxes
📌 Simple Lipids

Simple lipids yield fatty acids and glycerol (or long-chain alcohols) upon hydrolysis. There are two main types:

• Fats and oils — yield fatty acids and glycerol
• Waxes — yield fatty acids and long-chain alcohols

Fats and Oils — Triacylglycerols

Both fats and oils are called triacylglycerols because they are esters composed of three fatty acids joined to glycerol (a trihydroxy alcohol).

FeatureFatsOils
State at 25°CSolidLiquid
Fatty acidsMostly saturatedMostly unsaturated
SourceMostly animalMostly plant
ExamplesButter, lardOlive oil, sunflower oil

Structure of a Triglyceride

A triglyceride is made of one glycerol molecule (a 3-carbon alcohol) with three fatty acid chains attached to it via ester bonds. Each fatty acid is a long hydrocarbon chain ending in a carboxyl group (–COOH).

Waxes

  • A wax is an ester of a long-chain alcohol and a fatty acid
  • The alcohol is usually mono-hydroxy
  • Fatty acid chains in waxes are typically 12–34 carbons long
  • Function: protection, waterproofing (plant cuticles, bird feathers, bee honeycombs)
🔬
Compound Lipids
Lipids with extra components
📌 Compound Lipids

Compound lipids are lipids that contain additional chemical components beyond fatty acids and glycerol (or another alcohol). The two main types are phospholipids and glycolipids.

1. Phospholipids

Phospholipids are made of a fatty acid + glycerol (or other alcohol) + phosphoric acid. They are the significant lipid constituents of cell membranes.

  • Amphipathic — each phospholipid has:
    • A hydrophilic (water-loving) head — the phosphate group
    • A hydrophobic (water-fearing) tail — two fatty acid chains
  • In water, phospholipids spontaneously form a bilayer — the basis of all cell membranes
  • Heads face outward (toward water); tails face inward (away from water)

2. Glycolipids

  • Lipids with a carbohydrate attached by a glycosidic (covalent) bond
  • Role: maintain stability of the cell membrane and facilitate cellular recognition
  • Crucial for the immune response and for cells connecting to each other to form tissues
  • Found on the surface of all eukaryotic cell membranes, extending from the phospholipid bilayer into the extracellular environment
🧬
Derived Lipids
Products of lipid hydrolysis
📌 Derived Lipids

Derived lipids are the hydrolysis products of simple and compound lipids. They include fatty acids, glycerol, sphingosine, and steroid derivatives.

Sterols

Sterols such as vitamin D and cortisone are lipids that have four-carbon rings. A very familiar sterol is cholesterol, which is part of cell membranes.

  • Cholesterol — a component of cell membranes and a starting material for other lipids
  • Used to synthesize sex hormones (testosterone and estrogen)
  • Liver cells manufacture cholesterol when they break down saturated fats
  • Excess cholesterol collects on the inner linings of blood vessels, impeding blood flow and contributing to heart disease
  • Because the liver converts saturated fats into cholesterol, it's important to limit dietary intake of saturated fats
📝 Steroid hormones are lipids

Human sex hormones (testosterone and estrogen) are considered lipids because they are made from cholesterol — a sterol. This is why they can pass through cell membranes easily and act inside cells.

⚖️
Saturated vs Unsaturated Fats
The chemistry that determines health

Fatty acids differ in the number of double bonds in their hydrocarbon chains. This difference determines whether they are solid or liquid at room temperature — and whether they are healthy or harmful.

FeatureSaturated FatsUnsaturated Fats
Double bondsNone — all carbons are single-bondedAt least one double bond
StructureStraight chains — pack tightlyKinked chains (from double bonds) — don't pack tightly
State at room tempSolidLiquid
SourceMostly animal (butter, lard, red meat)Mostly plant (olive oil, sunflower oil)
Health effectRaises cholesterol; linked to heart diseaseLower cholesterol; generally healthier
ExampleButter, lard, coconut oilOlive oil, fish oil

Types of Unsaturated Fats

  • Monounsaturated fats — have just one double bond. Example: olive oil
  • Polyunsaturated fats — have more than one double bond. Example: fish oil, sunflower oil
📝 Why fat properties matter

Saturated fats pack tightly because their chains are straight — hence they're solid at room temperature. Unsaturated fats have kinks at the double bonds, so they don't pack tightly — hence they're liquid at room temperature.

This is why:

• Lipids in plants are less saturated than those in animals
• Limiting dietary intake of saturated fats is important for heart health

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 3.3
What you should remember
  • Lipids contain C, H, and O but less oxygen than carbohydrates; they're insoluble in water but dissolve in organic solvents
  • Functions: cell membranes, energy storage, insulation, protection, hormones, nerve transmission, nutrient transport, waterproofing
  • Three main classes: simple, compound, and derived lipids
  • Triglycerides = 1 glycerol + 3 fatty acids; fats are solid, oils are liquid
  • Phospholipids are amphipathic (hydrophilic head + hydrophobic tails) — they form cell membranes
  • Glycolipids have a carbohydrate attached — important for cell recognition
  • Sterols (like cholesterol) have four carbon rings — used to make sex hormones
  • Saturated fats (no double bonds) are solid and linked to heart disease; unsaturated fats (with double bonds) are liquid and healthier
