☀️ Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is the most important metabolic pathway on Earth. It captures sunlight and converts it into chemical energy — feeding nearly every living thing and producing the oxygen we breathe. Without photosynthesis, life as we know it would not exist.

📖 Reading: ~30 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources

Introduction
What you will learn

If biologists were asked to designate the most important metabolic pathway, most would not hesitate to say photosynthesis. It is the process by which plants, algae, and some microbes harness solar energy and convert it into chemical energy. It feeds every ecosystem on Earth, produces the oxygen in our atmosphere, and removes carbon dioxide — regulating our climate.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define photosynthesis and write its overall equation
  • Describe the structure of a leaf and chloroplast
  • Identify the main photosynthetic pigments
  • Explain the light-dependent reactions
  • Explain the Calvin cycle (light-independent reactions)
  • Compare C3, C4, and CAM plants
  • Describe the importance of photosynthesis for life on Earth
📌
What is Photosynthesis?
MoE Textbook §3.4, p. 107
📌 Photosynthesis (MoE Textbook, p. 107)

Photosynthesis is a series of chemical reactions that uses light energy to assemble CO₂ into glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) and other carbohydrates. The plant uses water in the process and releases oxygen gas (O₂) as a by-product.

The Overall Equation

6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

Autotrophs vs Heterotrophs

FeatureAutotrophsHeterotrophs
Meaning"Self-feeders" — produce their own food"Other-feeders" — obtain carbon by consuming organic molecules
Energy sourceSunlight (photoautotrophs) or chemicals (chemoautotrophs)Organic compounds from other organisms
ExamplesPlants, algae, cyanobacteriaAnimals, fungi, most bacteria
📝 We are heterotrophs

Human beings are heterotrophs. So are all other animals, all fungi, and many microbes. We depend entirely on autotrophs (especially plants) for the food we eat and the oxygen we breathe.

🍃
Leaf & Chloroplast Structure
Where photosynthesis happens

External Leaf Structure

  • Epidermis — the outer leaf layer; secretes a waxy coating called the cuticle that helps the plant retain water
  • Guard cells — special cells in the epidermis that regulate gas exchange
  • The leaf has three main parts: leaf base, leaf lamina, and petiole

Internal Leaf Structure

  • Epidermis with stomata — microscopic pores (singular: stoma) that allow CO₂ to enter and O₂ to exit
  • Mesophyll cells — the main photosynthetic tissue in the interior of the leaf; contain the chloroplasts
  • Vascular system — xylem vessels (bring water) and phloem (carry sugar out)

The Chloroplast

The chloroplast is the organelle where photosynthesis occurs. There are about half a million chloroplasts in a chunk of leaf with a top surface area of 1 mm². A typical mesophyll cell has about 30–40 chloroplasts, each measuring about 2–4 μm by 4–7 μm.

📝 Two sites, two reactions

Photosynthesis happens in two places inside the chloroplast:

• Thylakoid membranes — where the light-dependent reactions occur
• Stroma — where the Calvin cycle (light-independent reactions) occurs

🎨
Photosynthetic Pigments
Capturing the light
📌 Photosynthetic Pigments

Photosynthetic cells contain special pigments that absorb light energy. Different pigments respond to different wavelengths of visible light. There are three basic classes of pigments: chlorophylls, carotenoids, and phycobilins.

🟢
Chlorophylls

Greenish pigments containing a porphyrin ring. Most important is chlorophyll a (all plants, algae, cyanobacteria). Chlorophyll b occurs in green algae and plants. Chlorophyll c in Chromista and dinoflagellates.

🟠
Carotenoids

Red, orange, or yellow pigments — include carotene (gives carrots their color). They are accessory pigments that pass absorbed energy to chlorophyll. Example: fucoxanthin (brown pigment in algae).

🔵
Phycobilins

Water-soluble pigments found in the cytoplasm or in the stroma of the chloroplast. They occur only in Cyanobacteria and Rhodophyta (red algae).

Absorption Spectra

  • Chlorophyll a absorbs violet-blue and reddish orange-red wavelengths
  • Chlorophyll b absorbs mostly blue and yellow light
  • Carotenoids absorb in the blue-green region
  • None of them absorbs green — that's why leaves look green (green light is reflected to our eyes)
  • Only absorbed light (largely blue and red) is useful for photosynthesis
📝 Why plants have multiple pigments

Since each pigment absorbs only a narrow range of the spectrum, plants produce several kinds of pigments — each of a different color — to capture more solar energy. Together, they cover a wider range of wavelengths.

💡
Light-Dependent Reactions
The first stage of photosynthesis

The light reactions are the steps of photosynthesis that convert solar energy into chemical energy. They occur in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast.

What Happens in the Light Reactions

  • Water is split — providing electrons and protons (H⁺) and giving off O₂ as a by-product
  • Light absorbed by chlorophyll drives a transfer of electrons and H⁺ from water to an acceptor called NADP⁺ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate)
  • NADP⁺ is reduced to NADPH by adding a pair of electrons along with an H⁺
  • The light reactions also generate ATP using chemiosmosis to power the addition of a phosphate group to ADP — a process called photophosphorylation

Products of the Light Reactions

ProductRole
ATPThe "energy currency" — powers the Calvin cycle
NADPH"Reducing power" — carries electrons to the Calvin cycle
O₂By-product — released into the atmosphere
📝 No sugar yet

The light reactions produce no sugar. They only produce ATP and NADPH (plus O₂ as a by-product). Sugar is made in the second stage — the Calvin cycle.

Photosystems I and II

1. Electrons (e⁻) in chlorophyll molecules in photosystem II are excited by photons of light and escape to a primary electron acceptor.
2. The conditions in the chloroplast cause water to split: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻. This light-dependent splitting of water is called photolysis. The electrons replace those lost from chlorophyll.
3. The primary electron acceptor passes electrons to the next molecule in an electron transport chain (plastoquinone or Pq), then through a series of cytochromes, finally to plastocyanin (Pc).
4. One molecule in the cytochrome complex is a proton pump. As electrons pass through, the energy they lose powers the pump, moving protons from the stroma into the thylakoid space. This drives the chemiosmotic synthesis of ATP.
5. Electrons in chlorophyll molecules in photosystem I are excited and escape. They are replaced by electrons that passed down the electron transport chain from photosystem II.
6. The electrons pass along a second electron transport chain involving ferredoxin (Fd) and NADP reductase. At the end, they react with H⁺ and NADP⁺ in the stroma to form NADPH.
📝 Summary of the light reactions

Light energy is used to excite electrons. The light-dependent reactions:

• Produce oxygen gas
• Convert ADP → ATP and NADP⁺ → NADPH
• Provide the energy and reducing power needed for the Calvin cycle

🔄
Calvin Cycle
The light-independent reactions

In the Calvin cycle (also called the light-independent reactions), the energized electrons from the light reactions provide energy to form carbohydrates from carbon dioxide molecules. It is sometimes called the Calvin cycle because of its cyclical nature.

📝 "Light-independent" ≠ "dark reactions"

Although the Calvin cycle doesn't use light directly as a reactant (and can take place at day or night), it depends on the products of the light reactions (ATP and NADPH) to function. That's why we call it "light-independent" rather than "dark" reactions.

Where It Happens

The Calvin cycle takes place in the stroma of the chloroplast.

The Three Stages

1. Carbon fixation: A CO₂ molecule combines with a five-carbon acceptor molecule, ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP). This makes a six-carbon compound that splits into two molecules of a three-carbon compound, 3-phosphoglyceric acid (3-PGA). This reaction is catalyzed by the enzyme RuBP carboxylase/oxygenase (rubisco).
2. Reduction: ATP and NADPH are used to convert the 3-PGA molecules into molecules of a three-carbon sugar, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P). This stage gets its name because NADPH donates electrons (reduces) a three-carbon intermediate to make G3P.
3. Regeneration: Some G3P molecules go to make glucose, while others are recycled to regenerate the RuBP acceptor. Regeneration requires ATP.

Summary — Three Turns of the Calvin Cycle

InputOutput
Carbon: 3 CO₂ + 3 RuBP6 G3P (1 exits to make glucose, 5 recycled to regenerate 3 RuBP)
ATP: 9 ATP → 9 ADP(6 during fixation, 3 during regeneration)
NADPH: 6 NADPH → 6 NADP⁺(during the reduction step)
📝 To make one glucose

A G3P molecule contains three fixed carbon atoms, so it takes two G3Ps to build a six-carbon glucose molecule. It would take six turns of the cycle — 6 CO₂, 18 ATP, and 12 NADPH — to produce one molecule of glucose.

🌵
C3, C4, and CAM Plants
Different strategies for fixing carbon

The Calvin cycle is also known as the C3 pathway because a three-carbon molecule (3-PGA) is the first stable compound. Although all plants use the Calvin cycle, some plants have evolved additional adaptations.

The Problem — Photorespiration

  • Photosynthesis has a theoretical efficiency of about 30% in ideal conditions
  • But a plant's efficiency in nature is typically only 0.1% to 3%
  • One reason is photorespiration — a wasteful process that begins when rubisco adds O₂ instead of CO₂ to RuBP
  • This wastes ATP and NADPH — the plant loses CO₂ it fixed in previous turns

The Two Adaptations

FeatureC3 PlantsC4 PlantsCAM Plants
PathwayCalvin cycle onlyC4 + Calvin cycleCAM + Calvin cycle

Stomata openDuring the dayDuring the dayAt night

📝 Why C4 and CAM matter

In hot, dry climates, plants that minimize photorespiration have a significant competitive advantage. C4 plants physically separate the Calvin cycle from oxygen-rich air spaces in the leaf (using bundle-sheath cells). CAM plants open their stomata only at night — saving water. Both adaptations allow plants to thrive in conditions where C3 plants struggle.

🌍
Importance of Photosynthesis
Why it matters for all life

The MoE Textbook emphasises that photosynthesis is critical for many reasons. Here's a summary of its importance:

1. Source of oxygen: The oxygen in the air comes from photosynthesis. Plants continuously replenish oxygen in the air.
2. Source of food: All of our food comes directly or indirectly from photosynthesis.
3. Carbon cycle: Photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere (inhibiting global warming).
4. Fossil fuels: Ancient photosynthesis created the fossil fuels (coal, petroleum, natural gas) we use today.
5. Foundation of food chains: Plants are the primary producers at the base of nearly every food chain.
6. Oxygen balance: Photosynthesis balances respiration, maintaining atmospheric O₂ and CO₂ levels.
7. Symbiotic relationships: Photosynthesis supports the symbiotic relationships among plants, humans, and animals.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 3.5
What you should remember
  • Photosynthesis = using light energy to convert CO₂ and H₂O into glucose and O₂
  • Equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
  • It occurs in the chloroplast — light reactions in the thylakoid membranes, Calvin cycle in the stroma
  • Three classes of pigments: chlorophylls, carotenoids, phycobilins
  • Light-dependent reactions produce ATP, NADPH, and O₂; water is split (photolysis)
  • Calvin cycle has three stages: carbon fixation, reduction, regeneration — catalyzed by rubisco
  • It takes six turns of the Calvin cycle to make one glucose molecule
  • C3 plants (most plants) fix carbon directly; C4 plants (corn, sugarcane) separate steps; CAM plants (cacti) open stomata only at night
  • Photosynthesis is vital because it produces oxygen, food, fossil fuels and removes CO₂ from the atmosphere
