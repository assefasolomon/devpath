🔄 Cellular Metabolism

Every second, thousands of chemical reactions occur inside every living cell. Some build molecules up, others break them down. Together, these reactions make up metabolism — the sum of all chemical activity that keeps a cell alive. This lesson explores how cells organize, control, and coordinate these reactions.

📖 Reading: ~18 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Think of a cell as a tiny chemical factory. Thousands of reactions happen every second — some break down nutrients for energy, others build the molecules the cell needs to grow and repair itself. This constant chemical activity is called metabolism. In this lesson, you'll learn how cells organize these reactions into pathways, how enzymes control them, and how cells keep metabolism balanced.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define cellular metabolism
  • Explain what metabolic pathways are and how they work
  • Describe the role of enzymes in metabolism
  • Explain feedback inhibition and why it matters
  • Compare anabolism and catabolism
  • Explain the role of nutrition in maintaining metabolism
📌
What is Metabolism?
MoE Textbook §3.3, p. 104
📌 Metabolism (MoE Textbook, p. 104)

Metabolism (from the Greek word for "change") consists of the chemical reactions that change or transform energy in a cell. These reactions are organized into step-by-step sequences called metabolic pathways, in which the product of one reaction becomes the starting point or substrate of another.

Key Characteristics of Metabolism

  • All chemical reactions in a cell are part of metabolism
  • Reactions are organized into pathways — not random
  • Each step is catalyzed by a specific enzyme
  • The product of one reaction becomes the substrate of the next
  • A specific molecule is altered in a series of defined steps, resulting in a certain product
📝 Why metabolism matters

Without metabolism, a cell couldn't extract energy from food, build new molecules, or carry out any of the functions that keep it alive. Metabolism is the sum of all biological chemistry — it's what makes life possible.

🔗
Metabolic Pathways
Step-by-step chemical reactions
📌 Metabolic Pathways

Metabolic pathways are a series of reactions catalyzed by multiple enzymes. Each reaction produces an intermediate product that becomes the starting material for the next reaction. This step-by-step organization allows the cell to extract energy in small, manageable amounts and to control reactions precisely.

How Pathways Work

  • Each reaction in a pathway is catalyzed by a specific enzyme
  • The product of one reaction becomes the substrate of the next
  • Intermediates are called metabolites
  • Pathways can be linear (A → B → C → D) or branched (A → B → C, and B → E → F)
  • Cells can regulate pathways by activating or inhibiting enzymes at key steps
📝 Why pathways are step-by-step

If glucose were broken down in a single explosive step, all its energy would be released at once as heat — destroying the cell. By breaking it into many small steps, the cell captures energy in small packets (ATP) instead of losing it all as heat.

⚙️
Enzymes — The Catalysts
Speeding up metabolism
📌 Enzyme

An enzyme is a macromolecule (usually a protein) that speeds up a chemical reaction without being consumed by it. Enzymes enable metabolic reactions to proceed fast enough to sustain life.

Key Features of Enzymes

  • Biological catalysts — they speed up reactions
  • Highly specific — each enzyme works on a specific substrate
  • Not consumed — they can be reused many times
  • Lower activation energy — making reactions easier to start
  • Sensitive to conditions — pH and temperature affect enzyme activity
  • Named after their substrate or function (e.g., lactase breaks down lactose)
📝 Why life needs enzymes

Without enzymes, most metabolic reactions would happen far too slowly to support life. A reaction that takes seconds with an enzyme might take years without one. Enzymes are the "spark plugs" of metabolism.

⏹️
Feedback Inhibition
How cells keep metabolism balanced
📌 Feedback Inhibition

Feedback inhibition is a regulatory mechanism where the end product of a pathway inhibits an upstream process. It's an important way that cells keep metabolism balanced and prevent wasteful overproduction.

How Feedback Inhibition Works

  • A pathway produces a final product (e.g., an amino acid)
  • When enough of the product has accumulated, it binds to an earlier enzyme in the pathway
  • This binding inhibits (slows down) the enzyme — usually the first step
  • Result: the pathway slows down, saving energy and raw materials
  • When the product is used up, the enzyme is no longer inhibited, and the pathway speeds up again
📝 Real-life analogy

Think of feedback inhibition like a thermostat. When the room gets warm enough, the thermostat turns off the heater. When it cools down, the heater turns back on. Similarly, feedback inhibition keeps the cell's metabolism steady.

🏗️
Anabolism (Synthesis)
Building molecules up
📌 Anabolism (MoE Textbook, p. 105)

Anabolism is the subcategory of metabolism that helps in the construction of molecules from smaller units. It is the chemical process in which nutrients are used to form comparatively complex molecules in the living cell from simpler structures.

Key Features of Anabolism

  • Also known as biosynthesis
  • Builds up — small molecules → large molecules
  • Requires energy (in the form of ATP) — endergonic
  • Produces macromolecules like proteins, nucleic acids, and polysaccharides
  • Uses enzymes and cofactors to catalyze the reactions
  • Examples: making proteins from amino acids; making DNA from nucleotides; making starch from glucose
📝 Anabolism = growth

Anabolism is the "building" phase of metabolism. It's how organisms grow, repair tissues, and store energy. When you eat protein and your body uses it to build muscle, that's anabolism at work.

🔥
Catabolism (Degradation)
Breaking molecules down
📌 Catabolism (MoE Textbook, p. 105)

Catabolism is the subcategory of metabolism that breaks down large or complex molecules — such as proteins, polysaccharides, and fats — into small molecules like amino acids, monosaccharides, and fatty acids. It is a destructive (breaking-down) state of metabolism.

Key Features of Catabolism

  • Breaks down — large molecules → small molecules
  • Releases energy — exergonic; the energy is captured in ATP
  • Includes glycolysis and the citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle)
  • Provides the raw materials for anabolism
  • Examples: breaking down glucose into CO₂ and water; breaking down proteins into amino acids; digesting starch into glucose
📝 Catabolism = energy release

Catabolism is the "breaking-down" phase of metabolism. It's how organisms release energy from food. When you digest your lunch and your cells extract energy from glucose, that's catabolism at work.

Anabolism vs Catabolism — Summary

FeatureAnabolismCatabolism
DirectionBuilds up (synthesis)Breaks down (degradation)
EnergyRequires energy (endergonic)Releases energy (exergonic)
MoleculesSmall → largeLarge → small
ExamplesProtein synthesis, DNA synthesis, photosynthesisGlycolysis, Krebs cycle, digestion
PurposeBuild cell components, store energyProvide energy and raw materials
🍎
Nutrition & Metabolism
The link between diet and metabolism
📌 From the MoE Textbook, p. 105

To maintain a good metabolism rate, nutrition is required. The main goal of nutrition is to keep anabolism always greater than catabolism. This ensures that the body can build more than it breaks down — supporting growth, repair, and maintenance.

Nutrition and Metabolism — Key Points

  • Anabolism > Catabolism = growth and repair (positive balance)
  • Anabolism = Catabolism = stable body mass (maintenance)
  • Anabolism < Catabolism = weight loss, wasting (negative balance)
  • Good nutrition provides the raw materials needed for anabolism
  • It also provides the fuel for catabolism to release energy
  • The balance between the two determines whether you grow, stay the same, or lose mass
📝 Eating for a healthy metabolism

A balanced diet provides:

• Carbohydrates — primary fuel for catabolism (glycolysis, Krebs)
• Proteins — raw materials for anabolism (building muscle, enzymes)
• Lipids — energy storage, insulation, cell membranes
• Vitamins and minerals — cofactors for enzymes

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 3.4
What you should remember
  • Metabolism = the sum of all chemical reactions that change or transform energy in a cell
  • Metabolic reactions are organized into metabolic pathways — step-by-step sequences where the product of one reaction is the substrate of the next
  • Each step is catalyzed by a specific enzyme — a protein that speeds up the reaction
  • Feedback inhibition — the end product of a pathway inhibits an earlier step, preventing overproduction
  • Anabolism = building up molecules (requires energy; endergonic)
  • Catabolism = breaking down molecules (releases energy; exergonic)
  • Good nutrition keeps anabolism greater than catabolism — supporting growth, repair, and health
