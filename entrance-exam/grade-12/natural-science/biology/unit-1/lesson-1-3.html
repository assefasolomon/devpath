🍞 Food Security

Food is the most basic human need — yet millions of people around the world, including many in Ethiopia, don't have reliable access to enough nutritious food. This lesson explores what food security means, why it matters, and how biology helps us achieve it.

📖 Reading: ~15 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Every person needs reliable access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food to live a healthy and active life. When this access is not guaranteed, we say there is food insecurity. Ethiopia has made significant progress in recent decades, but food security remains one of the country's most important challenges.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define food security and food insecurity
  • Distinguish between chronic and transitory food insecurity
  • Explain the role of biology in improving food security
  • Describe school gardening and its benefits
  • Identify practical solutions to food insecurity in Ethiopia
📌
What is Food Security?
Key definitions
📌 Food Security (FAO Definition)

Food security exists when all people, at all times, have physical, social, and economic access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food that meets their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life.

📌 Food Insecurity

Food insecurity is the state of being without reliable access to a sufficient quantity of affordable, nutritious food. It can range from mild uncertainty about food to severe hunger.

The Three Pillars of Food Security

PillarMeaningExample
AvailabilityEnough food is physically present in the areaLocal harvests, imports, food aid
AccessPeople can actually obtain the food (economically and physically)Affordable prices, roads to markets
UtilizationFood is safe, nutritious, and properly usedClean water, cooking facilities, nutrition knowledge
📝 The fourth pillar — Stability

Many organizations add a fourth pillar: stability — ensuring availability, access, and utilization remain consistent over time, even during crises (droughts, conflicts, economic shocks).

⚠️
Types of Food Insecurity
Chronic vs transitory
TypeDefinitionExample
Chronic food insecurity Long-term or persistent — occurs when people are unable to meet minimum food requirements over a sustained period Poverty, ongoing drought, poor farming systems
Transitory food insecurity Short-term and temporary — occurs when there's a sudden drop in access to food Drought, flood, conflict, economic crisis, pandemic
Seasonal food insecurity Follows a cyclical pattern — occurs at certain times of the year Between harvests, during the rainy season

Causes of Food Insecurity in Ethiopia

  • Drought — irregular rainfall causes crop failures
  • Soil degradation — erosion and nutrient loss reduce yields
  • Rapid population growth — more people needing more food
  • Poverty — many people cannot afford to buy enough food
  • Conflict and displacement — people forced to leave their farms
  • Limited technology — many farmers lack irrigation, fertilizers, and improved seeds
  • Climate change — shifting rainfall patterns and increased temperatures
  • Poor infrastructure — bad roads make it hard to distribute food
🧬
Biology's Role in Food Security
How biology helps feed the world

Biology directly contributes to food security in many ways — from improving crops to managing pests to preserving food.

🌾
Improved Crop Varieties

Plant breeding and genetic engineering produce crops with higher yields, drought tolerance, and disease resistance.

🐛
Integrated Pest Management

Using biological controls (natural predators, resistant varieties) reduces crop losses without excessive chemicals.

🌱
Soil Biology

Understanding soil microorganisms helps maintain soil fertility naturally.

💧
Water Management

Biological knowledge supports irrigation, drought-resistant plants, and efficient water use.

🧫
Food Preservation

Microbiology guides fermentation, canning, drying, and other preservation techniques.

🐄
Animal Science

Improved livestock breeds, vaccines, and nutrition increase meat, milk, and egg production.

School Gardening
Practical biology in action

School gardening is one of the most powerful practical applications of biology for food security. It teaches students how to grow food and gives them skills they can use for life.

Benefits of School Gardening

  • Practical biology learning — students see firsthand how plants grow, how photosynthesis works, and how soil and water interact
  • Nutrition education — students learn to grow and eat healthy vegetables
  • Food supply — school gardens can supplement school meals and family diets
  • Environmental awareness — students learn about soil health, composting, and water conservation
  • Life skills — gardening teaches patience, responsibility, and teamwork
  • Community impact — students share what they learn with their families and communities

Setting Up a School Garden

StepAction
1Choose a suitable spot — good sunlight, access to water, near the school
2Prepare the soil — remove weeds, add compost or manure
3Choose crops — vegetables like kale, carrots, onions, tomatoes
4Plant seeds or seedlings — follow spacing guidelines
5Water regularly — morning or evening, not midday
6Control pests and weeds — use natural methods when possible
7Harvest and share — use in school meals or share with families
8Compost — recycle plant waste back into the soil

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 1.3
What you should remember
  • Food security = all people, at all times, have physical, social, and economic access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food
  • Three pillars: Availability, Access, Utilization (some add Stability)
  • Chronic food insecurity is long-term; transitory is short-term; seasonal follows yearly cycles
