💊 Medicine

Biology is the foundation of modern medicine. From diagnosing diseases to developing vaccines to transplanting organs, biology provides the knowledge and tools that save millions of lives every year. This lesson explores how biology is applied in medicine.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Modern medicine is one of the greatest achievements of biological science. Vaccines have eradicated smallpox and nearly eliminated polio. Antibiotics have turned once-deadly infections into minor inconveniences. Genetic testing can predict disease risk. Organ transplants save thousands of lives each year — all because of our understanding of biology.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define health and explain the biological basis of medicine
  • Describe how diagnostic techniques like PCR work
  • Explain the biological principles behind vaccines
  • Understand immunotherapy and its role in cancer treatment
  • Describe the biology of organ transplantation
📌
What is Health?
Key definitions
📌 Health (WHO Definition)

Health is a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being — not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.

📌 Medicine

Medicine is the science and practice of diagnosing, treating, and preventing disease. Modern medicine is built on biology, chemistry, and physics, with biology as its foundation.

Biology's Contributions to Medicine

🔬
Diagnostics

PCR, ELISA, microscopy, imaging — detecting disease at the molecular level.

💉
Vaccines

Training the immune system to prevent disease — smallpox eradicated, polio nearly gone.

💊
Pharmaceuticals

Antibiotics, antivirals, and other drugs developed from biological knowledge.

🧬

Genetic testing, gene therapy, personalized medicine, genetic counseling.

Organ and tissue transplants save lives — with careful management of immune rejection.

Diagnostics & PCR
Detecting disease at the molecular level

Diagnostics is the process of identifying a disease, condition, or injury. Modern diagnostics rely heavily on biology.

📌 PCR — Polymerase Chain Reaction

PCR is a technique that copies a specific piece of DNA millions of times so it can be detected and analyzed. It's used to diagnose infectious diseases (like COVID-19, HIV, TB), detect genetic disorders, and identify cancer mutations.

How PCR Works — Three Steps

StepTemperatureWhat happens
1. Denaturation~95 °CDouble-stranded DNA separates into two single strands
2. Annealing~55 °CPrimers (short DNA sequences) bind to the target DNA regions
3. Extension~72 °CDNA polymerase builds new strands, copying the target DNA

These three steps are repeated 25–40 times. Each cycle doubles the amount of DNA — after 30 cycles, one copy becomes over a billion copies.

Other Diagnostic Techniques

  • ELISA — detects antibodies or antigens in blood; used for HIV, hepatitis, and other infections
  • Microscopy — direct observation of cells and microorganisms; used for malaria, TB, and blood disorders
  • Genetic testing — analyzes DNA for mutations linked to inherited diseases
  • Imaging — X-rays, CT, MRI, and ultrasound visualize internal structures
  • Biopsy — examination of tissue samples for cancer and other diseases
💉
Vaccines
Training the immune system
📌 Vaccine

A vaccine is a biological preparation that provides active acquired immunity to a particular infectious disease. It typically contains an agent resembling a disease-causing microorganism — often made from weakened or killed forms of the microbe, its toxins, or one of its surface proteins.

How Vaccines Work

  • The vaccine introduces a harmless form of the pathogen (or its antigen) into the body
  • The immune system recognizes the antigen as foreign and produces antibodies against it
  • The immune system also creates memory cells that "remember" the pathogen
  • If the real pathogen ever enters the body, memory cells respond quickly and effectively — preventing illness

Types of Vaccines

TypeHow it's madeExamples
Live attenuatedWeakened but live pathogenMMR, chickenpox, BCG
InactivatedKilled pathogenPolio (IPV), flu shot
ToxoidInactivated toxinTetanus, diphtheria
SubunitSpecific protein from pathogenHepatitis B, HPV
mRNAGenetic instructions for antigenCOVID-19 (Pfizer, Moderna)
📝 Vaccines save millions of lives

According to the WHO, vaccines prevent 3.5–5 million deaths every year from diseases like diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, influenza, and measles. Smallpox — once a global killer — has been completely eradicated thanks to vaccination.

🎯
Immunotherapy
Harnessing the immune system to fight disease
📌 Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy is a type of treatment that uses the body's own immune system to fight diseases like cancer. Rather than attacking the disease directly, immunotherapy helps the immune system recognize and destroy abnormal cells.

Types of Immunotherapy

TypeHow it works
Monoclonal antibodiesLab-made antibodies that bind to specific targets on cancer cells, marking them for destruction
Checkpoint inhibitorsDrugs that "release the brakes" on the immune system so it can attack cancer cells
CAR T-cell therapyT-cells are removed from the patient, engineered to recognize cancer, and returned
Cancer vaccinesVaccines that stimulate the immune system against cancer cells
CytokinesProteins that boost immune cell activity
📝 Why immunotherapy is exciting

Traditional cancer treatments (surgery, radiation, chemotherapy) attack cancer directly — and often damage healthy cells too. Immunotherapy is different: it trains the patient's own immune system to do the work, often with fewer side effects and longer-lasting results.

🫀
Transplantation
The biology of organ transplants
📌 Transplantation

Transplantation is the transfer of organs, tissues, or cells from one person (the donor) to another (the recipient) to replace a damaged or failing organ. Common transplants include kidney, heart, liver, lung, pancreas, and cornea.

The Immune Challenge

The biggest challenge in transplantation is immune rejection. The recipient's immune system recognizes the donor organ as foreign because of differences in proteins called HLA antigens (Human Leukocyte Antigens) on the surface of donor cells.

How Rejection is Managed

  • HLA matching — the closer the donor and recipient match, the lower the risk of rejection
  • Immunosuppressant drugs — suppress the immune system to prevent it from attacking the donor organ
  • Blood type matching — ABO compatibility is essential
  • Crossmatching — testing for pre-existing antibodies against the donor
  • Regular monitoring — lifelong check-ups to detect early signs of rejection
📝 Organ donation

Ethiopia and many other countries face a critical shortage of organ donors. Educating the public about organ donation and improving transplant infrastructure can save thousands of lives each year.
