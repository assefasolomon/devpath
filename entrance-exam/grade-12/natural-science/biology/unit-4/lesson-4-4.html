🦴 Evidence for Evolution

How do we know evolution actually happened? Evidence comes from many sources — from the structure of our own bodies to the fossils in ancient rocks to the DNA in every cell. This lesson explores the four main lines of evidence that support the theory of evolution.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Evolution is not just a theory — it's supported by multiple independent lines of evidence from different fields of biology. Each line of evidence points to the same conclusion: that modern organisms share common ancestors and have changed over time.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Explain how comparative anatomy provides evidence for evolution
  • Distinguish between homologous and analogous structures
  • Describe how embryology supports common ancestry
  • Explain what fossils tell us about evolution
  • Describe how fossils are dated
  • Explain how biochemistry supports the theory of evolution
🦴
Comparative Anatomy
MoE Textbook §4.2.7.1

Comparative anatomy is one of the strongest forms of evidence for evolution. It looks at the structural similarities of organisms and uses these similarities to determine their possible evolutionary relationships.

📌 Homologous structures

Homologous structures are structures with the same basic anatomy and a common evolutionary origin, but having a different function. They indicate an evolutionary relationship and a common ancestor.

The Classic Example — Mammalian Forelimbs

The forelimbs of humans, whales, cats, and bats are all very similar in structure. Each possesses:

  • The same number of bones — humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals, phalanges
  • Arranged in almost the same way
  • But with different external features that function in different ways
OrganismForelimb used for
HumanManipulation — arm for grasping
CatRunning — leg
WhaleSwimming — flipper
BatFlying — wing
📝 Pentadactyl limb

Because of these structural similarities, scientists have determined that the basic pattern — called a pentadactyl limb (a limb with five digits) — must have evolved just once. All organisms with this kind of limb descended from that original type, sharing a common ancestor.

📌 Analogous structures

Analogous structures are structures with the same function but different anatomy and different evolutionary origin. Because they are so different structurally, they cannot indicate that two species share a common ancestor.

Analogous Structures — Example

  • The wings of a bat, bird, and mosquito all serve the same function (flying)
  • But their anatomies are very different
  • The bird wing has bones inside and is covered with feathers
  • The mosquito wing has neither
  • They are analogous structures that evolved separately
📝 Why homologous structures matter

Homologous structures → evidence of common ancestry (divergent evolution)
Analogous structures → NOT evidence of common ancestry; they are examples of convergent evolution (independent evolution of similar features)

🐣
Embryology
MoE Textbook §4.2.7.2

Comparative embryology studies the way in which the embryos of vertebrates develop before they hatch or are born. This development shows similarities which support a common ancestry.

Key Observations

  • Early in development, all vertebrate embryos have gill slits and tails
  • The 'gill slits' are not gills — they connect the throat to the outside, but in many species they disappear later in development
  • In fish and larval amphibians, they contribute to the development of gills
  • The embryonic tail does not develop into a tail in all species
  • In humans, it is reduced during development to the coccyx (tailbone)
📝 What this suggests

The more similar the patterns of embryonic development, the more closely related the species are assumed to be. The similarity in the development of vertebrates suggests a common ancestor.

Fish, reptiles, birds, and mammals — all very different as adults — look remarkably similar as embryos. This shared developmental pattern is powerful evidence of common ancestry.

🦕
Palaeontology
MoE Textbook §4.2.7.3 — The fossil record
📌 Palaeontology

Palaeontology is the study of fossils. Fossils are formed when certain remains of organisms or plants get embedded in the soil or water and are preserved for many hundreds of years. They appear as skeletal remains, footprints, moulds, or intact structures.

Two Categories of Fossils

CategoryDescriptionExamples
Body fossils Remains of dead animals or plants, or the imprint left from the remains Bones, teeth, skin impressions, hair, hardened shells (e.g. trilobite, ammonite)
Trace fossils Something made by the animal while it was living, that has hardened into stone Footprints, burrows, coprolite (animal faeces)

How Fossils Provide Evidence

  • By studying fossils, we can establish similarities between present organisms and their ancestors
  • Fossils prove the theory of evolution and common ancestry
  • They show transitional forms — species that have features of both ancestral and modern groups
  • Fossils show that species that lived long ago are often different from those living today
📝 Amber fossils

Some fossils are preserved in amber — fossilised tree resin. A spider trapped in amber millions of years ago can be preserved with incredible detail — sometimes with the entire body intact. Amber protects the specimen from microorganisms and air, which would normally cause decomposition.

⏳
Dating Fossils
How we know how old fossils are

Determining the age of fossils is essential for understanding the sequence of evolution. Scientists use two main techniques:

1. Stratigraphy — Relative Dating

  • Sedimentary rocks are laid down in layers called strata
  • The oldest strata and oldest fossils are found in the lowest layers
  • More recent rocks and fossils are in the layers above them, nearer to the surface
  • The depth of the strata is related to age; the thickness indicates the time period during which the stratum was formed
  • The named geological periods — Tertiary, Cretaceous, Jurassic — refer to specific time spans

2. Radiometric Dating — Absolute Dating

  • Some minerals in rocks and organic matter (e.g. wood, bones, shells) contain radioactive isotopes
  • The abundances of parent and daughter isotopes in a sample can be measured and used to determine age
  • The time it takes for half the parent isotopes to decay into daughter isotopes is the half-life
  • Radiocarbon dating (C-14) — measures the ratio of radioactive C-14 to normal carbon; suitable for fossils up to 60,000 years old
  • Potassium-argon dating (K-40) — suitable for much older rocks and fossils, up to millions of years

Half-life principle: During one half-life, half of the radioactive atoms decay. So starting with a certain number of atoms:
• After 1 half-life — 50% remain
• After 2 half-lives — 25% remain
• After 3 half-lives — 12.5% remain
• After n half-lives — (1/2)ⁿ remain

📝 Ethiopia's fossil treasures

Ethiopia is one of the most important countries in the world for studying human evolution. Fossils of many hominin species have been found in the Afar region, the Omo Valley, and the Middle Awash. Some of the most famous discoveries include:

• Lucy (Dinkinesh) — Australopithecus afarensis, 3.2 million years old
• Ardi — Ardipithecus ramidus, 4.4 million years old
• Selam — a young A. afarensis child, 3.3 million years old

This is why Ethiopia is often called the "cradle of mankind".

🧬
Biochemistry
MoE Textbook §4.2.7.4

Organisms that share very similar molecules and biochemical pathways are closely related evolutionarily. This is one of the strongest forms of evidence because it's based on the actual molecules of life.

The Key Idea

  • Species that are closely related are believed to have the most similar DNA and proteins
  • Species that are distantly related share fewer similarities
  • Chemicals used in such analysis include DNA, RNA, haemoglobin, and cytochrome c

The Chimpanzee Evidence

A comparison of DNA sequences shows that 98% of our DNA is the same as chimpanzees. This confirms that chimpanzees are the closest relatives of humans.

DNA Hybridisation

To measure the similarity of DNA between two species, scientists use a technique called DNA hybridisation:

  • The double helix of DNA from both species is heated to separate it into single strands
  • Single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) from both species is mixed and cooled
  • The ssDNA from the two species will hybridise (bind) as it cools, but not perfectly along all its length
  • The percentage of mismatching is measured
  • This gives the percentage similarity of the DNA samples

Haemoglobin Comparisons

The haemoglobin molecule is similar in all animals that possess it, but there are differences. For example:

  • The haemoglobin of the lamprey (a primitive fish-like animal) has only one polypeptide chain, not four
  • Most animals have haemoglobin with four chains, but the chains do vary
  • By comparing the amino acid sequences of haemoglobin, scientists can determine how different species diverged from the evolutionary line that led to humans
  • Species with more similar haemoglobin are more closely related
📝 Why biochemistry is powerful evidence

Biochemistry provides evidence that is independent of anatomy, embryology, and fossils — yet points to the same conclusions. When multiple independent lines of evidence agree, the case for evolution becomes overwhelmingly strong.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 4.4
What you should remember
  • Comparative anatomy: Homologous structures (same anatomy, different function) suggest common ancestry; analogous structures (same function, different anatomy) do not
  • Pentadactyl limb — the basic five-digit limb pattern evolved once and was inherited by all mammals
  • Embryology: Vertebrate embryos are remarkably similar early in development (gill slits, tails), suggesting common ancestry
  • Palaeontology: Fossils provide evidence of past life and extinct species
  • Stratigraphy: Older fossils are found in lower rock layers
  • Radiometric dating: Uses radioactive isotopes (C-14, K-40) to determine absolute age; relies on half-life
  • Biochemistry: Similar DNA and proteins indicate close evolutionary relationships; humans share ~98% of DNA with chimpanzees
  • DNA hybridisation measures the percentage similarity between two species' DNA
  • Multiple independent lines of evidence all point to the same conclusion — modern organisms share common ancestors
