🐦 Darwinism & Neo-Darwinism

In 1859, Charles Darwin published On the Origin of Species — a book that would transform biology forever. His theory of natural selection explained how species change over time. Today, Neo-Darwinism combines Darwin's ideas with modern genetics. This lesson explores both.

📖 Reading: ~22 min

🎥 Videos: 4 resources

Introduction
What you will learn

Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace jointly proposed that evolution occurs because of a phenomenon called natural selection. Darwin's theory has become the foundation of modern biology. In this lesson, we explore Darwin's theory, its key postulates, the classic example of Galapagos finches, and how Darwin's ideas were later refined into Neo-Darwinism.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe who Charles Darwin was and his contribution to biology
  • Explain the significance of the Galapagos Islands in Darwin's work
  • List the four postulates of Darwinism
  • Give a modern example of natural selection (antibiotic resistance)
  • Describe the three postulates of Neo-Darwinism
  • Compare Darwinism and Neo-Darwinism
👨‍🔬
Charles Darwin
The father of evolution
📌 Charles Darwin (1809–1882)

Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace jointly proposed that evolution occurs because of a phenomenon called natural selection. Darwin's 1859 book, On the Origin of Species, laid the foundation for modern evolutionary biology.

The Voyage of the Beagle

  • Darwin was highly influenced by an essay titled 'Principles of Geology' written by Charles Lyell — which argued that Earth is very old and has changed gradually
  • Darwin visited the Galapagos Islands during his voyage on HMS Beagle (1831–1836)
  • He collected specimens and made detailed observations across many regions
  • These observations led him to develop the theory of natural selection over the following decades
📝 Darwin's delay

Darwin returned from his voyage in 1836 but did not publish his theory until 1859 — more than 20 years later. He wanted to gather as much evidence as possible and was aware of the controversy his ideas would cause. He was finally prompted to publish when Alfred Russel Wallace independently arrived at the same conclusions.

🏝️
The Galapagos Islands
Darwin's natural laboratory

The Galapagos Islands are a small group of islands in the Pacific Ocean, about 600 miles off the coast of Ecuador in South America. Darwin visited five of the Galapagos Islands and made drawings and collected specimens.

The Finches of the Galapagos

Darwin studied the finches found on the different islands. He noted:

  • There were many similarities between them
  • There were also obvious differences — especially in their beaks
  • Some finches had pointed beaks for eating insects
  • Others had thick beaks for crushing seeds

Darwin's Conclusion

An 'ancestral finch' had colonised the islands from the mainland. In the absence of predators, these finches were able to adapt to the different conditions on the islands. Over time, they evolved into different species. Some became insect eaters with pointed beaks; others became seed eaters with crushing beaks.

📝 Adaptive radiation

This is a classic example of adaptive radiation — a process where one ancestral species diversifies into many new species, each adapted to a different niche. The finches' beaks are adaptations to different food sources.

📋
Four Postulates of Darwinism
The core of Darwin's theory

Darwin's theory of natural selection rests on four main postulates:

1
Geometric Increase

All species tend to produce more offspring than can possibly survive. However, the space and food supply are limited — they cannot support the number of organisms that would result from unchecked growth.

2
Struggle for Existence

Since the number of individuals produced is far more than can be supported, there is an everlasting competition between organisms at all levels of life — for food, space, and mates.

3
Variation Under Nature

No two individuals of a species are exactly similar — they have small differences called variations. Without variation, evolution is impossible. Variation gives rise to new characters, and heredity passes them on to the next generation (inheritance of useful variations).

4
Natural Selection (Survival of the Fittest)

Due to the struggle for existence and useful heritable variations, only those individuals survive which show high selective value. In the course of time, they develop various adaptive modifications to suit the changed conditions of life. This is natural selection.

📝 "Survival of the fittest"

Natural selection is sometimes summed up as "survival of the fittest" — because the "fittest" organisms (those most suited to their environment) are the ones that reproduce most successfully, and are most likely to pass on their traits to the next generation.

Important: "Fittest" does not mean physically strongest. It means best adapted to the current environment. A small bird with the right beak shape may be "fitter" than a large, strong bird with the wrong beak.

💊
Antibiotic Resistance
Natural selection in action

Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is a modern, observable example of natural selection. It shows evolution happening in real time.

How It Works

Step 1: A population of non-resistant bacteria exists.
Step 2: The bacteria multiply by the billions. A few of these bacteria will mutate randomly.
Step 3: Some mutations make the bacterium drug-resistant.
Step 4: In the presence of the drug (antibiotic), only the drug-resistant bacteria survive.
Step 5: The drug-resistant bacteria multiply and thrive — passing on their resistance genes to the next generation.

📝 Why this matters

Antibiotic resistance is a major public health concern. When we overuse antibiotics (e.g., for viral infections where they don't work, or without completing the full course), we create conditions that favour the resistant bacteria. This is why doctors emphasize responsible antibiotic use.

🔄
Neo-Darwinism
Darwin's theory updated with genetics
📌 Neo-Darwinism

Neo-Darwinism is a revised version of Darwin's theory of evolution by means of natural selection. This theory, now accepted by most biologists, combines Darwin's original theory, genetic theory, and theories about animal behaviour.

Why Neo-Darwinism Was Needed

  • Charles Darwin knew very little about genetics
  • Mendel had not yet published his ground-breaking work on inheritance when Darwin wrote On the Origin of Species
  • Later, scientists incorporated our knowledge of genes and gene action into the theory of natural selection to give a better understanding of what drives evolution

The Gene Pool

Genes determine features. But when we think about how a population might evolve into a new species, we need to think not just about the alleles each individual might carry, but also about all the alleles (all the genes) available in the population. We call this the gene pool of the population.

Three Postulates of Neo-Darwinism

1
Genetic Variability

Populations contain genetic variation — different alleles that affect traits. This variation is the raw material for evolution. Sources include mutation, meiosis, and sexual reproduction.

2
Natural Selection

Some alleles give individuals a survival advantage. Those individuals reproduce more, and their advantageous alleles become more common in the gene pool over generations.

3
Reproductive Isolation

Populations become reproductively isolated from each other. Over time, they accumulate genetic differences and eventually cannot interbreed — becoming separate species.

📝 Key contributors

Scientists who contributed to Neo-Darwinism include Huxley and R.A. Fisher. The theory reconciles Darwin's original ideas with the genetics of de Vries and Mendel.

Darwinism vs Neo-Darwinism — Comparison

FeatureDarwinismNeo-Darwinism
Time period18591930s–present (Modern Synthesis)
Main mechanismNatural selectionNatural selection + genetics
Source of variationUnknown (not explained)Mutation, meiosis, sexual reproduction
Unit of evolutionIndividualPopulation gene pool
SpeciationAccumulation of small changesGenetic variability + natural selection + reproductive isolation

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 4.3
What you should remember
  • Charles Darwin (1809–1882) and Alfred Russel Wallace proposed natural selection as the mechanism of evolution
  • Darwin's book On the Origin of Species (1859) laid the foundation of modern evolutionary biology
  • The Galapagos finches showed Darwin how one ancestral species could diversify into many — adaptive radiation
  • Four postulates of Darwinism: geometric increase, struggle for existence, variation, natural selection
  • Natural selection = survival of the fittest — meaning best adapted, not necessarily strongest
  • Antibiotic resistance is a modern, observable example of natural selection
  • Neo-Darwinism = Darwin's theory + modern genetics + behavioural theory
  • Three postulates of Neo-Darwinism: genetic variability, natural selection, reproductive isolation
  • The gene pool is the sum of all alleles in a population — the raw material for evolution
