⚖️ Natural Selection in Action

Natural selection doesn't always work the same way. Depending on the environment, it can favour one extreme, the middle, or both extremes — and these different "modes" can lead to new species. This lesson explores the three types of natural selection and how new species form.

📖 Reading: ~22 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Natural selection is the driving force behind evolution. It brings about changes over time in populations that can, eventually, lead to different populations of the same species becoming different species. But natural selection doesn't always work in the same way. Depending on the selection pressure, it can push a population in different directions.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe three types of natural selection — directional, stabilising, and disruptive
  • Explain what a species is and why the definition matters
  • Describe allopatric speciation (geographic isolation)
  • Describe sympatric speciation (reproductive isolation)
  • Explain the role of polyploidy in plant evolution
  • Distinguish between divergent, convergent, and parallel evolution
📊
Three Types of Natural Selection
Different ways selection changes a population

All types of natural selection work in the same manner — but their influence on a population is different. There are three main types:

➡️
Directional Selection

One extreme of a range of values has a survival advantage. The range of values for the population shifts towards the extreme with the selective advantage.

🎯
Stabilising Selection

The two extremes are at a selective disadvantage compared to those showing the mean values for a particular feature. The range is compressed around the mean.

↔️
Disruptive Selection

Both extremes have a selective advantage compared with the mean. Two distinct types begin to emerge showing the extreme values of the original population.

Real-World Examples

TypeExampleResult
Directional Antibiotic resistance in bacteria — the antibiotic selects for resistant bacteria Population shifts towards resistance
Stabilising Human birth weight — babies born too small or too large have higher mortality Average birth weight remains stable
Disruptive Finches with either very large or very small beaks survive, but medium beaks don't Two distinct beak sizes emerge
📝 Which type leads to new species?

Disruptive selection is most likely to lead to speciation because it creates two distinct groups within a population. If these groups become reproductively isolated, they can evolve into two separate species.

🆕
Speciation
How new species form
📌 Species (MoE Textbook, p. 180)

A species is a group of similar organisms with a similar biochemistry, physiology, and evolutionary history that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring.

Speciation

Speciation is the formation of new species. Natural selection provides a mechanism by which new populations of a species can arise. But at what point can these populations be considered as distinct species?

  • If two populations become so different that individuals from these different populations cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring, then we must think of them as different species
  • As long as two populations are able to interbreed, they are unlikely to evolve into distinct species
  • They must go through a period when they are prevented from interbreeding

The Two Main Ways

There are two main ways in which speciation can occur — allopatric and sympatric. Both involve isolating mechanisms that prevent different populations from interbreeding for a period of time.

  • During this period, mutations that arise in one population cannot be passed to the other
  • As a result, and because of different selection pressures in different environments, genetic differences between the two populations increase
  • Eventually, the two populations become so different that they are unable to interbreed — they are reproductively isolated
🌄
Allopatric Speciation
Geographic isolation first
📌 Allopatric Speciation

Allopatric speciation occurs when a population from an existing species becomes geographically isolated and the isolated population develops into a new species.

How It Works

  • A physical barrier (mountain range, river, ocean, desert) separates a population into two groups
  • The two groups can no longer interbreed
  • They experience different selection pressures in their different environments
  • Mutations and genetic drift accumulate independently in each group
  • Over many generations, the two groups become so different that they can no longer interbreed — new species form
📝 Example

The Galapagos finches are a classic example of allopatric speciation — an ancestral finch colonised one island, then individuals occasionally flew to other islands. Each island population adapted to local conditions and, over time, evolved into a distinct species.

🏝️
Sympatric Speciation
Reproductive isolation first
📌 Sympatric Speciation

Sympatric speciation occurs when a population from an existing species develops into a new species without becoming geographically isolated from other members of the original species.

How It Works

  • Populations remain in the same geographic area
  • They become reproductively isolated by other mechanisms:
    • Different breeding times (e.g. flowering at different times)
    • Different courtship behaviours
    • Different habitats within the same area
    • Polyploidy — a change in chromosome number that instantly creates reproductive isolation
  • Over time, the two groups diverge genetically and become separate species
📝 Allopatric vs Sympatric

• Allopatric = geographic isolation first, then reproductive isolation
• Sympatric = reproductive isolation first, without geographic isolation

🔬
Polyploidy
A special case in plant evolution
📌 Polyploidy

Polyploidy occurs when an organism has more than two sets of homologous chromosomes. Poly means many. Polyploid cells have many sets of chromosomes per cell — sometimes four sets, sometimes eight or more.

Why It Matters

  • Polyploidy has been important in plant evolution because it has allowed infertile hybrids to become fertile
  • When different species form hybrids, very often the hybrid cannot produce offspring because all the chromosomes cannot form bivalents (homologous pairs) in meiosis
  • So they cannot form sex cells and cannot reproduce
  • But if the chromosome number were to double, then all chromosomes would be able to form homologous pairs
  • Meiosis and sex-cell formation can take place — and the hybrid is now fertile
📝 Key terms

• Polyploid — having more than two sets of homologous chromosomes
• Tetraploid — having exactly four sets of homologous chromosomes (e.g. some human liver cells have 92 chromosomes — they are tetraploid)

Polyploidy is common in plants (many crop plants like wheat, potatoes, and bananas are polyploid) but rare in animals.

🔀
Divergent, Convergent & Parallel Evolution
Three patterns of evolution

Evolution over time can follow several different patterns. Factors such as environment and predation pressures can have different effects on the ways in which species evolve. There are three main patterns:

↗️↘️
Divergent Evolution

Two species gradually become increasingly different. Often occurs when closely related species diversify to new habitats. Responsible for the diversity of life on Earth.

↘️↗️
Convergent Evolution

Species of different ancestry begin to share analogous traits because of a shared environment or other selection pressure. Example: whales and fish both evolved to move through water.

➡️➡️
Parallel Evolution

Two species evolve independently of each other, maintaining the same level of similarity. Usually occurs between unrelated species that do not occupy the same niche.

📝 Divergent vs Convergent — Key Difference

• Divergent: one ancestor → many different descendants (homologous structures)
• Convergent: many different ancestors → similar features (analogous structures)

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 4.5
What you should remember
  • Natural selection is the driving force of evolution
  • Three types: directional (favours one extreme), stabilising (favours the mean), disruptive (favours both extremes)
  • A species = group of organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring
  • Speciation = the formation of new species; requires reproductive isolation
  • Allopatric speciation = geographic isolation first, then reproductive isolation
  • Sympatric speciation = reproductive isolation first, without geographic separation
  • Polyploidy = having more than two sets of chromosomes; important in plant evolution because it can make sterile hybrids fertile
  • Divergent evolution = one ancestor → many different species (homologous structures)
  • Convergent evolution = different ancestors → similar features (analogous structures)
  • Parallel evolution = unrelated species maintain similar features independently
