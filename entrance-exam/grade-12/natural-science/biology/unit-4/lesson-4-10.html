Origin of Life — Quick Review
From Lesson 4.1

The Five Theories

TheoryCore claimStatus
Special creationismA supreme being created lifeReligious, not scientific
Spontaneous generationLife arises from non-living matterDisproved by Redi & Pasteur
Eternity of lifeLife has always existedIncompatible with Big Bang
Cosmozoan (panspermia)Life came from spaceUnlikely (life can't survive space)
Biochemical (abiogenesis)Life arose from chemical reactionsCurrently accepted; supported by Miller's experiment
📝 Key experiments

• Redi (1668) — showed maggots come from fly eggs, not rotting meat
• Pasteur (1860s) — showed microorganisms come from outside, not from broth
• Miller (1953) — produced amino acids from simple gases and electrical sparks

📝 Four postulates of Darwinism

1. Geometric increase — more offspring than can survive
2. Struggle for existence — competition for limited resources
3. Variation under nature — no two individuals are identical
4. Natural selection — the fittest survive and reproduce

Evidence for Evolution — Quick Review
From Lesson 4.4
Type of evidenceKey findings
Comparative anatomyHomologous structures (same anatomy, different function) suggest common ancestry — e.g. pentadactyl limb. Analogous structures (same function, different anatomy) do not.
EmbryologyVertebrate embryos share gill slits and tails early in development — suggests a common ancestor
PalaeontologyFossils show the history of life and transitional forms
StratigraphyOlder fossils found in lower rock layers
Radiometric datingUses radioactive isotopes (C-14, K-40) and half-life to determine absolute ages
BiochemistrySimilar DNA and proteins indicate close relationships; humans share ~98% of DNA with chimpanzees
DNA hybridisationMeasures the percentage similarity between two species' DNA

Natural Selection — Quick Review
From Lesson 4.5

Three Types of Selection

TypeWhat is favouredResult
DirectionalOne extremePopulation shifts towards the favoured extreme
StabilisingThe meanPopulation stays around the average
DisruptiveBoth extremesTwo distinct groups emerge

Speciation

  • Species = group of organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring
  • Speciation = formation of new species; requires reproductive isolation
  • Allopatric speciation = geographic isolation first
  • Sympatric speciation = reproductive isolation first, without geographic separation
  • Polyploidy — more than two sets of chromosomes; important in plant evolution

Three Patterns of Evolution

  • Divergent — one ancestor → many different species (homologous structures)
  • Convergent — different ancestors → similar features (analogous structures)
  • Parallel — unrelated species evolve similarly and independently

Human Evolution — Quick Review
From Lesson 4.6

Key Facts

  • We did not evolve from chimpanzees — we share a common ancestor (~6 million years ago)
  • Two distinctive features: large brain and bipedalism
  • Bipedalism evolved before large brains — proven by Lucy
  • Lucy — discovered 1974 at Hadar, Ethiopia; 3.2 million years old; Australopithecus afarensis
  • Ardi — discovered 1992 in Afar, Ethiopia; 4.4 million years old; Ardipithecus ramidus
  • Out of Africa — Homo sapiens appeared in Africa and migrated out ~60,000–70,000 years ago, beginning near Ethiopia
📝 Brain size evolution

Australopithecus: 0.7–1.0% of body mass
Modern humans: 1.8–2.3% of body mass
Human brain uses 25% of resting energy; great apes use 8%.

Mutations & Drift — Quick Review
From Lessons 4.7 & 4.8

Point Mutations

  • Substitution — one base replaced; only affects one codon
  • Addition / Deletion — cause frameshift mutations that alter everything after the mutation
  • Sickle-cell anaemia — a substitution changes GAG → GTG, causing valine to replace glutamate in haemoglobin
  • Chromosomal mutations — duplication, deletion, inversion, translocation

Genetic Drift & Gene Flow

  • Genetic drift — random changes in allele frequencies; stronger in small populations
  • Bottleneck effect — catastrophe reduces population to a random subset of survivors
  • Founder effect — small group colonises new area; founders don't represent full genetic diversity
  • Gene flow — movement of individuals (and their genes) between populations
  • Immigration — organisms join a population; emigration — organisms leave a population
📝 Four mechanisms of evolution

Mutation (source of variation) · Natural selection (adaptive) · Genetic drift (random) · Gene flow (movement)

Unit 4 Complete!
Key takeaways
What you should remember from Unit 4
  • Evolution = gradual change in genetic composition of a population over generations
  • Biochemical theory (abiogenesis) is the current scientific view of the origin of life
  • Lamarckism (inheritance of acquired characters) was disproved; Darwinism (natural selection) is accepted
  • Neo-Darwinism combines Darwin's theory with genetics: genetic variability, natural selection, reproductive isolation
  • Four lines of evidence: comparative anatomy, embryology, palaeontology, biochemistry
  • Homologous structures = common ancestry; analogous structures = convergent evolution
  • Three types of selection: directional, stabilising, disruptive
  • Speciation = formation of new species; requires reproductive isolation (allopatric or sympatric)
  • Human evolution: bipedalism came before large brains; Lucy, Ardi, Selam from Ethiopia
  • Mutations are the source of all genetic variation; sickle-cell anaemia is caused by a substitution
  • Genetic drift (bottleneck, founder effect) and gene flow are additional mechanisms of evolution
  • Extinction is driven mainly by human activities — especially habitat loss
