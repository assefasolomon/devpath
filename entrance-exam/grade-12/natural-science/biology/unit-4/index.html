🌍 Definition & Theories of the Origin of Life

How did life on Earth begin? This question has fascinated humans for thousands of years. In this lesson, we define evolution and explore the major theories proposed to explain the origin of life — from divine creation to biochemical reactions in the primordial oceans.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

The origin of life is one of the most complex and controversial subjects in science. For centuries, humans have proposed different theories — some based on religion, some on observation, and some on scientific experimentation. In this lesson, you'll learn about the major theories and the evidence for and against each.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define evolution
  • Explain the theory of special creationism
  • Describe the theory of spontaneous generation and how it was disproved
  • Explain the eternity of life theory
  • Describe the cosmozoan (panspermia) theory
  • Explain the biochemical origin (abiogenesis) theory and Miller's experiment
📌
What is Evolution?
MoE Textbook §4.1, p. 150
📌 Evolution (MoE Textbook, p. 150)

Evolution is the gradual change in the genetic composition of a population over successive generations, which may be caused by meiosis, hybridisation, natural selection, or mutation. This leads to a sequence of events by which the population diverges from other populations of the same species and may lead to the origin of a new species.

Key Points About Evolution

  • Evolution is a gradual change — it happens over many generations
  • It involves changes in the genetic composition of a population
  • It can be driven by several mechanisms: mutation, natural selection, genetic drift, gene flow, meiosis, and hybridisation
  • Populations evolve — not individuals
  • Over time, evolution can lead to the origin of new species
📝 Evolution vs the origin of life

These are related but different questions:

• Origin of life — How did the first living things come into existence? (This lesson)
• Evolution — How did life diversify after it began? (Lessons 4.2–4.9)

✝️
Special Creationism
MoE Textbook §4.2.1
📌 Creationism (or Special Creationism)

Creationism is a theory claiming that the different forms of life on Earth were created by a supreme being. It explains that the formation of life on Earth may have taken place due to supernatural or divine forces.

Key Features

  • States that at some stage, some supreme being created life on Earth
  • There are many different versions of special creation, linked with different religions
  • There is considerable variation in how rigidly the theory is interpreted within a religion
  • Not linked to scientific thinking — it relies on faith and divine revelation rather than observation and experimentation
  • Fundamental differences exist between special creationism and scientific thinking
📝 Science vs religion

Science relies on observation, provable events, and empirical facts. Religion addresses morality, purpose, and meaning. The two are different kinds of inquiry — they answer different questions.

🪱
Spontaneous Generation
MoE Textbook §4.2.2
📌 Spontaneous Generation

Spontaneous generation suggests that life can evolve spontaneously from non-living objects. It was once believed that life could come from nonliving things, such as mice from corn, flies from bovine manure, maggots from rotting meat, and fish from the mud of previously dry lakes.

Historical Background

  • Aristotle (384–322 BC) was one of the earliest scholars to articulate this theory
  • Jan Baptista van Helmont (1580–1644) proposed that mice could arise from rags and wheat kernels left in an open container for 3 weeks
  • The theory persisted into the 17th century

How It Was Disproved

1. Francesco Redi (1668) — Italian scientist. Placed fresh meat in three jars: one open, one sealed, one covered with gauze. Days later, maggots appeared only in the open jar and on the gauze. He demonstrated that maggots came from fly eggs — not from the meat itself.

2. Louis Pasteur (1860s) — French scientist. Showed that broth only went sour if microorganisms were allowed to enter. Using swan-necked flasks, he demonstrated that no microorganisms appeared in sterilised broth unless they entered from outside.

The Conclusion

  • Both Redi (macro-organisms) and Pasteur (micro-organisms) showed that living things only arise from pre-existing organisms
  • This disproved the theory of spontaneous generation
  • But what about the first ever cell? It couldn't come from a pre-existing cell — because it was the first
📝 The remaining question

If life only comes from life, how did the first life come to be? This is what the biochemical origin theory tries to answer.

♾️
Eternity of Life
MoE Textbook §4.2.3
📌 Eternity of Life Theory

According to this theory, there is no beginning and no end to life on Earth. Life neither needs special creation nor does it need to be generated from non-living matter. Supporters believe life is an inherent property of the Universe and has always existed, as has the Universe.

Key Features

  • Life has always existed — no origin required
  • The Universe itself has always existed
  • Life is an inherent property of the Universe
  • Avoids the question of how life began by saying it never began
📝 Modern cosmology

Modern cosmology supports the Big Bang theory — the idea that the Universe had a beginning about 13.8 billion years ago. This makes the "eternity of life" theory difficult to reconcile with current scientific understanding.

☄️
Cosmozoan Theory
Panspermia or Spore Broth Theory
📌 Cosmozoan Theory (Panspermia)

According to the cosmozoan theory, life has reached Earth from other cosmological structures — such as meteorites — in the form of highly resistant spores. The 'protoplasm' reached the Earth in the form of spores, germs, or other simple particles from some unknown part of the universe with cosmic dust, and subsequently evolved into various forms of life.

Key Features

  • Proposed by Richter (1865)
  • Supported by Helmholtz (1884) — speculated that 'protoplasm' reached Earth with falling meteorites
  • Life forms (or the organic molecules needed for life) came from elsewhere in the Universe
  • Also called panspermia or the spore broth theory
📝 Evidence against cosmozoan theory

Living matter cannot survive the extreme cold, dryness, and ultraviolet radiation of space. So the idea that spores could travel through space and reach Earth intact is considered unlikely by most scientists.

🧪
Biochemical Origin
Abiogenesis — the current scientific view
📌 Biochemical Theory (Abiogenesis)

The biochemical theory suggests that life on Earth originated as a result of a number of biochemical reactions producing organic molecules, which associated to form cells. This is sometimes called abiogenesis — life arising from non-living matter through natural chemical processes.

Oparin-Haldane Hypothesis (1920s)

  • Aleksandr Oparin (Russian biologist, 1924) and J.B.S. Haldane (English biologist, 1929) independently proposed similar ideas
  • They proposed that common gases in the early Earth atmosphere combined to form simple organic chemicals
  • These chemicals then combined to form more complex molecules
  • The complex molecules became separated from the surrounding medium and acquired some of the characteristics of living organisms
  • They became able to absorb nutrients, grow, divide (reproduce), and so on

Miller's Spark-Discharge Experiment (1953)

Stanley Miller tested the Oparin-Haldane model in the laboratory by mixing basic gases (methane, ammonia, hydrogen, water vapour) and passing electrical sparks through them to simulate lightning. His experiment produced:

  • Simple organic compounds — amino acids, the building blocks of proteins
  • Nucleic acid bases — the building blocks of DNA and RNA
  • This provided experimental support for the Oparin-Haldane model

The First Organisms

  • The first organisms appeared about 4 billion years ago
  • They were prokaryotes — they had no true nucleus
  • They likely had RNA rather than DNA as their genetic material
  • They gave rise to three distinct lines of evolution:
    • Archaeobacteria — thermophilic sulphobacteria, methanobacteria, halophilic bacteria
    • Eubacteria — ordinary bacteria and cyanobacteria
    • Eukaryotes — protists, fungi, plants, animals

The Great Oxygenation Event

  • One great change was the shift from a reducing atmosphere to an atmosphere containing oxygen
  • This took place about 2.4 billion years ago
  • The oxygen came from photosynthesis by cyanobacteria
  • This made aerobic respiration possible — releasing far more energy than anaerobic pathways
  • More active organisms — the animals — could now evolve, perhaps 600–700 million years ago
📝 Photoautotrophs vs chemoautotrophs

Photoautotrophs use light energy to make organic molecules (e.g. cyanobacteria, plants).
Chemoautotrophs use energy from chemical reactions (often inorganic) to make organic molecules (e.g. sulphobacteria in deep-sea vents).

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 4.1
What you should remember
  • Evolution = the gradual change in the genetic composition of a population over successive generations
  • Special creationism — the belief that a supreme being created life; not scientific
  • Spontaneous generation — the belief that life arises from non-living matter; disproved by Redi and Pasteur
  • Eternity of life — the belief that life has always existed and never began
  • Cosmozoan theory (panspermia) — the belief that life came to Earth from space
  • Biochemical theory (abiogenesis) — the current scientific view; life originated through natural chemical reactions
  • Oparin-Haldane hypothesis — early Earth gases combined to form organic molecules
  • Miller's experiment (1953) produced amino acids and nucleic acid bases, supporting the theory
  • The Great Oxygenation Event (~2.4 billion years ago) — cyanobacteria photosynthesis added oxygen, enabling aerobic respiration
