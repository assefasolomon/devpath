🎲 Genetic Drift & Gene Flow

Natural selection isn't the only force that changes populations. Evolution can also happen by chance (genetic drift) and by migration (gene flow). This lesson explores how random events and movement of individuals shape the genetic makeup of populations.

📖 Reading: ~18 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Allele frequencies in a population can change due to natural selection (because some alleles are advantageous) — but they can also change due to chance alone (genetic drift), or because individuals move between populations (gene flow). These are all mechanisms of evolution.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define genetic drift and explain how it changes allele frequencies by chance
  • Describe the bottleneck effect and the founder effect
  • Define gene flow and explain the roles of immigration and emigration
  • Explain why small populations are more affected by genetic drift
  • Compare all four mechanisms of evolution: mutation, natural selection, genetic drift, gene flow
🎲
Genetic Drift
MoE Textbook §4.1.7, p. 200
📌 Genetic Drift (MoE Textbook, p. 200)

Genetic drift is a mechanism of evolution in which allele frequencies of a population change over generations due to chance events. It's change due to "sampling error" in selecting the alleles for the next generation from the gene pool of the current generation.

How Genetic Drift Works

  • Allele frequencies can change due to chance alone — not because of any advantage
  • The alleles that form the next generation's gene pool are a sample of the alleles from the current generation
  • When sampled from a population, the frequency of alleles differs slightly due to chance alone
  • Alleles can increase or decrease in frequency due to drift
  • The average expected change in allele frequency is zero — since increasing or decreasing is equally probable
  • A small percentage of alleles may continually change frequency in a single direction for several generations
  • A very few new mutant alleles can drift to fixation in this manner
📝 The coin-flip analogy

Just as flipping a fair coin can occasionally give a string of heads or tails, allele frequencies can drift in one direction for several generations purely by chance.

Small vs Large Populations

  • The variance in the rate of change of allele frequencies is greater in small populations than in large populations
  • The overall rate of genetic drift (measured in substitutions per generation) is independent of population size
  • If the mutation rate is constant, large and small populations lose alleles to drift at the same rate
  • But large populations have more alleles in the gene pool — they lose them more slowly
  • Smaller populations have fewer alleles, but these are lost more quickly

The Rabbit Example (from the textbook)

Imagine a small rabbit population with 8 brown individuals (genotype BB or Bb) and 2 white individuals (genotype bb). Initially, the frequencies of the B and b alleles are equal.

  • Suppose only 5 rabbits reproduce — purely by chance (the others die for reasons unrelated to coat colour)
  • In the surviving group, the frequency of the B allele is 0.7 and the frequency of the b allele is 0.3
  • Because the 5-rabbit sample had different allele frequencies than the population as a whole, the frequencies of B and b in the population have shifted
  • What if only 2 of the BB offspring survive and reproduce? By the third generation, the b allele could be completely lost from the population
📝 Why genetic drift matters

Genetic drift can cause random loss of alleles — including potentially beneficial ones. This is especially important in small, endangered populations, where random events can eliminate genetic diversity.

🍾
The Bottleneck Effect
An extreme form of genetic drift
📌 Bottleneck Effect

The bottleneck effect is an extreme example of genetic drift that happens when the size of a population is severely reduced. Events like natural disasters (earthquakes, floods, fires) can decimate a population — killing most individuals and leaving behind a small, random assortment of survivors.

How It Works

  • The allele frequencies prior to the disaster may be very different from those of the population after the event
  • Some alleles may be missing entirely
  • The smaller population is more susceptible to the effects of genetic drift for generations (until its numbers return to normal)
  • This can cause even more alleles to be lost
📝 The marble analogy (from the textbook)

Imagine a bottle filled with marbles, representing individuals in a population. If a bottleneck event occurs, a small, random assortment of individuals survive the event and pass through the bottleneck, while the vast majority of the population is killed off.

The genetic composition of the random survivors is now the genetic composition of the entire population. A population bottleneck yields a limited and random assortment of individuals.

Consequences

  • Reduced genetic diversity — many alleles are lost
  • The population may be less able to adapt to new environmental challenges
  • Harmful recessive alleles can become more common by chance
  • Recovery of genetic diversity can take many generations
🏝️
The Founder Effect
Another extreme form of genetic drift
📌 Founder Effect

The founder effect occurs when a small group of individuals breaks off from a larger population to establish a colony. The new colony is isolated from the original population, and the founding individuals may not represent the full genetic diversity of the original population.

Key Features

  • Alleles in the founding population may be present at different frequencies than in the original population
  • Some alleles may be missing entirely
  • The small size of the new colony means it will experience strong genetic drift for generations
  • The founder effect is similar in concept to the bottleneck effect, but it occurs via a different mechanism (colonisation rather than catastrophe)

Bottleneck vs Founder Effect

FeatureBottleneck EffectFounder Effect
TriggerA catastrophe that kills most of the populationA small group breaking off to establish a new colony
DirectionThe population shrinksThe population moves to a new area
ResultRandom survivors determine the gene poolThe founders determine the gene pool
SimilarityBoth reduce genetic diversity; both cause genetic drift for generations
📝 Example: Ellis-Van Creveld syndrome

The Ellis-Van Creveld syndrome is a form of dwarfism with extra fingers (polydactyly) that is unusually common in the Old Order Amish community of Pennsylvania. The condition traces back to a single founder who immigrated to the community in the 1700s. Because the community was small and isolated, the allele for the syndrome became more common than it would have been in a larger population — a classic example of the founder effect.

🔄
Gene Flow
MoE Textbook §4.1.8, p. 204
📌 Gene Flow (MoE Textbook, p. 204)

Gene flow — also called migration — is any movement of individuals, and/or the genetic material they carry, from one population to another.

How Gene Flow Works

  • Gene flow includes many different kinds of events — pollen being blown to a new destination, or people moving to new cities or countries
  • If gene versions are carried to a population where those gene versions previously did not exist, gene flow can be a very important source of genetic variation
  • Gene flow tends to reduce differences between populations (because alleles move between them)
  • Gene transfer is the flow of alleles from one species to another — especially common in bacteria

Immigration vs Emigration

TermMeaningEffect on population
Immigration New organisms join a population Adds new alleles; changes allele frequencies
Emigration Members of a population leave, taking their genes with them Removes alleles; changes allele frequencies
📝 Gene flow vs genetic drift

• Gene flow tends to make populations more similar to each other (alleles spread between them)
• Genetic drift tends to make populations more different from each other (random changes happen independently in each population)

⚖️
All Mechanisms of Evolution
Putting it all together

Evolution can be driven by four main mechanisms. Each changes allele frequencies in a population, but in different ways.

MechanismWhat it doesResult
Mutation Introduces new alleles into the gene pool Source of all genetic variation
Natural selection Favours alleles that increase survival and reproduction Adaptive evolution — populations become better suited to their environment
Genetic drift Random changes in allele frequencies Random evolution — no adaptation; stronger in small populations
Gene flow Movement of alleles between populations Reduces differences between populations

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 4.8
What you should remember
  • Genetic drift = changes in allele frequencies due to chance events, not natural selection
  • Genetic drift is stronger in small populations — random events can eliminate alleles completely
  • Bottleneck effect = a catastrophe kills most of the population, leaving a random assortment of survivors
  • Founder effect = a small group breaks off to establish a new colony; the founders don't represent the full genetic diversity of the original population
  • Both bottleneck and founder effects reduce genetic diversity and cause genetic drift for generations
  • Gene flow = movement of individuals (and their genes) between populations
  • Immigration = new organisms join a population; emigration = members leave a population
  • Gene flow tends to make populations more similar; genetic drift makes them more different
  • The four mechanisms of evolution: mutation, natural selection, genetic drift, gene flow
