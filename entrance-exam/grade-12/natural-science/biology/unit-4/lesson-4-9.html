⚠️ Causes of Species Extinction

Extinction is a natural part of evolution — but today, species are disappearing at a rate far faster than at any time in recent history. This lesson explores the causes of extinction, both natural and human-driven, and what can be done to protect the species we have left.

📖 Reading: ~18 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Extinction is the permanent loss of a species. Throughout Earth's history, species have gone extinct naturally — sometimes at a slow, steady rate, and sometimes in mass extinction events. But today, human activities are driving extinction at an unprecedented rate. Evidence suggests that both anthropogenic (human) effects and natural disasters play a significant role in the direct and indirect causes of species extinction.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define extinction and explain why it happens
  • Describe the anthropogenic causes of species extinction
  • Describe the natural causes of extinction
  • Identify threatened species in Ethiopia
  • Describe conservation solutions for protecting species
📌
What is Extinction?
MoE Textbook §4.1.9, p. 206
📌 Extinction

Extinction is the permanent loss of a species from Earth. It occurs when the last individual of a species dies, or when the remaining population becomes too small (or too genetically uniform) to survive and reproduce.

Background vs Mass Extinction

  • Background extinction — the normal, slow rate at which species disappear over geological time
  • Mass extinction — a rapid, large-scale loss of many species in a short period (e.g. the extinction of dinosaurs ~66 million years ago)
  • Scientists believe we may be entering a sixth mass extinction — this time driven by human activity
📝 Why extinction matters

Every species plays a role in its ecosystem — as a predator, prey, pollinator, decomposer, or competitor. When a species goes extinct, it can have cascading effects on other species and on the ecosystem as a whole. Extinction also means the loss of unique genetic material — forever.

🏭
Anthropogenic Causes
Human-driven extinction

Human activities are the dominant driver of modern extinction. The main anthropogenic causes are:

🌳
Habitat Loss

The single biggest cause. Deforestation, urbanisation, agriculture, and mining destroy the places where species live.

🏭
Pollution

Air pollution, water pollution, plastic waste, and toxic chemicals poison ecosystems.

🌡️
Climate Change

Rising temperatures, changing rainfall patterns, and extreme weather push species beyond their tolerance limits.

🎯
Overexploitation

Overhunting, overfishing, and illegal wildlife trade — including poaching for ivory, skins, and exotic pets.

🐟
Invasive Species

Species introduced from other regions can outcompete, prey on, or introduce diseases to native species.

👥
Human Population Growth

More people means more land needed for housing, food, and resources — putting pressure on natural habitats.

📝 How they interact

These threats don't act alone — they reinforce each other. For example:

• Climate change causes droughts → crops fail → people clear more forest → habitat loss increases
• Habitat loss fragments populations → genetic diversity drops → species more vulnerable to disease

🌋
Natural Causes
Extinction without humans

Extinction also happens naturally — and always has. The main natural causes are:

Catastrophic Events

  • Meteorite impacts — like the asteroid that killed the dinosaurs 66 million years ago
  • Massive volcanic eruptions — which can block sunlight, cool the planet, and disrupt food chains
  • Tsunamis and earthquakes — localised destruction of habitats

Climate Shifts

  • Ice ages — cooling climates push species towards the equator or into extinction
  • Sea level changes — flooding or draining of habitats
  • Long-term climate cycles — natural shifts that species may not adapt to

Biological Factors

  • Competition — a better-adapted species can outcompete another
  • Predation — a new predator can decimate a prey population
  • Disease — outbreaks can wipe out vulnerable populations
  • Genetic drift — especially in small populations; harmful alleles can accumulate
📝 Background vs mass extinction

Background extinction is the natural, slow loss of species that occurs continuously. Mass extinctions are rare events where a large fraction of species disappear in a short geological time. There have been five mass extinctions in Earth's history — and many scientists believe we are now in the beginning of a sixth.

🇪🇹
Ethiopia's Threatened Species
Local extinction risks

Ethiopia has many endemic species — species found nowhere else in the world. Many are under threat.

SpeciesStatusThreats
Ethiopian wolf (Canis simensis) Endangered (fewer than 500 remain) Habitat loss, disease (rabies from domestic dogs), human encroachment
Walia ibex (Capra walie) Endangered Habitat loss, poaching, restricted to Simien Mountains
Mountain nyala (Tragelaphus buxtoni) Endangered Habitat loss, hunting, restricted range
Gelada baboon (Theropithecus gelada) Least concern (but declining) Habitat loss, human settlement
Ethiopian bush crow (Zavattariornis stresemanni) Endangered Habitat loss, restricted to southern Ethiopia
📝 Why Ethiopia matters

Ethiopia is one of the world's biodiversity hotspots — a region with high species diversity and high threat levels. Protecting Ethiopia's unique species is a global priority, not just a national one. Because these species are endemic (found nowhere else), their extinction would mean permanent global loss.

🌿
Conservation Solutions
What we can do

While extinction is serious, it's not inevitable. There are many conservation actions that can help protect threatened species:

🌳
Habitat Protection

Establishing national parks, forest reserves, and wildlife sanctuaries. In Ethiopia: Simien Mountains, Bale Mountains, Awash, Omo, Gambella.

🚫
Anti-Poaching

Enforcing laws against illegal hunting and wildlife trade. Training and equipping rangers.

🌱
Reforestation

Planting native trees and restoring damaged habitats. Ethiopia's Green Legacy Initiative is a leading example.

🧬
Captive Breeding

Breeding endangered species in zoos and reserves to increase their numbers, then releasing them back into the wild.

👥
Community-Based Conservation

Involving local communities in conservation, ensuring they benefit from protecting wildlife.

📚
Education & Awareness

Teaching people about the value of biodiversity and how to protect it.

🌍
International Cooperation

Conventions like CITES (wildlife trade), CBD (biodiversity), and the Paris Agreement (climate).

🔬
Scientific Research

Monitoring populations, understanding threats, and developing effective conservation strategies.

📝 What you can do

Even as a student, you can help:

• Plant trees and support reforestation
• Reduce, reuse, and recycle to limit waste
• Learn about local species and share your knowledge
• Support conservation organisations
• Make environmentally responsible choices in daily life

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 4.9
What you should remember
  • Extinction = the permanent loss of a species from Earth
  • Background extinction happens slowly; mass extinctions are rapid, large-scale events
  • Anthropogenic causes of extinction: habitat loss, pollution, climate change, overexploitation, invasive species, human population growth
  • Natural causes of extinction: catastrophic events (meteorites, volcanoes), climate shifts, and biological factors (competition, disease)
  • The biggest cause of modern extinction is habitat loss due to human activity
  • Ethiopia has many endemic species at risk — Ethiopian wolf, walia ibex, mountain nyala, Ethiopian bush crow
  • Because these species are endemic, their extinction would mean permanent global loss
  • Conservation strategies: habitat protection, anti-poaching, reforestation, captive breeding, community-based conservation, education, international cooperation
  • Everyone can help — by planting trees, reducing waste, learning, and making environmentally responsible choices
