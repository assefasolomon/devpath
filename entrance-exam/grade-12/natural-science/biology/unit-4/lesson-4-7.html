🔬 Mutations

Mutations are the ultimate source of all genetic variation. Without mutations, evolution would have no raw material to work with. This lesson explores the types of mutations, how they change DNA, and their consequences — from harmless to deadly.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Mutations are random changes in the genetic material of an organism. They are the ultimate source of all genetic variation — the raw material on which natural selection acts. Without mutations, evolution would be impossible. But most mutations are either harmless or harmful; only a small fraction are beneficial.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define mutation and classify its types
  • Explain point mutations (substitution, addition, deletion)
  • Describe the difference between a substitution and a frameshift mutation
  • Explain how sickle-cell anaemia results from a substitution
  • Describe chromosomal mutations
  • Identify the causes of mutation
📌
What is a Mutation?
MoE Textbook §4.1.6, p. 196
📌 Mutation (MoE Textbook, p. 196)

A mutation is any spontaneous change in the genetic material of an organism. Mutations are random — they are not directed by the environment or by the needs of the organism.

Two Broad Categories

  • Large structural changes — involving whole chromosomes or parts of chromosomes (chromosomal mutations)
  • Small changes — involving only a single base (point mutations)

Why Mutations Matter

  • They are the ultimate source of all genetic variation
  • Without mutation, natural selection would have no raw material — evolution would be impossible
  • Most mutations are neutral or harmful; only a small fraction are beneficial
  • Beneficial mutations increase in frequency through natural selection
🔹
Point Mutations
Changes at a single base
📌 Point Mutation

A point mutation is a mutation in which one of the bases in the DNA sequence of a gene is altered, usually by being copied wrongly when the DNA replicates. Each point mutation involves a change to just one base — but the change to the gene can be dramatic.

Three Types of Point Mutation

🔄
Substitution

One base is replaced by a different base. Only the codon containing the mutation is changed.

➕
Addition

An extra base is inserted into the DNA sequence. This is a frameshift mutation — all codons after the insertion are altered.

➖
Deletion

A base is "missed out" during replication. This is also a frameshift mutation.

📝 Consequences

These mutations occur quite randomly when DNA is replicating. The result can be that the protein the gene should code for is:

• Not made at all, or
• A different protein is made

🔤
Substitution
Replacing one base with another

In a substitution, one base is replaced by another base. Only the codon containing the mutation is affected — the reading frame stays the same.

Example: A Single Base Change

In this example, guanine (G) replaces thymine (T) in a substitution:

Original: GAC GGG ATT GAG GAG Asp Gly Ile Glu Glu Mutated: GAC GGG ATG GAG GAG Asp Gly Met Glu Glu
  • The original triplet ATT coded for the amino acid isoleucine
  • The new triplet ATG codes for methionine
  • A different protein will be synthesised
  • One different amino acid in a protein does not always make a functional change — some substitutions are neutral
🔀
Addition and Deletion
Frameshift mutations

In a deletion mutation, a base is "missed out" during replication. In an addition mutation, an extra base is added. Both are more significant than substitutions.

📌 Frameshift Mutation

Frameshift mutations are mutations caused by the addition or deletion of a base, which changes the reading frame of the entire sequence after the point of the mutation.

Why They Are More Serious

  • They do not just alter the triplet in which the mutation occurs
  • Because there is one fewer or one extra base, the entire sequence after the point of mutation is altered
  • A totally different mRNA is produced (if one is produced at all)
  • Result: a non-functional protein or no protein at all
  • Sometimes a whole triplet is missed out or inserted — this leads to one extra or one fewer amino acid in the polypeptide chain

The Reading Frame Analogy

A helpful way to think about frameshifts — using letters to represent bases:

Original: THE MAN WAS HOT AND RAN FOR HIS HAT Delete S: THE MAN WAH OTA NDR ANF ORH ISH AT ← no longer makes sense
📝 Mis-sense coding

In genetic terms, the "gibberish" sequence after a frameshift is called mis-sense coding. The protein made from this sequence will be completely wrong — and usually non-functional.

🩸
Sickle-Cell Anaemia
A real-world example of a substitution

Sickle-cell anaemia is one of the best-known examples of a substitution mutation causing a genetic disease. It affects the haemoglobin molecule in red blood cells.

The Mutation

  • A substitution of just one base in the sixth triplet of the gene coding for one of the four polypeptides in haemoglobin
  • The triplet changes from GAG to GTG
  • This results in the amino acid valine replacing glutamate in the polypeptide chain
  • The different haemoglobin molecule formed causes the condition known as sickle-cell anaemia
Normal: Val His Leu Thr Pro Glu Glu GTG CAC CTC ACT CCA GAG GAG Sickle: Val His Leu Thr Pro Val Glu GTG CAC CTC ACT CCA GTG GAG

Consequences

  • The abnormal haemoglobin (HbS) causes red blood cells to become crescent (sickle) shaped when oxygen levels are low
  • Sickle-shaped cells are rigid and sticky, blocking blood vessels
  • This causes pain, organ damage, and anaemia
  • Sickle-cell trait provides resistance to malaria — which is why the allele is common in malaria-endemic regions like parts of Africa
📝 A single base matters

Sickle-cell anaemia is a powerful demonstration of how a single base change in DNA can have life-altering consequences. One letter changed from A to T — and the whole shape of the haemoglobin molecule changes.

🧬
Chromosomal Mutations
Larger-scale changes
📌 Chromosomal Mutation

Chromosomal mutations occur when there is any change in the arrangement or structure of the chromosomes. They occur most often during meiosis at crossing over in prophase I.

Types of Chromosomal Mutation

TypeWhat happens
DuplicationA section of a chromosome is duplicated, so it appears twice
DeletionA section of a chromosome is lost
InversionA section of a chromosome is reversed end-to-end
TranslocationA section of one chromosome breaks off and attaches to another chromosome

Consequences

  • They are much bigger events than point mutations
  • They usually result in the death of a cell
  • They may also affect the whole organism
  • If essential parts of the DNA are affected, a foetus may be aborted
⚠️
Causes of Mutation
What triggers DNA changes

Mutations can occur spontaneously, or they can be caused by external factors called mutagens.

Spontaneous Causes

  • Errors during DNA replication — rare mistakes by DNA polymerase
  • Errors during meiosis — especially during crossing over in prophase I

Induced Causes (Mutagens)

Type of mutagenExamples
Physical mutagensUV radiation, X-rays, gamma rays, radioactivity
Chemical mutagensTobacco smoke, certain industrial chemicals, some pesticides
Biological mutagensSome viruses and transposons ("jumping genes")
📝 Most mutations are silent

Most mutations are neutral — they occur in non-coding DNA or don't change the amino acid sequence of the protein. Only a small fraction have a phenotypic effect, and of those, most are harmful. Beneficial mutations are rare — but they are the ones that drive evolution.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 4.7
What you should remember
  • A mutation is any spontaneous change in the genetic material of an organism
  • Mutations are the ultimate source of all genetic variation
  • Two categories: point mutations (single base) and chromosomal mutations (larger-scale changes)
  • Three types of point mutation: substitution (one base replaced), addition (extra base), deletion (missing base)
  • Substitutions alter only one codon; additions and deletions cause frameshifts that alter everything after the mutation
  • Sickle-cell anaemia is caused by a substitution changing GAG → GTG, causing valine to replace glutamate in haemoglobin
  • Chromosomal mutations include duplication, deletion, inversion, and translocation
  • Causes: spontaneous errors in DNA replication/meiosis, or mutagens like UV radiation, X-rays, and certain chemicals
