🧑 Human Evolution

Who are we and where have we come from? Ethiopia is at the heart of the story of human evolution — the "cradle of mankind." This lesson explores our primate ancestry, the fossils that changed everything, and the journey of Homo sapiens out of Africa.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

You will often hear people say "we evolved from monkeys" or "we evolved from chimpanzees." None of these statements is accurate. There has been a line of evolution for millions of years that has given rise to old world monkeys, new world monkeys, the great apes, and the different species of humans that have lived. But we are Homo sapiens — the latest of several humans to live on the planet.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe the primate evolutionary tree
  • Identify the two distinctive features of modern humans
  • Describe the significance of Lucy (Dinkinesh) and Ardi
  • Explain how brain size changed during human evolution
  • Describe the Out of Africa migration of Homo sapiens
  • Understand Ethiopia's role in the story of human evolution
🌳
The Primate Evolutionary Tree
Our place in the primate family

Modern humans (Homo sapiens) belong to the primate order — a group that includes lemurs, tarsiers, monkeys, and apes. But we are not descended from any living primate. Instead, we share a common ancestor with them.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Genus (plural: genera)A group of closely related species
HomininAny member of the genus Homo. This includes modern humans (Homo sapiens), Neanderthals, and Homo erectus
HominidA group of species that includes all the species belonging to the genus Homo along with other species such as Ardipithecus ramidus (Ardi) and Australopithecus afarensis (Lucy)

The Line of Evolution

The evolutionary tree of primates has been branching for tens of millions of years:

  • Around 63 million years ago — the first primates appeared
  • Around 40 million years ago — New World and Old World monkeys diverged
  • Around 18 million years ago — the great apes (including orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees, and humans) diverged from other primates
  • Around 8 million years ago — the common ancestor of gorillas, chimpanzees, and humans lived
  • Around 6 million years ago — the common ancestor of chimpanzees and humans lived
📝 What the tree shows

Branching points in the evolutionary tree represent ancestors. At these points, it is assumed that an ancestral type became divided into at least two populations that subsequently evolved along different lines. For example, humans and chimpanzees both evolved from a common ancestor that lived about 6 million years ago.

⭐
Two Distinctive Features of Modern Humans
What sets us apart from other primates

We have two features in particular that distinguish us from other primates:

🧠
Very Large Brain

Modern human brains are much larger, relative to body size, than those of other primates. This enables complex thought, language, culture, and tool use.

🚶
Bipedalism

The ability to truly walk on just two legs. Bipedalism freed our hands for tool use and allowed us to see over tall grass and travel long distances efficiently.

Which Came First?

There was a lot of debate amongst biologists as to which of these features — large brain or bipedalism — came first in human evolution. The fossil record has now answered this question:

  • The fossil Lucy showed that bipedalism evolved before large brains
  • The fossil Ardi showed that the common ancestor of humans and chimpanzees could not have resembled a chimpanzee, because chimpanzees are not truly bipedal
📝 The answer is clear

Bipedalism came before big brains. Our ancestors stood up on two legs long before their brains began to enlarge significantly.

🦴
Lucy (Dinkinesh)
The fossil that changed everything

Dinkinesh (Lucy) and Ardi are important fossils in explaining the evolution of modern humans and chimpanzees from a common ancestor.

Discovery and Significance

  • Discovered by Donald Johanson and Tom Gray in 1974 at Hadar, Ethiopia
  • Dated to about 3.2 million years ago
  • An adult female of about 25 years old
  • Belonged to the species Australopithecus afarensis
  • Her skeleton was about 40% complete — an unusually high proportion for a fossil skeleton
  • She was about 107 cm (42 inches) tall and about 28 kg (62 lbs) in weight

Key Findings from Lucy

  • Her pelvis, femur (upper leg bone), and tibia show that she was bipedal — she could walk upright on two legs
  • There is also evidence that she was partly arboreal (tree-dwelling)
  • The proportions of her humerus and femur were mid-way between those of modern humans and chimpanzees
  • Her brain was about the same size as that of a chimpanzee
📝 Why Lucy was so important

At the time of her discovery, Lucy represented one of the oldest fossil hominins. Crucially, she settled a major scientific debate:

Her brain was about the same size as that of a chimpanzee, but her skeleton showed clear bipedalism. This proved that bipedalism evolved before large brains — a major insight in the history of human evolution.

🦴
Ardi
Our oldest near-ancestor

Discovery

  • First discovered in 1992 in the Afar Desert in Ethiopia
  • It took 17 years for the full analysis to be completed
  • Research papers were finally published in 2009, giving Ardi a unique position in human evolution
  • Ardi was 1.2 million years older than Lucy
  • She was female and belonged to the species Ardipithecus ramidus

Key Findings from Ardi

  • Ardi was bipedal — she could walk upright on two legs
  • At 4.4 million years old, Ardi is the nearest fossil to the 'common ancestor' of humans and chimpanzees that has so far been found
  • There were signs of being adapted for both bipedal walking and arboreal life
📝 Why Ardi matters

Ardi's discovery finally proved that the common ancestor of humans and chimpanzees could not have resembled a chimpanzee — because chimpanzees are not truly bipedal. If the common ancestor had been like a chimp, it would not have been bipedal. Since Ardi was bipedal, the common ancestor must have been bipedal too. This fundamentally changed our understanding of human ancestry.

🧠
Brain Evolution
How brain size changed over time

During the course of human evolution, the brain has gotten bigger. Studies on comparative anatomy of fossils revealed that the cranial capacity has increased with each new hominid species evolved.

Brain Size as a Proportion of Body Mass

SpeciesBrain as % of body mass
Australopithecus species0.7% to 1.0%
Modern humans (Homo sapiens)1.8% to 2.3%

Energy Requirement

  • The brain of Homo sapiens uses 25% of the resting energy requirement
  • The brain of the great apes uses only 8%
  • Our large brain is "expensive" to maintain — but the benefits are enormous

Advantages of a Large Brain

A larger brain allows humans to:

  • Run faster and in a more upright posture
  • Plan in advance to avoid attack
  • Develop and use tools and weapons
  • Develop complex language, culture, and social organisation
📝 A bigger brain is not enough

These abilities depend on other physical adaptations such as longer legs, more nimble fingers, and a straighter spine. Without the larger brain to coordinate the activities, the physical changes would not confer the same advantage. Brain and body evolved together.

🌍
Out of Africa Migration
How modern humans colonised the world

Homo sapiens (modern humans) first appeared in Africa and have since migrated to all other parts of the world. The migration began near Ethiopia.

The Migration Route

  • Homo sapiens first appeared in Africa about 200,000–300,000 years ago
  • A major migration out of Africa occurred about 60,000–70,000 years ago
  • Humans spread through the Middle East, then across Asia
  • They reached Australia about 50,000 years ago
  • They reached Europe about 45,000 years ago
  • They crossed into the Americas about 15,000–20,000 years ago

Adaptation to Different Environments

As humans moved from Africa into different areas of the world, they encountered different environments. Different selection pressures in these environments resulted in different human populations evolving along different lines. For example, in colder climates:

  • Shorter, squatter body shape — this reduces the surface-area-to-volume ratio and reduces heat loss by radiation
  • Increased layer of adipose tissue under the skin to act as an insulator
  • Increased hairiness — reduces heat loss by convection
📝 Are we still evolving?

Humans have been evolving into different 'races' for thousands of years. The classification of these races is difficult, and there is some disagreement about their exact nature. But with modern transportation and globalisation, human populations are now mixing more than ever — which means they are less likely to evolve into separate species.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 4.6
What you should remember
  • Homo sapiens is the species that all humans alive today belong to
  • We did not evolve from chimpanzees — we share a common ancestor that lived about 6 million years ago
  • Two distinctive features of modern humans: a very large brain and true bipedalism
  • Bipedalism evolved before large brains — proven by the fossil Lucy
  • Lucy (Dinkinesh) — discovered in 1974 at Hadar, Ethiopia, by Donald Johanson and Tom Gray; 3.2 million years old; Australopithecus afarensis
  • Ardi — discovered in 1992 in the Afar Desert, Ethiopia; 4.4 million years old; Ardipithecus ramidus; proved the common ancestor was not chimp-like
  • Brain size increased as hominids evolved — from 0.7–1.0% of body mass in Australopithecus to 1.8–2.3% in modern humans
  • Out of Africa: Homo sapiens first appeared in Africa and migrated out about 60,000–70,000 years ago, beginning near Ethiopia
  • Humans adapted to colder climates with shorter bodies, more fat, and increased hairiness
