⚗️ The Endocrine System

The endocrine system is the body's other control network — slower than the nervous system but with longer-lasting effects. It uses chemicals called hormones to control growth, metabolism, reproduction, and much more. This lesson explores the major endocrine glands and their hormones.

📖 Reading: ~28 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

The endocrine system interacts with the nervous system to coordinate and integrate body activities by means of hormones. While the nervous system uses electrical impulses for rapid responses, the endocrine system uses chemical messengers (hormones) that travel in the blood and act more slowly but with longer-lasting effects.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define glands and hormones
  • Explain the difference between endocrine and exocrine glands
  • Describe protein hormones and steroid hormones and how they act
  • Identify the location and function of major endocrine glands: hypothalamus, pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid, adrenal, pancreas, gonads, pineal
  • Describe the hormones each gland produces
  • Understand the effects of hypo- and hyper-secretion
📌
Glands & Hormones
MoE Textbook §5.6.1
📌 Glands

Glands are structures which produce hormones and other useful substances. The endocrine glands that produce hormones have no ducts, so they are sometimes known as ductless glands. They secrete hormones directly into the blood.

📌 Hormones

Hormones are chemicals produced in one part of an organism which produce specific effects on a different part of the organism. They are carried from glands to the body parts throughout the bloodstream.

Endocrine vs Exocrine Glands

FeatureEndocrine GlandsExocrine Glands
DuctsNo ducts (ductless)Have ducts
SecretionDirectly into the bloodOnto an epithelial surface via ducts
ProductsHormonesEnzymes, sweat, saliva, milk, etc.
TargetSpecific target cells with receptorsDirect site of action
ExamplesPituitary, thyroid, adrenal, pancreas (endocrine part)Salivary, sweat, sebaceous, lacrimal, mammary
📝 Some glands are both!

The liver and pancreas are both exocrine AND endocrine glands. They are exocrine because they secrete products (bile and pancreatic juice) into the gastrointestinal tract through ducts, and endocrine because they secrete other substances (like insulin and glucagon) directly into the bloodstream.

🔬
Hormone Mechanisms
How hormones work

Hormones act in different ways depending on their chemical nature. There are two main types:

Protein Hormones

  • Transported in the blood or lymph without a transporter
  • When the hormone arrives at the target cell, it binds with a specific receptor embedded in the cell membrane of the target cell
  • The binding activates a series of chemical reactions ("cascade reactions") in the cytoplasm
  • The product of these reactions is a substance known as the "secondary messenger" (usually cyclic adenosine monophosphate or cAMP)
  • The secondary messenger acts on behalf of the protein hormone, causing a potent effect in the target cell
  • Since protein hormones never diffuse to the DNA of target cells, no new proteins or enzymes are made at the end

Steroid Hormones

  • Bind to a protein transporter during transport in blood or lymph
  • When they arrive at the target cells, the protein transporter (being fat-insoluble) is repelled by the cell membrane
  • The steroid hormone (being fat-soluble) diffuses into the cytoplasm of target cell
  • Then diffuses across the nuclear envelope and enters the nucleus of target cell
  • Binds to a specific receptor located on a particular gene of the target cell's DNA
  • This binding alters the genetic information within that gene, resulting in a new messenger RNA (mRNA) being produced after transcription
  • The new mRNA is translated into a new protein or enzyme in the cytoplasm
  • The new protein or enzyme causes a specific effect to occur within the target cells
📝 Key difference

Protein hormones — act via secondary messengers; do NOT enter the nucleus; do NOT make new proteins
Steroid hormones — enter the nucleus; DO make new proteins by altering gene expression

🧠
The Hypothalamus
The link between nervous and endocrine systems

The hypothalamus connects the nervous system to the endocrine system. It receives and processes signals from other brain regions and pathways, where the hypothalamus translates them into hormones — the chemical messengers of the endocrine system.

Key Functions

  • Hormones flow to the pituitary gland via the infundibulum
  • Some hormones are stored in the pituitary for later release
  • Others spur the pituitary to secrete its own hormones
  • The hypothalamus controls the other endocrine glands and regulates all the major internal functions
🎯
The Pituitary Gland
The master gland
📌 The Pituitary Gland

The pituitary gland, found in the brain and about the size of a pea, is sometimes described as the controller of the endocrine orchestra (master gland). The hormones made in this tiny gland control the secretion of many other hormones. Because of its position in the brain, it is also involved in the co-ordination between the nervous and hormonal systems of control.

Two Lobes

  • Anterior lobe (adenohypophysis) — about 3 times larger than the posterior lobe. Under hormonal control by the hypothalamus (blood vessels transport "releasing hormones" to the anterior lobe). Contains 5 types of glandular cells that produce different types of hormones.
  • Posterior lobe (neurohypophysis) — under nervous control by the hypothalamus (nerve fibers innervate the posterior lobe). Does NOT produce hormones — it only releases hormones made by the hypothalamus.
📝 Anterior vs posterior

Anterior pituitary — produces its own hormones
Posterior pituitary — stores and releases hormones made by the hypothalamus

Thyroid & Parathyroid Glands
Metabolism and calcium regulation

The Thyroid Gland

  • A small, butterfly-shaped gland located inferior to the larynx (voice box) and attached to the trachea
  • Divided into two lateral lobes
  • Thyroid follicles utilize iodine and synthesize thyroglobulin (TGB) to be stored in the colloids
  • Upon stimulation of TSH, TGB is converted into two hormones:
    • Triiodothyronine (T3)
    • Thyroxine (T4)
  • These hormones promote normal metabolism
  • The thyroid also secretes calcitonin to lower blood calcium and phosphate levels

Thyroid Disorders

ConditionCauseEffect
HypothyroidismUnder-secretion of thyroid hormonesGoiter, cretinism, myxedema
HyperthyroidismOver-secretion of thyroid hormonesGraves' disease

The Parathyroid Glands

  • Four oval-shaped glands embedded in the posterior surface of the thyroid gland
  • Each is about the size of a grain of rice (about 3–5 mm in diameter and 30–60 mg in weight)
  • Release parathyroid hormone (PTH)
  • PTH controls the calcium levels in the blood
  • Bones, kidneys, and small intestine respond to PTH by increasing the calcium levels in the blood
  • Calcium is very important for our bodies — especially for muscle and nerve function

Parathyroid Disorders

  • Hypo-secretion → tetany (muscle spasms)
  • Hyper-secretion → osteitis fibrosa cystica (bone weakening)
⚡
The Adrenal Glands
Fight-or-flight and metabolism

Adrenal glands, also known as suprarenal glands, are small, triangular-shaped glands located on the top of both kidneys. Adrenal glands are composed of two parts — the cortex and the medulla — which are responsible for the production of different hormones.

Adrenal Cortex

  • The outer portion of the adrenal gland, attached to the superior surface of the kidney
  • Divided into 3 regions (from outside to inside):
    • Zona glomerulosa
    • Zona fasciculata
    • Zona reticularis
  • Secretes over 30 steroid-based substances and several steroid hormones — all crucial for normal homeostasis

Adrenal Medulla

  • The inner portion of the adrenal gland
  • Made of modified nerve tissue that is under direct regulation of sympathetic nerves of the autonomic nervous system
  • Contains glandular cells called chromaffin cells
  • Secretes two closely related hormones:
    • Epinephrine (adrenaline)
    • Norepinephrine (noradrenaline)
📝 The hormone of "fight or flight"

Adrenaline is a well-known hormone produced by your adrenal glands. It is the hormone of "fight or flight." If you are stressed, angry, excited, or frightened, your adrenal glands will secrete lots of adrenaline.

Carried rapidly around in your blood, adrenaline affects many different organs — from the pupils of your eyes (it dilates them) to the beating of your heart (it speeds it up). Adrenaline basically prepares your body for action, so you can run fast to escape or fight successfully if you need to.

The Pancreas
Blood glucose regulation

Pancreas is a small pink organ found below the stomach. It is both exocrine and endocrine in physiology.

Exocrine vs Endocrine Functions

  • Exocrine (99% of mass) — composed of cells called acini which secrete digestive enzymes and fluids into the small intestine through the pancreatic ducts
  • Endocrine (1% of mass) — little groups of cells called islets of Langerhans (or pancreatic islets) that secrete hormones to regulate blood glucose level

Hormones of the Pancreas

Cell typeHormoneEffect on blood glucose
Alpha cells (α)GlucagonRaises blood glucose level
Beta cells (β)InsulinLowers blood glucose level
Delta cells (δ)Somatostatin (GHIH)Regulates carbohydrate metabolism by inhibiting secretion of glucagon

Pancreatic Disorders

  • Hypo-secretion of insulin → diabetes mellitus (high blood glucose)
  • Hyper-secretion of insulin → hyperinsulinism (low blood glucose)
📝 Diabetes — the two main types

Type 1 diabetes — the body cannot produce insulin (autoimmune destruction of beta cells)
Type 2 diabetes — the body produces insulin but cells don't respond to it properly (insulin resistance)
Both lead to high blood glucose levels if untreated.

Gonads & Pineal Gland
Reproductive hormones and daily rhythms

The Gonads

The gonads are the endocrine glands that produce the sex hormones. These are the testes in boys and ovaries in girls, which become active at the time of puberty.

The Ovaries (Female)

  • Two walnut-sized organs found low in the abdomen on either side of the uterus
  • Produce eggs and hormones
  • Girls often go into puberty slightly earlier than boys — between the ages of 8 and 14
  • Puberty in girls is controlled by hormones from the pituitary gland in the brain and from the ovaries themselves
  • FSH from the brain stimulates the ovaries to become active and start producing oestrogen
  • As oestrogen levels rise, all kinds of changes are triggered and the female secondary sexual characteristics develop

The Testes (Male)

  • The testis is the male sex organ that also serves as an endocrine gland
  • Contains interstitial cells (Leydig's cells) that secrete testosterone
  • Testosterone develops male secondary sexual characteristics
  • Puberty in boys usually begins between the ages of 9 and 15
  • Pituitary gland increases production of FSH, which stimulates the testes to begin developing and producing testosterone
  • Rising levels of testosterone trigger the changes that affect the body during puberty

The Pineal Gland

  • Pine cone shaped, located deep in the cerebrum
  • Secretes melatonin
  • Regulates circadian rhythms — necessary to keep track of day/night cycles, sleep/wake rhythm, and menstrual and ovarian cycles

The Thymus Gland

  • A diminishing gland (over time) located between the lungs
  • Secretes a group of hormones such as thymosin
  • Affects the production and maturation of lymphocytes in body defenses

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 5.6
What you should remember
  • Hormones are chemicals produced in one part of the body that affect another part; glands produce them
  • Endocrine glands are ductless and secrete hormones into the blood; exocrine glands have ducts
  • Protein hormones act via secondary messengers; steroid hormones enter the nucleus and alter gene expression
  • The hypothalamus links the nervous and endocrine systems
  • The pituitary gland is the "master gland" — has anterior and posterior lobes
  • Thyroid produces thyroxine (T4) and T3 (metabolism) and calcitonin (lowers blood calcium)
  • Parathyroid produces PTH which raises blood calcium
  • Adrenal cortex produces steroid hormones; adrenal medulla produces adrenaline (fight or flight)
  • Pancreas produces insulin (lowers glucose) and glucagon (raises glucose)
  • Gonads (ovaries and testes) produce sex hormones — oestrogen and testosterone
  • Pineal gland produces melatonin, regulating circadian rhythms
