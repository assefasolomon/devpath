🏠 Homeostasis

The human body works best when its internal conditions stay within narrow limits — temperature around 37 °C, blood pH around 7.4, blood glucose within a tight range. This constant state of balance is called homeostasis. This lesson explores how the body maintains it.

📖 Reading: ~25 min

Introduction
What you will learn

Homeostasis is the body's ability to maintain a stable internal environment despite changes in the external environment. It's controlled mainly by the nervous and endocrine systems, and it's essential for life — even small deviations from normal can be dangerous.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define homeostasis
  • Describe the structure and function of the human kidney
  • Explain the role of the nephron in urine production
  • Describe thermoregulation (body temperature control)
  • Describe osmoregulation (water and salt balance)
  • Explain chemical regulation (blood glucose, pH, and CO₂)

What is Homeostasis?
MoE Textbook §5.8
📌 Homeostasis

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment in the body despite changes in the external environment. It involves keeping variables like body temperature, blood glucose, water content, and pH within narrow limits.

Key Mechanisms

  • Controlled mainly by the nervous and endocrine systems
  • Uses negative feedback — the body detects a change and reverses it
  • Involves multiple organs: kidney, liver, skin, lungs, brain

Four Key Types of Homeostasis

🌡️
Thermoregulation

Maintaining a constant body temperature around 37 °C. Human beings are homeotherms.

💧
Osmoregulation

Balancing water and salt content in the body. Controlled mainly by the kidneys and ADH.

🍬
Chemical Regulation

Maintaining blood glucose, pH, and CO₂ levels. Involves the liver, pancreas, and lungs.

🚰
Excretion

Removing waste products like urea and excess salts. Primarily done by the kidneys.

🫘
The Kidney
MoE Textbook §5.8.1
📌 The Kidneys

The kidneys are a pair of bean-shaped organs just above the waist. They are important organs with many functions in your body, including producing hormones, absorbing minerals, and filtering blood and producing urine.

Internal Structure — Three Regions

  • Renal cortex — the outer region. Contains the nephrons, which are the functional units of the kidney.
  • Renal medulla — the middle region
  • Renal pelvis — the expanded end of the ureter; collects urine and leads to the ureter
  • Ureters — urine-bearing tubes that exit the kidney and empty into the urinary bladder

How the Kidney Works

  • Blood flows into the kidney along the renal artery
  • The blood is filtered — fluid containing water, salt, urea, glucose, and many other substances is forced out into the kidney tubules
  • Everything the body needs is reabsorbed — including all of the sugar and the mineral ions needed by the body
  • The amount of water reabsorbed depends on the needs of the body
  • The waste product urea, excess ions, and unwanted water are released as urine
  • Each kidney has a very rich blood supply and is made up of millions of tiny microscopic tubules (nephrons) where all the filtering and reabsorption takes place
📝 The kidneys do more than filter

Beyond filtering blood, the kidneys also:

• Produce hormones (e.g. erythropoietin, which stimulates red blood cell production)
• Regulate blood pressure
• Maintain blood pH
• Balance electrolytes (sodium, potassium, calcium)

🔬
The Nephron
The functional unit of the kidney

Each kidney contains about a million nephrons. Each nephron is a microscopic tubule with several regions, each playing a role in the production of urine.

Part of nephronFunction
Bowman's capsuleThe site of ultrafiltration of the blood. The blood vessel feeding into the capsule is wider than the vessel leaving, which means the blood in the capillaries is under a lot of pressure. Several layers of cells act as a filter, so blood cells and large blood proteins cannot leave the blood vessels. However, water, salt, glucose, urea, and many other substances are forced out into the start of the tubule.
GlomerulusThe knot of blood vessels in the Bowman's capsule where the pressure builds up so that ultrafiltration occurs. The volume of the blood leaving the glomerulus is about 15% less than the blood coming in — a measure of the liquid which has moved into the capsule as a result of ultrafiltration.
First coiled (convoluted) tubuleLiquid entering this tubule is known as the glomerular filtrate. This is where much of the reabsorption takes place. All of the glucose is actively taken back into the blood along with around 67% of the sodium ions and around 80% of the water. It has many microvilli to increase the surface area for absorption.
Loop of HenléThe part of a kidney where the urine is concentrated and more water is conserved.
Second coiled (convoluted) tubuleWhere the main water balancing is done. If the body is short of water, more is reabsorbed into the blood in this tubule under the influence of anti-diuretic hormone (ADH). Ammonium ions and some drugs are also secreted from the blood into this tubule to get rid of them.
Collecting ductWhere the liquid (essentially urine) is collected. It contains about 1% of the original water, with no glucose at all. The level of salt in the urine will depend on the amount of salt in your diet and the water content of the urine. There is also a much higher concentration of urea (about 60 times more) in the urine than in the blood.
📝 Ultrafiltration

Ultrafiltration is filtration on a very small scale — the process that occurs in the Bowman's capsule. Because the blood vessel feeding the glomerulus is wider than the one leaving it, pressure builds up and forces small molecules (water, glucose, salts, urea) out of the blood and into the capsule.

The Bladder

  • Urine is formed constantly in the kidneys as it drips down to collect in the bladder
  • The bladder is a muscular sac which can hold between 600 and 800 cm³ of urine
  • However, we usually empty it when it contains only about 150–300 cm³

Astonishing numbers

  • On an average day, the kidneys produce around 180 litres of liquid filtered out of the blood in the glomerulus (glomerular filtrate)
  • But only about 1.5 litres of urine is produced
  • So more than 99% of the liquid filtered out of the blood is eventually returned to it
💧
ADH & Water Balance
The feedback mechanism

The amount of water lost from the kidney in the urine is controlled by a sensitive feedback mechanism involving the hormone anti-diuretic hormone (ADH).

When the Body Is Short of Water

1. The water content of the blood is too low (so the salt concentration of blood increases).
2. Special sense organs known as osmoreceptors in the brain detect this.
3. They stimulate the pituitary gland in the brain to release ADH into the blood.
4. This hormone affects the second coiled tubules of the kidneys, making them more permeable — so more water is reabsorbed back into the blood.
5. This means less water is left in the kidney tubules and so a more concentrated urine is formed.
6. At the same time, the amount of water in the blood increases so the concentration of salts in the blood returns to normal.

When the Body Has Too Much Water

  • If the water content of the blood is too high, the pituitary gland releases much less ADH into the blood
  • The kidney then reabsorbs less water back into the blood
  • This produces a large volume of dilute urine
  • Water is effectively lost from the blood, and the concentration of salts returns to normal
📝 Negative feedback

This system of osmoregulation is an example of negative feedback:

• As the water concentration of the blood falls, the level of ADH produced rises
• As the water concentration of the blood rises, the level of ADH released falls

The response always counteracts the change — keeping the system stable.

🌡️
Thermoregulation
MoE Textbook §5.8.2

It is vitally important that wherever we go and whatever we do, our body temperature is maintained at around 37 °C — the temperature at which our enzymes work best.

📌 Homeotherms

Homeotherms are organisms that maintain a constant internal body temperature regardless of their environment. Human beings are good examples. The temperature deep inside the body — the internal or core body temperature — must be kept stable. (Skin temperature can vary enormously without causing harm.)

How the Body Regulates Temperature

  • Sweating — when the body gets too hot, sweat glands secrete sweat. As it evaporates, it cools the body.
  • Shivering — when the body gets too cold, muscles contract and relax rapidly, generating heat.
  • Vasodilation — blood vessels near the skin widen when hot, bringing more blood to the surface to release heat.
  • Vasoconstriction — blood vessels narrow when cold, keeping blood away from the surface to conserve heat.
  • Hair and adipose tissue — insulation to reduce heat loss
📝 The role of the liver

Around 500 different reactions take place in the liver at any time. For many years, it has been believed that as a result of all these reactions the liver generates a lot of heat, which is then spread around the body by the bloodstream. This is another way the body maintains its core temperature.

💦
Osmoregulation
MoE Textbook §5.8.3

Osmoregulation is the maintenance of the balance of water and salts in the body. If the concentration of the body fluids changes, water will move into or out of the cells by osmosis and they could be damaged or destroyed.

How We Gain and Lose Water

  • Gain water: when we drink and eat
  • Lose water: constantly from the lungs when we breathe out (water evaporates into the air in the lungs and is breathed out)
  • Lose water: whenever we exercise or get hot, we sweat and lose more water
  • Balance maintained by: the kidneys, which remove any excess water as urine

The Kidney's Role in Osmoregulation

  • If we are short of water, we produce very little urine and most water is saved for use in the body
  • If we have too much water, our kidneys produce lots of urine to get rid of the excess
  • The ion concentration of the body (particularly ordinary salt) is also important
  • Some ions are lost via our skin when we sweat
  • Excess mineral ions are removed by the kidneys and lost in the urine
📝 Why the balance matters

The balance of water and salts in the body is very important because of the osmotic impact on the cells. If the balance is wrong, water will move into or out of cells, causing them to swell or shrink. The kidneys are vitally important in two aspects of homeostasis — both in excretion and in osmoregulation.

⚗️
Chemical Regulation
MoE Textbook §5.8.4

The Role of the Liver

The liver plays a vital role in maintaining a constant internal environment. It is the largest individual organ in the body, making up around 5% of body mass. The liver cells are very active in carrying out a wide range of functions, many of which help to maintain a constant internal environment.

  • It controls the sugar levels in the body (through stored glycogen in the liver itself)
  • It controls and balances the fats you eat and the cholesterol levels in the blood
  • It is an important organ where protein metabolism takes place
  • It breaks down excess amino acids and forms urea (through a process called deamination)
  • It carries out the breakdown of worn-out red blood cells — especially the red pigment haemoglobin
  • It is vital for the formation of bile, made in the liver and stored in the gallbladder
  • It controls toxins — the liver breaks down most of the poisons you take into the body, including alcohol
  • It is used to control temperature — the many reactions in the liver generate a lot of heat
📌 Deamination

Deamination is the process of removing the amino group from excess amino acids. If you eat too much protein, your body cannot store the excess amino acids or simply convert protein to fat. Instead, the amino acids are broken down in the liver. The amino (nitrogen-containing) part of the amino acid molecule is removed and converted into ammonia and then urea in the liver. The rest of the amino acid can be used in cellular respiration or converted to fat for storage.

The Role of the Respiratory System

The regulation of tissue oxygenation is another example of chemical regulation in the body. The respiratory chemoreceptors work by sensing the pH levels of their environment through the concentration of hydrogen ions.

Because most carbon dioxide is converted to carbonic acid (and bicarbonate) in the bloodstream, chemoreceptors are able to use blood pH as a way to measure the carbon dioxide levels of the bloodstream.

Two Types of Chemoreceptors

TypeLocation and function
Central chemoreceptorsLocated on the ventrolateral surface of the medulla oblongata. Detect changes in the pH of spinal fluid. Can be desensitized over time from chronic hypoxia (oxygen deficiency) and increased carbon dioxide.
Peripheral chemoreceptorsInclude the aortic body (which detects changes in blood oxygen and carbon dioxide, but not pH) and the carotid body (which detects all three). They do not desensitize, but have less impact on the respiratory rate compared to the central chemoreceptors.
📝 The body's need for balance

The respiratory system must try to maintain constant levels of O₂, CO₂, and H⁺ in the arterial blood, which then ensures relatively constant levels of these important substances in the interstitial fluid.

• For O₂, one needs an adequate supply to meet cellular metabolic requirements
• For CO₂ and H⁺, one needs to maintain the acid–base status of the body's cells

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 5.8
What you should remember
  • Homeostasis = maintenance of a stable internal environment despite external changes
  • Controlled mainly by the nervous and endocrine systems using negative feedback
  • The kidney has three regions: cortex, medulla, and pelvis; each contains millions of nephrons
  • The nephron includes: Bowman's capsule, glomerulus, first coiled tubule, loop of Henlé, second coiled tubule, collecting duct
  • Ultrafiltration occurs in the Bowman's capsule — small molecules are forced out of blood under pressure
  • Reabsorption occurs mainly in the first coiled tubule — all glucose, ~80% water, ~67% sodium ions are reabsorbed
  • ADH controls water reabsorption in the second coiled tubule — more ADH = more water reabsorbed (concentrated urine)
  • Thermoregulation maintains body temperature around 37 °C via sweating, shivering, vasodilation, and vasoconstriction
  • Osmoregulation balances water and salts; controlled by kidneys and ADH
  • Chemical regulation — the liver controls glucose, fats, proteins (via deamination), and toxins; chemoreceptors detect pH and CO₂ levels
