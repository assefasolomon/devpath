🧠 The Nervous System

The nervous system is the body's control centre — a highly complex network that coordinates rapid responses to everything we encounter. This lesson introduces its structure, its two main divisions, and the reflexes that keep us safe.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

All living organisms need some level of awareness of their surroundings so they can avoid danger, find food, and, in some cases, find a mate. In large and complex organisms like a human being, it's also very important that the different systems within the body are coordinated and work together. In human beings, the nervous system is the highly complex system that provides rapid, coordinated responses to the situations we meet in our lives.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define the nervous system
  • Identify the two main parts — CNS and PNS
  • Define reflex action and state its types
  • Distinguish between voluntary and reflex actions
  • Describe the reflex arc
  • Give examples of common reflexes
📌
What is the Nervous System?
MoE Textbook §5.1, p. 215
📌 The Nervous System (MoE Textbook, p. 215)

The nervous system is a highly complex system that provides rapid, coordinated responses to the situations we meet in our lives. It allows us to react to our surroundings and coordinate our behaviour.

Why Do We Need a Nervous System?

  • To be aware of our surroundings — so we can avoid danger and find food
  • To coordinate and control responses to changes in the environment
  • To make sure the different systems within our body work together
  • To pick up all sorts of changes in the environment and respond to them
  • To allow us to react to our surroundings and coordinate our behaviour
📝 The computer analogy

The nervous system works like a computerised machine — receiving inputs (senses), processing information (brain and spinal cord), and producing outputs (muscle movements, gland secretions). The brain is the processor, the nerves are the wiring, and the sense organs are the sensors.

🔀
The Two Main Parts
CNS and PNS

The Human Nervous System Has Two Main Parts

🧠
Central Nervous System (CNS)

Made up of the brain and spinal cord. It's the control centre — receives information, processes it, and sends out instructions.

⚡
Peripheral Nervous System (PNS)

Consists of the nerves that branch out from the brain and spinal cord. It carries information between the CNS and the rest of the body.

Reflex Action
The fastest responses
📌 Reflex Action

A reflex action is a sudden, automatic, and uncontrolled response of parts of the body or the whole body to an external stimulus. Reflexes are usually involved in helping us avoid danger or damage.

Key Features of Reflex Actions

  • Sudden — happen immediately, without delay
  • Automatic — no conscious thought required
  • Uncontrolled — we can't decide to stop them
  • Protective — help us avoid danger or damage
  • The messages do not reach a conscious area of the brain before instructions are sent out to take action
  • Many reflexes involve the spinal cord, whereas others involve the brain
📝 Reflexes free the brain for other things

We also have lots of reflexes that help us take care of our basic bodily functions — leaving the brain free for thinking about other things. Breathing is a good example: you don't consciously instruct the diaphragm to flatten and the intercostal muscles to contract, because breathing is a reflex action.

Involvement of Neurons

Reflex actions involve three types of neuron:

  • Affector neurons (sensory) — carry impulses from the sense organ to the CNS
  • Relay neurons (interneurons) — connect affector and effector neurons in the CNS
  • Effector neurons (motor) — carry impulses from the CNS to the effector (muscle or gland)

📝 The key difference

A reflex action is a sudden, automatic, and uncontrolled response to a stimulus. A voluntary action involves conscious thought — you decide to do it.

🔄
The Reflex Arc
The pathway of a reflex
📌 Reflex Arc

The reflex arc is the neural path of a reflex. It includes the receptors, neurons, and effectors involved in a reflex action. The brain and spinal cord act together as coordinators that process information coming from sensory receptors and neurons and instruct effector neurons and effectors to react.

The Pathway

1. Impulses from a sensory receptor in the skin pass along an affector neuron to the central nervous system (the spinal cord).
2. The neuron enters the spinal cord through the dorsal root.
3. When the impulse arrives at the synapse with a short relay neuron, a transmitter is released that causes an impulse to be sent along the relay neuron.
4. When the impulse reaches the synapse between the relay neuron and an effector neuron returning to the arm, another transmitter chemical is released.
5. This starts impulses travelling along the effector neuron to the organ (effector), which brings about the change.
6. The effector neuron leaves the spinal cord by the ventral root. In this example the impulses arrive in the muscles of the upper arm, causing them to contract and move your hand upwards sharply.

Key Terms

  • Dorsal root — root at the back of the spinal cord (carries affector/sensory neurons in)
  • Ventral root — root at the front of the spinal cord (carries effector/motor neurons out)
  • Receptor — the structure that detects the stimulus (e.g. a pain receptor in the skin)
  • Effector — the structure that responds (usually a muscle or gland)
📝 Why the reflex arc is fast

The reflex arc is fast because the impulse does not have to travel up to the brain first. It goes to the spinal cord, where the relay neuron passes the impulse straight back out through the effector neuron. The brain is informed after the action, which is why we often say "I didn't even think — I just reacted."

🌟
Examples of Reflexes
Common reflexes in daily life

Protective Reflexes

  • Blinking — if something comes towards your face, you blink to protect your eyes from physical damage
  • Pupillary light reflex — the pupil of an eye dilates or constricts depending on light levels; protects the retina from damage from too much light and enables vision in low light
  • Knee-jerk reflex — hitting the leg just below the knee cap causes the lower leg to jerk upwards and outwards as the leg straightens
  • Withdrawing your hand from a hot object — the classic reflex action; the hand moves away before you feel the pain

Reflexes That Keep Us Alive

  • Breathing — the diaphragm and intercostal muscles contract automatically, without conscious thought
  • Heartbeat — the heart beats continuously without us having to think about it
  • Coughing and sneezing — clear the airways of irritants
  • Swallowing — moves food safely from mouth to stomach
  • Vomiting — expels harmful substances from the stomach

The Knee-Jerk Reflex — A Classic Example

The knee-jerk reflex is often used by doctors to test the nervous system:

  • One person sits with one leg loosely crossed over the other
  • The other person hits the crossed leg just below the knee cap (patella) gently but firmly
  • This hits a ligament which stretches a muscle
  • Stretch receptors pick this up and set up a reflex arc
  • The impulse travels to the spinal cord and back to the quadriceps muscles in the thigh
  • These contract, causing the lower leg to jerk upwards and outwards
  • The leg drops straight back into its normal position

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 5.1
What you should remember
  • The nervous system is a highly complex system that provides rapid, coordinated responses
  • It has two main parts: the CNS (brain + spinal cord) and the PNS (nerves branching from the CNS)
  • A reflex action is a sudden, automatic, uncontrolled response to a stimulus
  • Reflexes are protective — they help us avoid danger or damage, and they free the brain for other tasks
  • Reflexes involve three types of neurons: affector, relay, and effector
  • The reflex arc is the neural pathway: Receptor → affector neuron → relay neuron → effector neuron → effector
  • Reflexes are fast because the impulse does not have to travel to the conscious brain first
  • Common reflexes: blinking, pupillary light reflex, knee-jerk reflex, pulling hand from a hot object, breathing, heartbeat
  • Voluntary actions involve conscious thought; reflex actions are automatic
