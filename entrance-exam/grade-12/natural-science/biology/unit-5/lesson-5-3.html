⚡ Peripheral Nervous System & Neurons

How does the brain communicate with the rest of the body? Through a vast network of neurons that extend from the CNS to every corner of your body. This lesson explores the PNS, the cells that carry its signals, and how those signals travel.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

The CNS (brain and spinal cord) is the body's control centre — but on its own, it can't do anything. It needs a network of nerves to carry information to and from every part of the body. That network is the Peripheral Nervous System (PNS). This lesson explores its divisions, the nerve cells (neurons) that make it work, and how signals travel along them.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define the peripheral nervous system (PNS)
  • Describe the somatic and autonomic nervous systems
  • Compare the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions
  • Describe the structure of a neuron
  • Identify the three types of neurons
  • Explain how signals pass across a synapse
  • Explain what a nerve impulse and action potential are
🔌
The Peripheral Nervous System
The wiring of the body
📌 Peripheral Nervous System (PNS)

The Peripheral Nervous System (PNS) is the division of the nervous system that contains all the nerves that lie outside of the central nervous system. Its primary role is to connect the CNS to the organs, limbs, and skin.

What the PNS Does

  • Nerves extend from the CNS to the outermost areas of the body
  • It allows the brain and spinal cord to receive and send information to other areas of the body
  • It allows you to react to stimuli in your environment

The Two Parts of the PNS

🏃
Somatic Nervous System

Responsible for carrying sensory and motor information to and from the CNS. Controls voluntary movement. Name comes from the Greek word soma, meaning "body."

❤️
Autonomic Nervous System

Regulates involuntary body functions such as blood flow, heartbeat, digestion, and breathing. Controls aspects of the body that are usually not under voluntary control.

📝 Involuntary = automatic

You don't have to consciously think about your heartbeat, blood flow, digestion, or breathing — the autonomic nervous system handles all of that automatically. That's why it's called "autonomic" — like an autonomous (self-governing) system.

🏃
The Somatic Nervous System
Voluntary movement

The somatic system is the part of the PNS that is responsible for carrying sensory and motor information to and from the central nervous system.

Key Features

  • The name comes from the Greek word soma, which means "body"
  • Responsible for transmitting sensory information to the CNS
  • Responsible for voluntary movement (skeletal muscles)
  • Controls movements you consciously decide to make

Examples

  • Waving your hand
  • Walking, running, or jumping
  • Writing, typing, or playing an instrument
  • Speaking and singing
  • Feeling touch, pressure, or temperature on your skin
❤️
The Autonomic Nervous System
Involuntary body functions

The autonomic system is part of your PNS that is responsible for regulating involuntary body functions. It controls aspects of the body that are usually not under voluntary control, allowing these functions to take place without the need to consciously think about them.

Examples of Autonomic Functions

  • Blood flow
  • Heartbeat
  • Digestion
  • Breathing
  • Sweating
  • Pupil dilation/constriction

Two Branches of the Autonomic System

The autonomic system is further divided into two branches — the sympathetic and parasympathetic systems. They work in opposition to keep the body balanced.

⚡
Sympathetic vs Parasympathetic
The "fight or flight" and "rest and digest" systems
🔥
Sympathetic System

Regulates the "fight-or-flight" response. Prepares the body to expend energy to respond to environmental threats. When action is needed, the sympathetic system triggers a response by accelerating heart rate, increasing breathing rate, boosting blood flow to muscles, activating sweat secretion, and dilating the pupils.

😌
Parasympathetic System

Helps maintain normal body functions and conserve physical resources. Once a threat is recognized, this system will slow the heart rate, slow breathing, reduce blood flow to muscles, and constrict the pupils. This allows us to return our bodies to a normal resting state.

Comparing the Two Branches

FunctionSympathetic SystemParasympathetic System
PurposeFight-or-flight (prepare for action)Rest-and-digest (recover and restore)
Heart rateIncreasesDecreases
Breathing rateIncreasesDecreases
Blood flow to musclesIncreasesDecreases

📝 A real-life example

Imagine you're walking down the street and see a snake. Your sympathetic system kicks in — your heart races, your pupils dilate, your breathing quickens, and you feel a rush of energy. You're ready to run or fight.

The snake turns out to be a stick. Once the threat is gone, your parasympathetic system takes over — your heart slows down, your breathing returns to normal, and your body goes back to its resting state. This constant balance is essential to good health.

Neurons
The cells of the nervous system
📌 Neurons

Neurons are nerve cells — the basic unit of communication in the nervous system. Neurons vary in size, shape, and structure depending on their role and location, but nearly all neurons have three essential parts: a cell body, an axon, and dendrites.

Parts of a Neuron

🟢
Cell Body (Soma)

The neuron's core. Contains the nucleus and specialized organelles. Carries genetic information, maintains the neuron's structure, and provides energy to drive activities. Enclosed by a membrane that protects it and allows it to interact with its surroundings.

🟡
Axon

A long, tail-like structure that joins the cell body at a specialized junction called the axon hillock. Most neurons have one main axon. Carries nerve impulses away from the cell body. Many axons are insulated with myelin.

🟠
Dendrites

Fibrous roots that branch out from the cell body. Like antennae, they receive and process signals from the axons of other neurons. Neurons can have more than one set of dendrites (dendritic trees).

Key Terms

  • Dendrites — tree-like branches that receive signals from other nerve cells at synapses
  • Axon — long process from nerve cell that carries the nerve impulse away from the cell body
  • Cell body — the neuron's core, containing the nucleus
  • Myelin sheath — fatty insulating sheath that grows around many nerves; makes the impulse travel as fast as possible
  • Node of Ranvier — periodic gap in the myelin sheath on the axon; facilitates rapid conduction of nerve impulses
  • Axon hillock — junction where the axon joins the cell body
📝 The discovery of the Node of Ranvier

The interruptions in the myelin covering were first discovered in 1878 by French histologist and pathologist Louis-Antoine Ranvier, who described the nodes as constrictions. These gaps allow the nerve impulse to "jump" from node to node — a process called saltatory conduction — making the impulse travel much faster.

🔀
Types of Neurons
Sensory, motor, and interneurons

In terms of function, scientists classify neurons into three broad types:

👁️
Sensory Neurons (Affector/Afferent)

Help you taste, smell, hear, see, and feel. Triggered by physical and chemical inputs from your environment. Carry messages from sensory receptors to the CNS. Typically have a long dendrite and short axon.

💪
Motor Neurons (Effector/Efferent)

Play a role in movements — voluntary and involuntary. Allow the brain and spinal cord to communicate with muscles, organs, and glands. Have a long axon and short dendrites. Transmit messages from the CNS to the effectors.

🔗
Interneurons

Neural intermediaries found in the brain and spinal cord. The most common type of neuron. They pass signals from sensory neurons and other interneurons to motor neurons and other interneurons. Often form complex circuits that help you react to external stimuli.

The Signal Pathway

Sense organ → afferent neuron (sensory) → central nervous system → efferent neuron (motor) → muscles/glands (effectors)

Types of Nerves

  • Nerves are bundles of neurons
  • Effector nerves — carry only effector neurons
  • Affector nerves — carry only affector neurons
  • Mixed nerves — carry a mixture of effector and affector neurons
🔗
Synapses
Where neurons meet
📌 Synapse

A synapse is the junction between two neurons (axon-to-dendrite) or between a neuron and a muscle. Whenever one neuron ends and another begins, there is a gap known as a synapse.

How Signals Cross the Synapse

  • The electrical impulses that travel along neurons cannot leap the gap
  • When an impulse arrives at the end of a neuron, chemicals are released
  • These chemical transmitters (neurotransmitters) cross the synapse
  • They are picked up by special receptor cells at the end of the next neuron
  • This starts up an electrical impulse, which then travels along the next neuron

Neurotransmitters

  • Neurotransmitters are often referred to as the body's chemical messengers
  • They are the molecules used by the nervous system to transmit messages between neurons, or from neurons to muscles
  • Acetylcholine (ACh) is an abundant neurotransmitter in the human body, found in both the CNS and PNS
  • An excitatory transmitter promotes the generation of an action potential in the receiving neuron
  • An inhibitory transmitter decreases the likelihood that the neuron will generate an action potential
  • Neuromodulators are not restricted to the synaptic cleft and can affect large numbers of neurons at once

Neuromuscular Junctions

  • The junction between an axon terminal of a motor neuron and a muscle fibre is called a neuromuscular junction
  • When the impulse reaches this junction, the motor neuron releases acetylcholine
  • This causes the muscle fibre to contract
⚡
Nerve Impulse & Action Potential
The electrical signal

The nervous system relies on nerve impulses travelling along the neurons. Each nerve impulse is a minute electrical event that works as a result of charge differences across the membrane of the axon.

📌 Action Potential

An action potential is a short-term change in the electrical potential on the surface of a cell when it is stimulated. The wave of positive charge inside the axon when the neuron is stimulated is called the action potential.

How the Action Potential Works

  • When a neuron is stimulated, the potential across the cell membrane changes
  • When a neurotransmitter binds to its receptor, it can open channels that let particular ions go through the membrane
  • When a sodium (Na⁺) channel is open, Na⁺ ions flow through the membrane into the cell for two reasons:
    • The resting potential keeps the inside negatively charged, so positive ions are attracted in
    • There is an attracting concentration gradient for sodium
  • The influx of positive ions makes the inside of the cell less negative — this is called depolarizing the cell
  • If the cell is depolarized from its resting potential (about −70 mV) to its threshold potential (about −55 mV), an abrupt change occurs — an action potential

Phases of an Action Potential

PhaseWhat happens
Resting potentialAbout −70 mV; inside is negative relative to outside
ThresholdAbout −55 mV; if reached, an action potential fires
DepolarizationNa⁺ flows in; membrane potential becomes positive
RepolarizationK⁺ flows out; potential returns to negative
HyperpolarizationInside becomes even more negative than usual
Refractory periodBrief period when the neuron is hard to re-excite

Key Terms

  • Resting potential — the potential difference across the membrane of a neuron at rest (about −70 mV)
  • Threshold potential — the voltage at which depolarization of a cell leads to generation of an action potential (about −55 mV)
  • Hyperpolarization — increasing neuronal membrane potential to more than its usual resting potential (making it harder to induce an action potential)
  • Refractory period — a brief period following the generation of an action potential, during which a neuron is hard to re-excite

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 5.3
What you should remember
  • The PNS consists of all nerves outside the CNS; its role is to connect the CNS to the organs, limbs, and skin
  • The PNS has two parts: somatic (voluntary) and autonomic (involuntary)
  • The autonomic system has two branches: sympathetic (fight or flight) and parasympathetic (rest and digest)
  • A neuron has three essential parts: cell body, axon, dendrites
  • Myelin sheath insulates axons and speeds up nerve impulses; nodes of Ranvier are gaps in the myelin
  • Three types of neurons: sensory (afferent), motor (efferent), interneurons
  • Signal pathway: sense organ → afferent neuron → CNS → efferent neuron → effector (muscle or gland)
  • At a synapse, chemicals called neurotransmitters cross the gap and start a new impulse in the next neuron
  • The resting potential is about −70 mV; the threshold potential is about −55 mV
  • An action potential is a short-term change in electrical potential that travels along the neuron
