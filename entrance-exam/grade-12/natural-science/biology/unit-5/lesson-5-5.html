👁️ Sense Organs

Our sense organs connect us to the world — they detect light, sound, chemicals, temperature, and touch, then send that information to the brain. This lesson explores the five main sense organs: skin, tongue, nose, eye, and ear.

📖 Reading: ~30 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Sense organs are specialized structures that detect stimuli from the environment and convert them into nerve impulses that the brain can interpret. This lesson covers the five main human sense organs — skin, tongue, nose, eye, and ear — and how each one works.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe the structure and function of the skin
  • Explain the sense of taste (tongue) and the five tastes
  • Explain the sense of smell (nose) and how it works
  • Describe the structure and function of the eye
  • Identify common eye defects
  • Describe the structure and function of the ear
  • Explain how the ear helps us hear and maintain balance
🖐️
The Skin
MoE Textbook §5.5.1
📌 The Skin

The skin is one of the largest organs in the human body in surface area and weight. It is a remarkably complex organ that carries out a number of important functions. It gives you the senses of touch, temperature, and pain.

Functions of the Skin

  • Contains a huge variety of sense organs (touch, temperature, pressure, pain)
  • Forms a waterproof layer around the body tissues — protects against loss of water by evaporation
  • Prevents gaining water by osmosis
  • Protects the body from the entry of bacteria and other pathogens
  • Protects the body from damage by UV light
  • Is an excretory organ — nitrogenous wastes are lost with the sweat
  • Is vital in controlling the body temperature

The Three Layers of Skin

LayerWhat it containsFunction
EpidermisOutermost layer — made up of dead cells. Contains keratinocytes, melanocytes, and Langerhans cells.Provides waterproof barrier; creates skin tone; protects against water loss and entry of pathogens
DermisMiddle layer — contains tough connective tissue, blood vessels, hair follicles, sweat glands, sebaceous glands, sensory receptorsInvolved in temperature control (homeostasis) and the sense of touch
HypodermisDeepest layer — fatty tissue and connective tissueEnergy store; insulation against heat loss

Cells of the Epidermis

  • Keratinocytes — produce the protein keratin, the main component of the epidermis. Keratin makes up hair, nails, and the surface layer of the skin. It forms the rigidity of skin and provides barrier protection.
  • Melanocytes — produce skin pigment called melanin
  • Langerhans cells — prevent things from getting into your skin

Skin Thickness

  • Thin skin covers most of the body; the thinnest skin is on the eyelids
  • Thick skin is present on the soles of the feet and palms of the hands
  • Thick skin has no hair follicles or sebaceous glands; thin skin does
📝 Collagen and elastin

In the dermis, there are collagen and elastin proteins that are necessary for skin health because they offer support and elasticity (the skin's ability to return to its original state after being stretched). Collagen is the most plentiful protein in the skin, making up 75–80%. Over time, the environment and ageing reduce the body's ability to produce collagen.

👅
The Tongue — Taste
MoE Textbook §5.5.2

The tongue is a muscular organ in the mouth. The tongue is covered with moist, pink tissue called mucosa. Tiny bumps called papillae give the tongue its rough texture.

Taste Buds

  • Thousands of taste buds cover the surfaces of the papillae
  • Taste buds are collections of nerve-like cells that connect to nerves running into the brain
  • The tongue is anchored to the mouth by webs of tough tissue and mucosa
  • The tether holding down the front of the tongue is called the frenum
  • The tongue is vital for chewing and swallowing food, as well as for speech

The Five Basic Tastes

The sensory receptors of the tongue and those found in the nostrils are sensitive to solutions of certain chemical substances. The sensory receptors of taste are located on the upper surface of the tongue, and to a lesser extent on the surface of the throat.

  • Sweet
  • Sour
  • Bitter
  • Salt
  • Umami — a very savoury flavour found in foods such as meat, cheese, broth, and mushroom (discovered more recently)
📝 The tongue map is a myth

For many years it was thought that the receptors for the four known senses had their areas of greatest concentration on different parts of the tongue. It has now been clearly shown that in fact all of the five different taste organs are spread out all over the tongue, although some of them may seem to be in a greater concentration in certain places. The old "tongue map" you may have seen in older textbooks is wrong.

👃
The Nose — Smell
MoE Textbook §5.5.3

The human nose is a sense organ of smell. Another word for smell is olfaction. The olfactory receptors in the nose help to identify food, mates, predators, and provide both sensual pleasure in the odour of flowers and perfume, as well as warnings of danger (e.g. spoiled food, fire, or chemical dangers).

Functions of the Nose

  • Smell — the primary sense
  • Conditioning of inhaled air — warms and humidifies the air
  • Filtering — hairs inside the nose prevent large particles from entering the lungs
  • The nasal passages not only allow the passage of air for respiration but also warm and filter the air

How We Smell

  • Specialized receptor cells of the olfactory epithelium detect and recognize smells
  • The air passes through the nasal cavity and through a thick layer of mucus to the olfactory bulb
  • The olfactory bulb is situated in the forebrain
  • Smells are recognized here because each smell molecule fits into a nerve cell like a puzzle piece
  • The cells then send signals to the brain via the olfactory nerve
  • The brain interprets those molecules as a specific smell
📝 Taste and smell are linked

For human beings to be able to taste and smell, chemicals must go into solution in the form of liquid coating the membranes of receptor cells before they can be detected.

• Smell receptors are more specialized for detecting vapours coming to the organism from distant sources
• Taste receptors are specialized for detection of chemicals present in the mouth itself
• Smell receptors are much more sensitive than taste receptors

The Eye — Vision
MoE Textbook §5.5.4

The eye transmits visual stimuli to the brain for interpretation and thereby functions as a sensory organ of vision. The eyeball is located in the eye orbit, a round, bony hollow formed by several different bones of the skull. In the orbit, the eye is surrounded by a cushion of fat. The bony orbit and fat cushion protect the eyeball.

External Structures of the Eye

  • Eyebrows — protect the eyes by preventing perspiration from running into the eyes; help shade the eyes from direct sunlight
  • Eyelids — protect the eye from foreign bodies and limit the amount of light entering the eye; distribute tears
  • Eyelashes — filter dust and dirt from air entering the eye
  • Conjunctiva — a thin, transparent, continuous membrane that lines the inside of the eyelids and covers most of the anterior eye
  • Lacrimal apparatus — glands and ducts that lubricate the eye (produce tears)
  • Extraocular muscles — six muscles attached to the outer surface of each eyeball that control six different directions of eye movement

Internal Structures of the Eye

StructureFunction
ScleraThe white visible portion of the eye — a dense, protective, white covering that physically supports the internal structures
CorneaThe transparent, anterior (front) part of the eye. Refracts light along with the lens. Well supplied with nerve endings, making it responsive to pain and touch.
IrisThe pigmented, colored portion of the eye. Controls the diameter of the pupil according to the light source.
PupilThe small aperture in the centre of the iris. Allows light to enter and focus on the retina.
LensA biconvex, transparent, avascular structure that refracts (bends) light rays onto the retina. Shape is controlled by the ciliary muscles.
Ciliary musclesControl the shape of the lens — bulge to focus on close objects, flatten to focus on far objects
ChoroidContains the vascularity necessary to nourish the inner part of the eye. Prevents light from reflecting internally.
RetinaThe innermost layer. Receives visual stimuli and sends it to the brain. Contains rods and cones.
RodsHighly sensitive to light; regulate black and white vision; function in dim light
ConesFunction in bright light; sensitive to color
Optic discWhere the optic nerve enters the eyeball — no retina here, so it is the "blind spot"
Fovea centralisArea highly concentrated with cones; highest visual resolution and color vision

How the Eye Adjusts to Light

  • The iris controls pupil size by muscles:
    • Circular muscles run around the iris
    • Radial muscles run across it (like spokes on a wheel)
  • Dim light: radial muscles contract, circular muscles relax → pupil dilates (gets bigger) → more light enters
  • Bright light: circular muscles contract, radial muscles relax → pupil constricts (gets smaller) → less light enters

Chambers of the Eye

  • Anterior chamber — between the cornea and iris; filled with aqueous humour (clear liquid produced by the ciliary body)
  • Posterior chamber — between the iris and the lens
  • Vitreous chamber — the largest chamber, behind the lens to the retina; filled with vitreous humour (clear, gelatinous)
📝 Common eye defects

Myopia (short-sightedness) — the eyeball is too long; distant objects appear blurry; corrected with concave lenses
Hyperopia (long-sightedness) — the eyeball is too short; close objects appear blurry; corrected with convex lenses
Astigmatism — the cornea is unevenly curved; corrected with special lenses
Cataract — the lens becomes cloudy; corrected with surgery
Glaucoma — increased pressure inside the eye; damages the optic nerve
Colour blindness — usually genetic; reduced ability to distinguish colours

The Ear — Hearing & Balance
MoE Textbook §5.5.5

Ears are specialized organs for hearing. They are also concerned with the balance and position of the body. The ear is divided into three regions: the outer ear, middle ear, and inner ear.

Outer Ear

  • Pinna (auricle) — a flap that helps to trap and funnel sound into the ear
  • Ear canal — a tube about 2 cm long in humans
  • Eardrum (tympanum) — a sheet of very thin membrane at the end of the ear canal
  • Small hairs at the entrance filter out dust particles
  • Cells lining the ear canal produce waxy material that traps dust and germs, and lubricates the eardrum

Middle Ear

  • Behind the eardrum is a cavity filled with air
  • Contains the three smallest bones in the human body:
    • Malleus (hammer) — attached to the eardrum
    • Incus (anvil) — between the malleus and stapes
    • Stapes (stirrup) — transmits sound from the incus to the cochlea
  • Connected to the throat by the Eustachian tube — equalizes air pressure
  • Two openings: the oval window and the round window

Inner Ear

  • Filled with fluid
  • Contains two sac-like structures called the sacculus and utriculus
  • Three semicircular canals — for balance and movement detection
  • A coiled tube called the cochlea — for hearing
  • The cochlea contains three tubes in one; the floor of the middle tube is lined with sensory cells linked to affector neurons
  • These nerve fibers join to form the auditory nerve which leads to the brain

The Mechanism of Hearing

1. The pinna collects sound waves and directs them to the eardrum through the ear canal.
2. When sound waves hit the eardrum, it vibrates.
3. The vibrations are transmitted through the ear ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes) to the oval window.
4. The ear ossicles also amplify the vibrations (make them bigger).
5. The vibrations of the stapes make the membrane at the oval window vibrate, which makes the fluid inside the cochlea vibrate.
6. The vibrations of the fluid cause the hair-like sensory cells to move.
7. This movement produces nerve impulses in the affector nerve fibres, which are transmitted to the brain for interpretation.

Range of Hearing

  • The human ear is sensitive to vibrations ranging from 20 vibrations per second (very low note) to 30,000 vibrations per second (very high note)
  • High notes are detected in the first part of the cochlea
  • Low notes are recorded in the last part of the cochlea
⚖️
The Sense of Balance
The semicircular canals

The semicircular canals in the inner ear are concerned with the detection of motion and balance.

How Balance Works

  • The swellings on each of the semicircular canals (ampullae) contain sensory cells attached to sensory nerve endings
  • The sensory cells have hairs enclosed in a core of jelly substance called a cupula
  • Whenever the body or head moves, the semicircular canals move with the head
  • The fluid in the semicircular canals also starts to move but it lags behind in its motion — so it apparently moves in the opposite direction
  • The moving fluid causes the cupula to tilt, pressing the hairs of the sensory cells
  • This pressing creates nerve impulses in the sensory nerve endings
  • The nerve impulses are transmitted to the brain, which interprets the direction and speed of the motion
📝 Why spinning makes you dizzy

The semicircular canals are all at right angles to each other, so each one is sensitive to movement in a different plane. One canal responds to nodding, one to shaking, and one to head tilting.

Fast spinning followed by instant interruption causes dizziness because the fluid in the semicircular canals keeps moving after the spinning has stopped. Your brain keeps receiving signals of movement even though your body has stopped — producing the sensation of dizziness.

📝 Activity 5.19–5.24 — Practical work
In small groups or individually, try these investigations:
  • Touch test: draw a grid on your partner's hand and test which areas are most sensitive to touch with a fine bristle
  • Temperature test: place one hand in hot water and one in cold water for one minute, then place both in room-temperature water — do they feel the same?
  • Pupil reflex test: observe the size of your partner's pupils in normal light, then cover the eyes for a minute and observe how the pupils change when the cover is removed
  • Eye dissection: examine a fresh eye from a sheep or cow — find the lens, retina, and other features

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 5.5
What you should remember
  • Skin — the largest organ; three layers: epidermis, dermis, hypodermis; senses touch, temperature, and pain
  • Tongue — taste organ; contains taste buds on papillae; detects five basic tastes: sweet, sour, bitter, salt, umami
  • Nose — smell organ (olfaction); receptors in the olfactory epithelium; interprets smells at the olfactory bulb
  • Eye — vision organ; cornea and lens refract light; iris controls pupil size; retina contains rods (dim light) and cones (color)
  • Common eye defects: myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism, cataract, glaucoma, colour blindness
  • Ear — hearing and balance; three regions: outer ear (pinna + ear canal + eardrum), middle ear (ossicles + Eustachian tube), inner ear (cochlea + semicircular canals)
  • Sound pathway: pinna → ear canal → eardrum → ossicles → oval window → cochlea → auditory nerve → brain
  • Semicircular canals detect motion and maintain balance; fluid lags behind causing the sensation of motion
