🍄 Fungi — Decomposers & Fermenters

Fungi are the recyclers of the living world. They decompose dead matter, ferment our food, produce life-saving antibiotics, and cause diseases. From the yeast in bread to the mold on old fruit to the mushrooms in your kitchen — fungi are everywhere, and essential to life on Earth.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources

Introduction
What you will learn

Fungi are a kingdom of their own — neither plants nor animals. They can't photosynthesize like plants, but they're not animals either. Instead, they absorb nutrients from their environment, often breaking down dead matter as decomposers. This makes them essential recyclers in every ecosystem. And it makes them incredibly useful to humans — for food, medicine, and industry.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define fungi and describe their general characteristics
  • Identify the different forms of fungi (yeasts, molds, mushrooms)
  • Describe hyphal structure and how fungi grow
  • Explain how fungi reproduce
  • Discuss the ecological importance of fungi
  • Explain the role of fungi in Ethiopian fermented foods
  • Identify harmful fungi and the diseases they cause

What are Fungi?
MoE Textbook §2.2.3, p. 45
📌 Fungi (MoE Textbook, p. 45)

Fungi (singular: fungus) are eukaryotic organisms whose cells have a distinct nucleus containing the cell's genetic material (DNA), surrounded by a special envelope called the nuclear membrane. They may be unicellular or multicellular.

📌 Key characteristics

• Eukaryotic — have a true nucleus
• Cannot photosynthesize — unlike plants
• Cell walls made of chitin — not cellulose like plants
• Absorb nutrients from their environment
• Reproduce sexually or asexually
• Can be unicellular (yeasts) or multicellular (molds, mushrooms)

📝 Fungi are NOT plants

Although large multicellular fungi like mushrooms may look somewhat like plants, they are fundamentally different:

• Fungi cannot carry out photosynthesis — they don't have chlorophyll
• Fungi have cell walls made of chitin, while plants have cellulose
• Fungi are heterotrophs — they must absorb their food from other organisms

🍄
Forms of Fungi
Three main forms

Fungi come in many different forms. The most common are yeasts, molds, and mushrooms.

🟡
Yeasts

Unicellular fungi. Oval microorganisms that are larger than bacteria. Reproduce by budding. Example: Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's yeast).

🟢
Molds

Multicellular fungi. Form visible masses called mycelia, composed of long filaments (hyphae) that branch and intertwine. Example: Rhizopus (bread mold).

🍄
Mushrooms

Multicellular fungi with a visible fruiting body. Often edible (though some are poisonous). Example: Agaricus bisporus (button mushroom).

🟤
Slime Molds

Amoeba-like fungi that grow on decayed plant matter and in wet areas. They are now usually classified as protists, but traditionally grouped with fungi.

📝 The classic example — bread mold

The cottony growths sometimes found on bread and fruit are mold mycelia. If you look closely at bread mold under a microscope, you'll see:

• Hyphae — the branching filaments that form the body of the mold
• Mycelium — the whole mass of hyphae
• Sporangiophores — the stalks that bear spore cases
• Sporangia — the round structures at the tips containing spores

🔬
Structure & Growth
Hyphae and mycelium
📌 Hyphae & Mycelium

The body of a multicellular fungus is made of hyphae (singular: hypha) — long, branching filaments. A mass of hyphae is called a mycelium (plural: mycelia).

How Fungi Grow & Feed

  • Hyphae grow at their tips, extending into new areas
  • They secrete digestive enzymes into their surroundings
  • The enzymes break down complex organic molecules into simpler ones
  • The fungus absorbs the simpler nutrients through its cell walls
  • This mode of feeding is called absorptive heterotrophy

Where Fungi Live

  • They colonize most habitats on Earth, preferring dark, moist conditions
  • They live in soil, seawater, freshwater, or on an animal or plant host
  • They obtain nourishment by absorbing organic materials from their environment
📝 Chitin — the fungal cell wall

True fungi have cell walls composed primarily of a substance called chitin. Chitin is the same tough material found in the exoskeletons of insects — not the cellulose found in plants. This is one of the key features that distinguishes fungi from plants.

🔄
Reproduction
Sexual and asexual

Fungi can reproduce both sexually and asexually.

Asexual Reproduction

  • Budding — a small daughter cell grows out from the parent cell (yeasts)
  • Fragmentation — pieces of hyphae break off and grow into new individuals
  • Spore formation — specialized cells called spores are produced in large numbers

Sexual Reproduction

  • Two hyphae of different mating types fuse
  • Their nuclei combine to form a diploid zygote
  • The zygote undergoes meiosis to produce sexual spores
  • Spores are then released and grow into new individuals
📝 Why spores matter

Fungal spores are tiny, lightweight, and produced in enormous numbers. They can travel long distances through air or water, allowing fungi to colonize new habitats quickly. Some spores can survive extreme conditions for years, waiting for the right conditions to germinate.

🌍
Ecological Importance
Fungi in the ecosystem

Fungi play a crucial role in the balance of ecosystems. They are essential to life on Earth.

Fungi as Decomposers

  • Break down dead organic matter — wood, leaves, dead animals
  • Release nutrients back into the soil for plants to use
  • Without decomposers like fungi, nutrients would be locked up in dead matter
  • The food web would be incomplete without organisms that decompose organic matter

Fungi as Symbionts

  • Mycorrhizae — fungi that live in symbiosis with plant roots, helping plants absorb water and minerals
  • Lichens — fungi living with algae or cyanobacteria; help colonize bare rock
  • These partnerships are vital for many plants — including crops

Fungi as Parasites

  • Some fungi are pathogens of plants and animals
  • They cause heavy crop losses (e.g., rice blast, wheat rust)
  • They cause diseases in humans and domestic animals (e.g., athlete's foot, ringworm, candidiasis)
📝 Fungi are indispensable

Without fungi as decomposers, dead plants and animals would pile up, and nutrients would not be recycled. Life as we know it would eventually stop. Fungi are not just useful — they are essential.

Ethiopian Fermented Foods
Fungi in our daily life

Fungi have been used in Ethiopia for thousands of years to make traditional fermented foods and beverages. They are central to our cuisine.

📌 From the MoE Textbook, p. 54

"The beneficial activities of yeasts and other fungi... have long been exploited as food, as a component in the processing of food, and as biological mechanisms in the process of alcohol production and traditional fermentation."

📝 Starter cultures and back-slopping

In traditional fermentation, a small amount of the previous batch is often saved and added to the new batch. This is called back-slopping. It keeps the same beneficial microorganisms alive across generations and ensures consistent flavour.

A starter culture is a preparation of microorganisms deliberately added to start fermentation. In traditional Ethiopian fermentation, back-slopping serves as the starter culture.

⚠️
Harmful Fungi
When fungi cause problems

While fungi provide huge benefits, some fungi cause serious problems. Their harmful activities as saprotrophs include damage to timber, fuel, food, and manufactured goods. As parasites, they cause heavy crop losses and diseases of humans and domestic animals.

Fungal Diseases of Humans

  • Athlete's foot — caused by Trichophyton species; itchy skin between the toes
  • Ringworm — caused by various dermatophyte fungi; circular rash on skin or scalp
  • Candidiasis (thrush) — caused by Candida albicans; affects mouth, throat, and other mucous membranes
  • Yeast infections — caused by overgrowth of Candida

Fungal Diseases of Plants & Animals

  • Rice blast — devastating fungal disease of rice crops
  • Wheat rust — reduces wheat yields significantly
  • Corn smut — fungal infection of maize
  • Foot rot in livestock — fungal infection causing lameness in sheep and cattle
  • Ringworm in animals — common skin infection in pets and livestock

Fungi in Food Spoilage

  • Molds spoil bread, fruit, and vegetables
  • Some produce mycotoxins — toxic compounds that can cause serious illness
  • Aflatoxin — produced by Aspergillus molds on peanuts and grains; a known carcinogen

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 2.4
What you should remember
  • Fungi are eukaryotic organisms with chitin cell walls; they cannot photosynthesize and absorb nutrients from their environment
  • The three main forms are yeasts (unicellular), molds (multicellular filaments), and mushrooms (multicellular fruiting bodies)
  • The body of a multicellular fungus is made of hyphae; a mass of hyphae is a mycelium
  • Fungi reproduce sexually and asexually (budding, fragmentation, spores)
  • Ecologically, fungi are the main decomposers in most ecosystems — they recycle nutrients
  • Fungi are used in Ethiopian fermented foods: injera, kocho, tej, tella, and more
  • Some fungi cause disease — athlete's foot, ringworm, candidiasis — and crop losses
