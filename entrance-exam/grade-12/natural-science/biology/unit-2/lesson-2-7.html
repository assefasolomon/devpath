🌍 Economic Importance & Disease-Causing Microorganisms

Microorganisms are essential to life on Earth — they recycle nutrients, produce our food, clean our environment, and provide life-saving medicines. But a small minority cause devastating diseases. This final lesson of Unit 2 explores both sides of the microbial world.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions

Introduction
What you will learn

Throughout this unit, you've learned about the different groups of microorganisms. Now it's time to see the big picture: what do microorganisms do for us — and what do some of them do to us? From agriculture to medicine to environmental cleanup, microbes are indispensable partners in human life. But they also include some of our deadliest enemies.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe the role of microbes in agriculture (nutrient cycling, bio-fertilizers)
  • Explain how microbes are used in sewage treatment and bioremediation
  • Describe the role of microbes in food production and medicine
  • Explain the carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorus cycles
  • Describe how pathogens cause disease
  • Identify the major routes of disease transmission

Microorganisms in Agriculture
MoE Textbook §2.4, pp. 68–75

Microorganisms play an indispensable role in agriculture. They help in organic matter decomposition, humus formation, and nutrient cycling. Their most important roles include:

🌱
Nitrogen Fixation

Bacteria (like Rhizobium) convert atmospheric N₂ into forms plants can use.

💧
Phosphate Solubilization

Microbes release phosphorus from insoluble minerals, making it available to plants.

⛏️
Potassium Mobilization

Microbes break down minerals to release potassium for plant nutrition.

🛡️
Biological Control

Microbes fight plant pathogens and pests — reducing chemical pesticide use.

🌿
Organic Matter Decomposition

Bacteria and fungi break down dead plants, releasing nutrients for new growth.

🪨
Biofertilizers

Beneficial microbes are used as natural fertilizers, enriching soil sustainably.

📝 Microbes and the elements of life

The chemical elements carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, sulfur, and phosphorus are essential for life and abundant — but not necessarily in forms that organisms can use. Microorganisms are primarily responsible for converting these elements into usable forms. Without them, life would grind to a halt.

♻️
Sewage Treatment & Bioremediation
Microbes clean the environment

Sewage Treatment

Anaerobic bacteria are used in wastewater treatment on a regular basis. The main role of these bacteria in sewage treatment is to reduce the volume of sludge and produce methane gas from it.

  • Anaerobic bacteria get enough oxygen from their food source — no need to add oxygen
  • The methane gas produced can be used as an alternative energy source
  • Also helps in phosphorus removal from wastewater
  • Reduces the volume of sludge, making disposal easier and cheaper

Bioremediation

📌 Bioremediation

Bioremediation is a natural process that relies on microorganisms and plants (or their derivatives) to degrade or alter environmental contaminants as these organisms carry out their normal life functions.

What Can Be Bioremediated?

  • Hydrocarbons — including oil spills
  • Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) — industrial chemicals
  • Polyaromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) — from fossil fuels
  • Radionuclides — radioactive contaminants
  • Heavy metals — mercury, lead, etc.
📝 Why bioremediation works

Microorganisms are everywhere, so pollutants in the environment always come into contact with them. Microbes break down or transform pollutants using their inherent metabolic processes. When the contaminant is present, the microbial population grows. When the contaminant is degraded, the population declines. The residues are usually harmless products — water, carbon dioxide, and cell biomass.

Microorganisms in Medicine
Life-saving applications

Microorganisms have made it possible to produce many life-saving medicines. They can be used as "factories" to make human proteins at scale.

Human Insulin — A Classic Example

Insulin is a medicine prescribed for diabetic patients. Before the 1980s, insulin was extracted from the pancreas of cows and pigs. Now it is produced by genetically engineered bacteria and yeast.

Step 1: The human insulin gene is identified and extracted.
Step 2: The gene is inserted into a plasmid (recombinant DNA).
Step 3: The plasmid is inserted into a bacterium (like E. coli), creating a transgenic bacterium.
Step 4: The bacteria are grown in large culture tanks.
Step 5: The bacteria produce human insulin, which is harvested and purified.
Step 6: The insulin is packaged for use by diabetic patients.

Other Medical Applications

  • Antibiotics — many are produced by fungi and bacteria (e.g., penicillin from Penicillium)
  • Vaccines — viruses and bacteria are used to produce vaccines
  • Growth hormones — human growth hormone is made by recombinant bacteria
  • Gene therapy — viruses are used as vectors to deliver healthy genes to cells
  • Drug delivery — microorganisms are used as vectors and plasmids to carry medicines
🧑
Microorganisms in Health
The gut microbiome

Our bodies contain ten times more microorganisms than body cells. These microorganisms perform various useful functions.

  • Digestion — E. coli and other bacteria in the intestine help digest food
  • Vitamin synthesis — gut bacteria produce vitamin K, biotin, and folic acid
  • Protection against pathogens — beneficial bacteria prevent harmful bacteria from growing
  • Immune system regulation — microbiota train and regulate the immune system
📝 The balance

While microorganisms take nutrients from the body, they give back far more in return. A healthy gut microbiome is essential for digestion, immunity, and overall health. This is why antibiotics are prescribed carefully — they can disrupt the beneficial microbiome.

The Role of Bacteria in Recycling Minerals
MoE Textbook §2.5

Many bacteria are decomposers. When organisms die, these bacteria break down the complex molecules in their bodies into much simpler molecules. The bacteria use some of these for their own metabolism, but in the process release minerals back into the environment. About 90% of all living organisms are made up of C, O, N, and H — and these substances are limited in their availability. For life to continue, they must be recycled.

The Carbon Cycle

  • Photosynthesis — plants remove CO₂ from the atmosphere
  • Respiration — animals and plants release CO₂
  • Decomposition — microbes break down dead matter, releasing CO₂
  • Combustion — burning fossil fuels releases CO₂
  • Ocean uptake — oceans absorb large amounts of CO₂

The Nitrogen Cycle

  • Nitrogen fixation — bacteria convert atmospheric N₂ into ammonia (NH₃)
  • Rhizobium — lives in root nodules of legumes (peas, beans, clover) and fixes nitrogen
  • Nitrification — Nitrosomonas converts NH₄⁺ to NO₂⁻; Nitrobacter converts NO₂⁻ to NO₃⁻
  • Assimilation — plants absorb nitrates to make proteins
  • Denitrification — denitrifying bacteria convert NO₃⁻ back to N₂ gas

The Sulfur Cycle

  • Sulfur is found in fewer types of organic molecules than nitrogen, but it is found in many proteins
  • Bacteria play key roles in converting between sulfate, sulfide, and organic sulfur compounds

The Phosphorus Cycle

  • Phosphorus occurs in soils as both organic and inorganic forms
  • Organic P — mostly of microbial origin; held tightly until decomposed
  • Inorganic P — mainly as aluminum, iron, or calcium compounds
  • Microbes carry out mineralization — releasing phosphorus from organic matter
📝 Bacteria are the only organisms that can fix nitrogen

Nitrogen is abundant in the atmosphere but in a form not usable by plants and animals. Only bacteria can naturally convert atmospheric nitrogen to a form available to plants and animals (nitrate). Without nitrogen-fixing bacteria, life as we know it would be impossible.

Disease-Causing Microorganisms
MoE Textbook §2.5.1
📌 Germ Theory of Disease

The theory that disease can be caused by microorganisms is called the germ theory. Organisms that cause disease are called pathogens. A disease caused by a microorganism infecting the body is an infectious disease.

Key Terms

TermDefinition
Germ theoryMicroorganisms cause infectious diseases
PathogenAny organism that causes a disease
Infectious diseaseA disease caused by a microorganism infecting the body; sometimes called communicable diseases because they can be transmitted from one person to another
Normal microbiota (flora)Microorganisms that live on or inside another organism without causing disease
Immune systemA complex network of cells, tissues, organs, and substances that helps the body fight infections
Inflammatory responseA complex biological reaction of the body when tissues are wounded or invaded by pathogens

Major Pathogen Groups

  • Viruses — HIV/AIDS, hepatitis, measles, mumps, rubella, influenza, rabies, yellow fever, polio
  • Bacteria — tuberculosis, anthrax, staphylococcal infections, E. coli, salmonella, cholera, typhoid
  • Fungi — ringworm, candidiasis, athlete's foot
  • Protozoa — malaria, Chagas disease, giardiasis, amoebiasis, leishmaniasis, trypanosomiasis
  • Parasitic worms (helminths) — round worm, tape worm, trichinosis
  • Prions — mad cow disease, vCJD

Routes of Disease Transmission

RouteHow it worksExamples
Droplet infectionRespiratory diseases spread by coughing, sneezing; inhaled by othersCommon cold, flu, pneumonia
Contaminated waterMicrobes in water colonize the gut when drunkCholera, typhoid fever
Contaminated foodFood poisoning by eating infected foodSalmonellosis, botulism
Direct contactSkin infections spread by touchingAthlete's foot, ringworm
Sexual intercourseOrganisms infecting sex organs pass between partnersHIV/AIDS, syphilis, gonorrhoea, candidiasis
Blood-to-blood contactSharing infected needles, transfusionsHIV/AIDS, hepatitis B
Animal vectorsDiseases spread through insect bitesMalaria (mosquito), sleeping sickness (tsetse fly)
📝 Not all infections are deadly

Of the thousands of species of viruses, bacteria, fungi, and parasites, only a tiny portion cause disease. Most microorganisms are harmless or beneficial. The germs we fear — the pathogens — are a small minority.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 2.7
What you should remember
  • Agriculture: microbes fix nitrogen, solubilize phosphate, mobilize potassium, decompose organic matter, and act as biological control agents
  • Sewage treatment: anaerobic bacteria reduce sludge volume and produce methane as a renewable energy source
  • Bioremediation: microbes degrade environmental contaminants (oil, PCBs, PAHs, radionuclides, heavy metals)
  • Food production: microbes make bread, beer, cheese, butter, yoghurt, injera, tej, tella
  • Medicine: bacteria produce human insulin and other medicines through genetic engineering
  • Health: gut microbiota aid digestion, produce vitamins (K, biotin, folic acid), and protect against pathogens
  • Mineral cycles: bacteria are essential in the carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorus cycles
  • Germ theory: pathogens cause infectious diseases; they spread by droplet, water, food, contact, sexual, blood, and vector routes
