🦠 Eubacteria — The True Bacteria

Eubacteria are the most abundant organisms on Earth. They are simple, single-celled, and lack a nucleus — yet they play critical roles in every ecosystem, from recycling nutrients to causing disease. This lesson explores their structure, shapes, and classification.

📖 Reading: ~20 min

Introduction
What you will learn

If you could count every living thing on Earth, you'd find that bacteria vastly outnumber everything else. They were the first organisms to appear on Earth — about 4 billion years ago — and the oldest known fossils are of bacteria-like organisms. Today they live in every environment, from boiling hot springs to the deepest ocean trenches to inside your own body.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define eubacteria and prokaryotes
  • Describe the structure of a bacterial cell
  • Identify the three main bacterial shapes
  • Explain clustering patterns (diplo-, strepto-, staphylo-)
  • Describe the Gram staining technique and what it reveals
  • Compare Gram-positive and Gram-negative cell walls

What are Eubacteria?
MoE Textbook §2.2.1, p. 34
📌 Eubacteria (MoE Textbook, p. 34)

Eubacteria literally means "true bacteria." They include all bacteria (except for archaea). Bacteria are relatively simple, single-celled (unicellular) organisms. Because their genetic material is not enclosed in a special nuclear membrane, bacterial cells are called prokaryotes.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
ProkaryotesUnicellular organisms that lack a nuclear membrane-enclosed nucleus
EukaryotesOrganisms whose cells have a nucleus enclosed within a nuclear envelope
UnicellularAn organism that has just one cell
MulticellularAn organism that has more than one cell

The Prokaryotic Cell — Structure

A bacterial cell has several distinct structures, each with a specific function:

  • Cell wall (peptidoglycan) — rigid outer layer; protects the cell and maintains shape
  • Cell membrane — controls what enters and leaves the cell
  • Cytoplasm — the gel-like interior where metabolic reactions occur
  • Nucleoid — the region containing the bacterial chromosome (single circular DNA molecule)
  • Plasmid — small circular DNA molecule carrying extra genes (often antibiotic resistance)
  • Ribosomes — sites of protein synthesis
  • Flagella — long, tail-like appendages used for movement
  • Pili — short hair-like projections used for attachment and DNA transfer (conjugation)
  • Slime capsule (glycocalyx) — outer protective layer that helps bacteria stick to surfaces and evade the immune system
📝 Prokaryotes vs Eukaryotes

The key difference is the nucleus. Prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea) have no membrane-bound nucleus — their DNA floats freely in the cytoplasm. Eukaryotes (animals, plants, fungi, protozoa) have a true nucleus that encloses their DNA. Prokaryotes are also typically much smaller and simpler than eukaryotes.

Where Do Bacteria Live?

  • Millions of bacteria exist in every environment
  • Inside and outside other organisms (including humans)
  • Cyanobacteria — have bluish pigments (in addition to chlorophyll) that enable photosynthesis
  • Some bacteria are harmful, but most serve useful purposes
  • They support many forms of plant and animal life, and are used in industrial and medicinal processes
📝 Fun fact

Bacteria are thought to have been the first organisms to appear on Earth, about 4 billion years ago. The oldest known fossils are of bacteria-like organisms. Cyanobacteria, one group of eubacteria, produced the oxygen that made animal life possible.

🔷
Bacterial Shapes
Three main forms

Bacteria are classified based on numerous features, including cell shape, nature of multi-cell aggregates, motility, formation of spores, and reaction to Gram stain. The three main shapes are:

⚪
Cocci

(singular: coccus)
Spherical bacteria. Examples: Streptococcus, Staphylococcus.

🟫
Bacilli

(singular: bacillus)
Rod-shaped bacteria. Examples: E. coli, Lactobacillus, Bacillus subtilis.

🌀
Spirochaetes

(singular: spirochaete)
Spiral or corkscrew-shaped bacteria. Examples: Treponema pallidum (syphilis), Borrelia (Lyme disease).

Clustering Patterns
How bacterial cells group together

Many bacteria naturally cluster together after cell division — this is another way to classify them. The clustering patterns are:

PatternDescriptionExample
Diplo-Pairs of cellsDiplococcus
Strepto-Chains of cellsStreptococcus
Staphylo-Grape-like clustersStaphylococcus
Tetra-Groups of fourVarious cocci
Sarcina-Cubical packets of eightSarcina
📝 Why clustering matters

Clustering patterns are used in medical diagnosis. For example, if a sample from a patient shows grape-like clusters of cocci, it's likely a Staphylococcus infection. If the cocci form chains, it's likely Streptococcus. This helps doctors quickly narrow down the cause of an infection.

🎨
Gram Staining
The most important diagnostic stain

Besides shape, bacteria can be classified by their cell wall composition. One very important method is the Gram stain, developed by Hans Christian Gram in 1884.

📌 Gram's Staining

Gram's staining is a test for distinguishing bacteria. It uses two different dyes — a purple one (crystal violet) and a red one (safranin) — and reveals the structure of the bacterial cell wall.

The Two Groups

TypeColor after Gram stainCell wallExamples
Gram-positive Purple Thick peptidoglycan layer — retains crystal violet stain All staphylococci, all streptococci, some Listeria species
Gram-negative Red / pink Thin peptidoglycan layer — loses crystal violet, takes up the red counterstain E. coli, Salmonella, Vibrio cholerae

How the Gram Stain Works

  • Step 1: Apply crystal violet (purple dye) to the bacterial smear — all cells stain purple
  • Step 2: Add iodine — forms a complex with crystal violet inside the cell
  • Step 3: Wash with alcohol (decolorizer) — Gram-positive cells retain the purple; Gram-negative cells lose it
  • Step 4: Apply safranin (red counterstain) — Gram-negative cells turn red
  • Result: Gram-positive = purple; Gram-negative = red/pink
📝 Why Gram staining is a "differential stain"

Differential staining is a staining procedure that distinguishes organisms based on their staining properties. Because the Gram stain divides bacteria into two distinct groups (purple vs red), it's called a differential stain. This is one of the first tests done in clinical labs to identify bacteria.

🧱
Cell Wall Structure
Peptidoglycan — the key to Gram staining
📌 Peptidoglycan

Peptidoglycan is the rigid layer of the cell walls of bacteria. It is a thin sheet composed of N-acetylglucosamine, N-acetylmuramic acid, and a few amino acids.

Gram-Positive vs Gram-Negative Cell Walls

FeatureGram-PositiveGram-Negative
Peptidoglycan layerThickThin
Outer membraneAbsentPresent (contains lipopolysaccharide, or LPS)
Periplasmic spaceSmallLarge
Teichoic acidsPresentAbsent
Gram stain resultPurpleRed / pink
Susceptibility to antibioticsMore susceptible to penicillinMore resistant to penicillin

Key Cell Wall Terms

  • Outer membrane — additional lipid bilayer found only in Gram-negative bacteria; contains porins and lipopolysaccharides
  • Periplasmic space — the space between the cell membrane and the outer membrane; contains enzymes for nutrient processing
  • Envelope — the entire outer covering of a bacterial cell (membrane + wall + any outer layers)
  • Teichoic acid — found in Gram-positive cell walls; may help with attachment and cell wall maintenance
  • Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) — the endotoxin found in Gram-negative outer membrane; causes fever and shock in severe infections
  • Porin proteins — channel proteins in the outer membrane that allow small molecules to pass through
  • Adhesion site — specific regions on the cell surface for attachment to host cells or surfaces
📝 Why this matters in medicine

Gram-negative bacteria have an extra outer membrane, which makes them more resistant to many antibiotics (like penicillin) and better protected from the immune system. This is why Gram-negative infections are often harder to treat than Gram-positive ones.

📝 Activity 2.4 — Looking at bacteria in yoghurt
Examine yoghurt under a microscope to see bacteria. You'll need:
  • Clean glass slides and cover glasses
  • Compound microscope
  • Methylene blue stain
  • A sample of yoghurt
Method:
  • Take a small sample of yoghurt with a pipette and place on a slide
  • Dilute with a drop of water if too thick
  • Lower a coverslip onto the sample
  • Observe at low power (×100) first, then switch to highest power
  • Identify bacteria by their shape (cocci, bacilli, spirals) and arrangement (pairs, chains, clusters)
  • Draw the different bacteria you can see

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 2.2
What you should remember
  • Eubacteria ("true bacteria") are prokaryotes — single-celled organisms without a membrane-bound nucleus
  • Bacteria were the first organisms on Earth, appearing about 4 billion years ago
  • The three main shapes are cocci (spherical), bacilli (rod-shaped), and spirochaetes (spiral)
  • Clustering patterns include diplo- (pairs), strepto- (chains), and staphylo- (clusters)
  • Gram staining divides bacteria into Gram-positive (purple — thick peptidoglycan) and Gram-negative (red — thin peptidoglycan with outer membrane)
  • Gram-negative bacteria have an outer membrane with lipopolysaccharide (LPS), making them more resistant to antibiotics
