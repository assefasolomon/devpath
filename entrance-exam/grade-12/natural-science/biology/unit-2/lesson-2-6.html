🧬 Viruses — The Edge of Life

Viruses are the most mysterious of all microorganisms. They are not truly alive — they cannot reproduce on their own, cannot carry out metabolism, and can even be crystallized like a chemical. Yet they infect every form of life on Earth, from bacteria to humans, and cause some of the most devastating diseases in history.

Introduction
What you will learn

Viruses are strange. They have genetic material (DNA or RNA) like living things, but they don't have cells, don't have a metabolism, and can't reproduce by themselves. They are obligate intracellular parasites — they must hijack a living cell's machinery to copy themselves. Because they occupy this strange boundary, scientists say viruses are "in between living and non-living."

  • Describe the structure of a virus
  • Identify different shapes and symmetry types
  • Compare DNA and RNA viruses

What are Viruses?
MoE Textbook §2.3, p. 55
📌 Virus (MoE Textbook, p. 55)

A virus is a very small, non-cellular parasite of cells. Its genome — composed of either DNA or RNA — is enclosed in a protein coat.

📌 A virus cannot reproduce by itself

A virus is a small parasite particle that cannot reproduce by itself. Once it infects a susceptible cell, however, a virus can direct the host cell machinery to produce more viruses.

Characteristics of Viruses

  • Have an inner core of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein coat known as the capsid
  • Most viruses range in size from 20–250 nm (nanometers)
  • Are inert outside their host cell
  • Are obligate intracellular parasites and filterable agents
  • Occupy a space between living and non-living — they can be crystallized and are non-living outside a host
  • Depend fully on the host cell machinery — metabolically inefficient
  • Are responsible for a number of dreadful diseases in humans and plants
📝 Living or non-living?

Viruses are often described as being "on the edge of life." They have genetic material like living things, but they:

• Cannot reproduce by themselves
• Do not carry out metabolism
• Do not grow or respond to stimuli
• Can be crystallized (a property of chemicals, not living things)

So they are best thought of as biological particles that must infect a living cell to reproduce.

🔬
Structure of Viruses
Simple, but effective

A virus is remarkably simple — it has just a few essential parts. The most basic structure is a nucleic acid core (either DNA or RNA, but not both) surrounded by a protein coat called the capsid.

The Four Basic Components

ComponentDescription
1. Genome Either DNA or RNA (not both). Can be linear or circular, single-stranded (ss) or double-stranded (ds).
2. Capsid The outer protein coat — protects the genome and helps introduce it into the host cell. Made of subunits called capsomeres, each composed of protomers.
3. Envelope Some viruses have an outer lipid envelope (acquired from the host cell membrane during budding). Contains glycoprotein spikes (peplomers).
4. Enzymes Some viruses carry enzymes to help with infection. Example: reverse transcriptase in retroviruses.

Enveloped vs Non-enveloped Viruses

🔴
Enveloped Viruses

Have an outer lipid envelope made of glycoprotein and phospholipid bilayer, surrounding the capsid. Envelope has spikes for host cell binding. Examples: HIV, influenza, coronaviruses.

🟢
Non-enveloped Viruses

Have only the capsid around the nucleic acid — no outer envelope. Called "naked viruses." Examples: adenovirus, poliovirus, papillomavirus.

Key Structural Terms

  • Capsomere — the morphological subunit of the capsid
  • Protomer — the smaller subunit that composes a capsomere
  • Nucleocapsid — the complete complex of nucleic acid + capsid
  • Peplomer — the glycoprotein spike projecting from the envelope
  • Hemagglutinin and Neuraminidase — specific peplomers involved in binding the virus to host cells (found on influenza virus)
📝 The envelope — a clever trick

Enveloped viruses get their outer coat by budding out of the host cell — they steal a piece of the host's cell membrane and use it as their own protective layer. This helps them evade the immune system. But it also means enveloped viruses are often more fragile — they dry out easily, so they spread mainly through direct contact or droplets.

🔷
Shapes & Symmetry
Different forms of viruses

The protein subunits of the capsid can assemble into different patterns, giving viruses different shapes. There are three main types of symmetry:

🌀
Helical Symmetry

Protein subunits arrange in a helical structure around the genome. Forms a rigid, flexible rod. Example: Tobacco Mosaic Virus.

💠
Icosahedral Symmetry

A 20-faced polyhedron with 12 vertices — rigid and protective. Examples: adenovirus, picornavirus, papovavirus.

🔬
Complex Symmetry

Complex structures with multiple components. Example: bacteriophages — have a head (with DNA), a tail sheath, and tail fibers.

📝 Bacteriophages — virus "moon landers"

Bacteriophages (viruses that infect bacteria) have the most complex structure. They look like tiny lunar landers with:

• Head — icosahedral capsid containing DNA
• Tail sheath — protein tube for injecting DNA
• Tail fibers — for attaching to bacterial cells

DNA vs RNA Viruses
Two classes of viral genomes
📌 The genetic material of a virus can be either DNA or RNA

Viruses that contain DNA as their genetic material are called DNA viruses. Those that contain RNA are called RNA viruses. Unlike other living cells, where ds DNA is always the genetic material, a viral genome may consist of linear or circular ds DNA, single-stranded DNA, ss linear RNA, or ds linear RNA.

Retroviruses

📌 Retrovirus

A retrovirus is an RNA virus that converts its genetic information from RNA into DNA after it has infected a host. It does this using an enzyme called reverse transcriptase (RNA-dependent DNA polymerase).

  • Retroviruses carry reverse transcriptase in their capsid
  • After entering the host cell, the enzyme converts viral RNA into DNA
  • The new DNA integrates into the host's genome
  • The host cell then transcribes and translates this DNA to produce new viral components
  • The most famous retrovirus is HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus)
📝 Why RNA viruses mutate faster

RNA viruses have a higher mutation rate than DNA viruses because the enzymes that copy RNA (RNA polymerases) make more mistakes than those that copy DNA. This is why it's so hard to make a vaccine against RNA viruses like influenza and HIV — they change so quickly.

🔄
Viral Reproduction
Six stages of infection

To replicate, viruses need to infect a host cell and hijack the host machinery to make many copies of the viral genome and lots of viral proteins — which are then assembled into new virus particles. There are six main stages in a typical virus replication cycle:

1. Attachment — The virus attaches to a specific receptor on the host cell surface.
2. Penetration — The virus enters the host cell (through the cell membrane or by being engulfed).
3. Uncoating — The viral genome is released from its capsid inside the host cell.
4. Replication — The viral genome is copied, and viral proteins are produced using the host's machinery.
5. Assembly — New viral components are assembled into complete virus particles.
6. Release — New virus particles exit the host cell (often by budding or by lysing the cell) — ready to infect new cells.

Lytic vs Lysogenic Cycle (in Bacteriophages)

  • Lytic cycle: The virus immediately uses the host cell to produce new viruses, then bursts the cell open (lysis), releasing many new viral particles
  • Lysogenic cycle: The viral DNA integrates into the host's chromosome and remains dormant, being copied along with the host DNA each time the cell divides — until stress triggers it to switch to the lytic cycle
🎗️
HIV & AIDS
The retrovirus that changed the world
📌 HIV — Human Immunodeficiency Virus

HIV is a virus that attacks the body's immune system. If HIV is not treated, it can lead to AIDS (Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome).

How HIV Works

1. HIV binds to CD4 receptors on helper T-cells of the immune system.
2. The virus enters the cell and uncoats, releasing its RNA genome.
3. Using reverse transcriptase, HIV makes a DNA copy of its RNA genome.
4. The DNA integrates into the host cell's chromosome.
5. The host cell produces new viral components and eventually new virus particles.
6. New viruses bud out and infect other cells — especially other T-cells.

From HIV to AIDS

  • HIV destroys CD4+ T-cells — critical to the immune system
  • As T-cells are destroyed, the body becomes immunodeficient
  • When CD4 count drops below a threshold, the person is said to have AIDS
  • The person becomes vulnerable to opportunistic infections — diseases that wouldn't normally cause illness in a healthy person
  • Death usually results from these secondary infections, not from HIV itself

Transmission of HIV

  • Unprotected sexual contact — the most common route worldwide
  • Blood-to-blood contact — sharing needles, contaminated blood transfusions
  • Mother to child — during pregnancy, birth, or breastfeeding
  • NOT transmitted by — hugging, shaking hands, sharing food, mosquito bites

Treatment & Prevention

  • Antiretroviral therapy (ART) — combination of drugs that suppress HIV, allowing the immune system to recover
  • ART does not cure HIV but allows people to live long, healthy lives
  • Prevention: condom use, sterile needles, testing, education, and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)
  • Prevention of mother-to-child transmission — ART for pregnant women
📝 HIV in Ethiopia

HIV/AIDS remains a significant public health issue in Ethiopia. According to serosurveys, Ethiopia has high prevalence of hepatitis B virus, and there are indications of high transmission for hepatitis C, hepatitis E, and HIV. National programs focus on testing, treatment, prevention, and education — especially for young people.

📝 Why we study viruses

Understanding viruses is essential for:

• Developing vaccines and antiviral drugs
• Diagnosing infections quickly (using PCR and other molecular tools)
• Tracking outbreaks and controlling spread
• Understanding how viruses evolve and how they cause disease

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 2.6
What you should remember
  • Viruses are non-cellular parasites of cells — they have DNA or RNA enclosed in a protein coat (capsid)
  • They are "on the edge of life" — they cannot reproduce on their own or carry out metabolism
  • Four basic components: genome, capsid, envelope (some), enzymes (some)
  • Three symmetry types: helical (TMV), icosahedral (adenovirus), complex (bacteriophages)
  • DNA viruses replicate in the nucleus and are more stable; RNA viruses replicate in cytoplasm and mutate faster
  • Retroviruses (like HIV) convert RNA → DNA using reverse transcriptase
  • Six stages of viral reproduction: attachment, penetration, uncoating, replication, assembly, release
  • HIV attacks CD4+ helper T-cells, leading to AIDS if untreated
  • Common viral diseases in Ethiopia: hepatitis B/C/E, HIV, polio, measles, rubella, mumps, rotavirus, yellow fever, rabies
