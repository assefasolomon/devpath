📉 Analysis of Frequency Distributions

Go beyond numbers and learn to describe the shape of a distribution. Master symmetry, skewness, kurtosis, and the identification of outliers using the quartile-based rules. Every dataset tells a story — this lesson teaches you to read it.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources

Introduction
What you will learn in this lesson

Two distributions can have the same mean and standard deviation but completely different shapes. One might be symmetric, another skewed left or right. Some have long tails, others are tightly peaked. Shape analysis reveals whether the data is balanced, whether outliers exist, and where the bulk of values lies.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Identify symmetric, positively skewed and negatively skewed distributions
  • Determine the direction of skew using the mean-median-mode relationship
  • Compute and interpret the quartile coefficient of skewness
  • Identify outliers using the 1.5×IQR rule
  • Describe the shape of a distribution using these measures
  • Choose the appropriate measure of central tendency based on the shape
📖
Explanation
Complete theory with visual aids

2.1 The Three Basic Shapes

Every distribution has a shape determined by where the data clusters and how long the tails are.

Symmetric
Both tails are equal length. Balanced around the centre.
Right-Skewed (Positive)
Long tail on the right. Mode < median < mean.
Left-Skewed (Negative)
Long tail on the left. Mean < median < mode.
📌 The Mean-Median-Mode Skew Rule

• Mean = Median = Mode → Distribution is symmetric
• Mean > Median > Mode → Distribution is right-skewed (positive)
• Mean < Median < Mode → Distribution is left-skewed (negative)

This rule works because the mean is pulled toward the long tail, while the mode stays near the peak.

📝 Which measure should you use?

• Symmetric distribution → use the mean (most informative)
• Skewed distribution → use the median (more robust to outliers)
• Categorical data → use the mode

2.2 Pearson's Coefficient of Skewness

The direction of skewness can be quantified using Pearson's coefficient. It measures how far the mean is from the mode, relative to the standard deviation.

📌 Pearson's Coefficient of Skewness

Sk = (Mean − Mode) / σ

Or, using the median (more common):

Sk = 3(Mean − Median) / σ

Interpretation:
• Sk = 0 → symmetric
• Sk > 0 → right-skewed (positive)
• Sk < 0 → left-skewed (negative)
• |Sk| > 1 → highly skewed

2.3 Quartile Coefficient of Skewness

When the extreme values may distort the mean and mode, the quartile coefficient gives a more robust measure of skew based on the quartiles.

📌 Quartile Coefficient of Skewness

Q_sk = (Q3 − 2Q2 + Q1) / (Q3 − Q1)

where Q1, Q2 (median), Q3 are the first, second and third quartiles.

Interpretation:
• Q_sk = 0 → symmetric
• Q_sk > 0 → right-skewed
• Q_sk < 0 → left-skewed

📝 When to use which

• Pearson's Sk: useful when you have mean, median and SD — especially for larger samples.
• Quartile Q_sk: robust to outliers — use it when the dataset may contain extreme values.

2.4 Identifying Outliers — The 1.5×IQR Rule

An outlier is a data value that lies far from the rest. A standard method for identifying outliers uses the interquartile range (IQR).

📌 The 1.5×IQR Rule

IQR = Q3 − Q1

Lower fence = Q1 − 1.5 × IQR
Upper fence = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR

Any value below the lower fence or above the upper fence is considered an outlier.

📝 Why 1.5×IQR?

In a symmetric distribution (normal curve), 1.5×IQR is roughly 2 standard deviations from the mean. Points beyond this range are statistically unusual. The IQR is preferred because it's robust to extreme values, unlike the range.

2.5 Kurtosis (Peakedness)

Kurtosis measures how peaked or flat a distribution is compared to the normal curve.

Leptokurtic
Tall, narrow peak with heavy tails. More values cluster near the mean, but more extreme outliers too.
Mesokurtic
Normal peak — same shape as the standard normal curve. Reference shape.
Platykurtic
Flat, wide peak with light tails. Values spread more evenly across the range.

Kurtosis is rarely tested extensively at Grade 12, but knowing the three terms prepares you for related questions.

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Identifying Skew from Mean and Median
Problem: A dataset has Mean = 45, Median = 42, Mode = 38. Describe the shape of the distribution.
Compare
Mean (45) > Median (42) > Mode (38).
Interpret
Since Mean > Median > Mode, the distribution is right-skewed (positively skewed) — the long tail is on the right.
✅ The distribution is right-skewed (positive skew).
Example 2Computing Pearson's Coefficient of Skewness
Problem: A dataset has Mean = 70, Median = 65, σ = 10. Compute Pearson's coefficient of skewness.
Formula
Use Sk = 3(Mean − Median) / σ
Substitute
Sk = 3(70 − 65) / 10 = 15 / 10 = 1.5
Interpret
Sk = +1.5 → the distribution is right-skewed. Since |Sk| > 1, the skew is strong.
✅ Sk = 1.5 — strongly right-skewed.
Example 3Quartile Coefficient of Skewness
Problem: For a distribution, Q1 = 40, Q2 = 50, Q3 = 65. Compute the quartile coefficient of skewness and interpret.
Formula
Q_sk = (Q3 − 2Q2 + Q1) / (Q3 − Q1)
Numerator
Q3 − 2Q2 + Q1 = 65 − 100 + 40 = 5
Denominator
Q3 − Q1 = 65 − 40 = 25
Result
Q_sk = 5 / 25 = 0.2 — positive → right-skewed.
✅ Q_sk = 0.2 — mildly right-skewed.
Example 4Identifying Outliers Using 1.5×IQR
Problem: A dataset has Q1 = 30, Q3 = 50. A value of 90 appears. Is it an outlier?
IQR
IQR = 50 − 30 = 20
Fences
Lower = 30 − 1.5(20) = 0
Upper = 50 + 1.5(20) = 80
Compare
Value 90 > 80 (upper fence). So 90 is above the upper fence.
✅ Yes — 90 is an outlier (above the upper fence of 80).
Example 5Choosing Between Mean and Median
Problem: A researcher collects income data for a city. The mean income is 8,000 ETB, but the median is 4,500 ETB. Which measure better represents "typical" income? Why?
Compare
Mean (8,000) > Median (4,500). This indicates a right-skewed distribution — a few very high incomes pull the mean upward.
Interpret
The mean is distorted by the high-income earners. The median represents the middle earner — where 50% of people earn less, 50% earn more.
Recommendation
For skewed data like income, the median is the better measure of "typical".
✅ Use the median (4,500 ETB) — it reflects the typical earner better than the mean, which is inflated by outliers.

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 5
✅ Key Points — Analysis of Frequency Distributions
  • Distributions have three basic shapes: symmetric, right-skewed (positive) and left-skewed (negative).
  • Mean-median-mode relationship: mean = median = mode → symmetric. Mean > median > mode → right skew. Mean < median < mode → left skew.
  • Pearson's coefficient of skewness: Sk = 3(Mean − Median)/σ. Positive → right skew, negative → left skew.
  • Quartile coefficient of skewness: Q_sk = (Q3 − 2Q2 + Q1)/(Q3 − Q1). Robust to outliers.
  • IQR = Q3 − Q1. The middle 50% spread of the data.
  • Outlier rule: a value is an outlier if below Q1 − 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR.
  • Kurtosis describes peakedness: leptokurtic (tall peak), mesokurtic (normal), platykurtic (flat).
  • Choose the median for skewed data (resistant to outliers); the mean for symmetric data; the mode for categorical data.
