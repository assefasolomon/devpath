Unit 8 · Further on Statistics · Social Science

🎯 Sampling Techniques

Before any statistical analysis can begin, data must be collected correctly. Learn the four major sampling methods, understand when each is appropriate, calculate sample sizes for stratified and systematic samples, and recognise sampling bias — all topics tested directly on the Ethiopian entrance exam.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
✏️ Examples: 5 worked
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn in this lesson

A survey of every person in a population — called a census — is ideal but often impossible. Ethiopia has over 120 million people; interviewing every one for a health study would take years and cost billions. Instead, we select a sample — a smaller, carefully chosen group that represents the whole. The method of selecting that group is critical: a bad sampling method produces misleading results no matter how sophisticated the analysis.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Distinguish between a population, a sample and a census
  • Explain the four main sampling techniques: simple random, systematic, stratified and cluster
  • Calculate the sampling interval for systematic sampling: k = N/n
  • Calculate stratum sample sizes for stratified sampling: nᵢ = (Nᵢ/N) × n
  • Identify sampling bias and explain why it makes results unrepresentative
  • Choose the most appropriate method for a given real-world context

Explanation
Complete theory with examples

2.1 Key Terms

Population (N)
The entire group being studied. Example: all secondary school students in Ethiopia.
Sample (n)
A selected subset of the population used to represent the whole. Should be large enough and unbiased.
Census
Data collected from every member of the population. Accurate but expensive and time-consuming.
Sampling Frame
The complete list of all members of the population from which the sample is drawn.
Sampling Bias
A systematic error where the sample does not fairly represent the population, leading to misleading conclusions.
Representative Sample
A sample whose characteristics closely match those of the population it is drawn from.

2.2 The Four Main Sampling Techniques

📌 1. Simple Random Sampling

Every member of the population has an equal probability of being selected. Members are chosen using a random number table, a lottery, or a random number generator.

How: Number all N members. Use random numbers to select n members.
Best for: Small, homogeneous populations with a complete sampling frame available.

✓ Advantages
  • Unbiased — every member has equal chance
  • Simple to understand
  • Results are statistically valid
✗ Disadvantages
  • Requires a complete list of the population
  • Can miss subgroups if sample is small
  • Impractical for large, geographically spread populations
📌 2. Systematic Sampling

Select every kth member from a list after a random starting point.

Sampling interval: k = N ÷ n (round to nearest whole number)
How: Choose a random start between 1 and k, then select every kth member.
Best for: Large populations with a complete ordered list.

✓ Advantages
  • Easy to carry out once the list exists
  • Spread evenly across the list
  • Quick to implement
✗ Disadvantages
  • Can introduce bias if the list has a hidden pattern at interval k
  • Still requires a complete ordered list
📌 3. Stratified Sampling

Divide the population into non-overlapping subgroups called strata (e.g. by gender, age group, region), then randomly sample from each stratum in proportion to its size.

Stratum sample size: nᵢ = (Nᵢ / N) × n
where Nᵢ = stratum size, N = population size, n = total sample size.

Best for: Heterogeneous populations where subgroups must all be represented.

✓ Advantages
  • Ensures all subgroups are represented
  • More precise than simple random for heterogeneous populations
✗ Disadvantages
  • Requires knowledge of population subgroup sizes
  • More complex to organise
📌 4. Cluster Sampling

Divide the population into clusters (groups — e.g. schools, villages, districts), randomly select a number of clusters, then survey every member in the selected clusters.

Best for: Populations spread over a large geographic area where visiting every location is impractical.

✓ Advantages
  • Very practical for geographically dispersed populations
  • Reduces travel/data-collection costs
✗ Disadvantages
  • Less precise — members within a cluster may be similar
  • Results depend on which clusters were selected

2.4 Sampling Bias

📌 Sampling Bias

Sampling bias occurs when the sampling method systematically favours some members of the population over others, making the sample unrepresentative.

Common sources of bias:
• Convenience sampling: selecting whoever is easiest to reach (e.g. only interviewing friends)
• Voluntary response bias: people who feel strongly are more likely to respond
• Undercoverage: some groups have little or no chance of being selected

⚠ Exam tip

The entrance exam often presents a scenario and asks you to identify the sampling method used OR to identify whether bias is present. Always ask: "Does every member of the population have a fair chance of being selected?" If not, bias exists.

Worked Examples
5 exam-style problems solved step by step
Example 1Identifying the Sampling Method
Problem: A researcher wants to study the reading habits of 1,000 university students. She numbers all students 1–1000 and uses a random number generator to select 50 of them. What sampling method is this?
Analysis
Every student has been numbered (sampling frame exists). Students are selected using a random process. Every student has an equal chance of being selected.
✅ This is Simple Random Sampling.
Example 2Systematic Sampling — Finding the Interval and Selecting the Sample
Problem: A factory has 480 workers on a payroll list. The quality control team wants a sample of 60. Describe how to use systematic sampling. If the random start is worker number 5, list the first four workers selected.
Interval
k = N/n = 480/60 = 8. Select every 8th worker.
First four
Start at 5, add 8 each time: workers 5, 13, 21, 29, ...
✅ k = 8. First four selected: workers 5, 13, 21, 29.
Example 3Stratified Sampling — Calculating Stratum Sizes
Problem: A school has 600 students: 240 Grade 10, 200 Grade 11 and 160 Grade 12. A stratified sample of 60 students is required. How many should be selected from each grade?
Grade 10
n₁₀ = (240/600) × 60 = 0.4 × 60 = 24
Grade 11
n₁₁ = (200/600) × 60 = 0.333 × 60 = 20
Grade 12
n₁₂ = (160/600) × 60 = 0.267 × 60 = 16
Check
24 + 20 + 16 = 60 ✓
✅ Grade 10: 24 · Grade 11: 20 · Grade 12: 16
Example 4Cluster Sampling Scenario
Problem: A health researcher wants to study living conditions across 200 villages in a rural region. Visiting all 200 is too costly. She randomly selects 20 villages and surveys every household in those 20 villages. What method is this and why is it appropriate?
Method
The villages are clusters. She selected a random sample of clusters (20 villages) and surveyed all members within them.
Why appropriate
The population is geographically dispersed across 200 villages. Cluster sampling drastically reduces travel cost and time while still allowing a random element (random cluster selection).
✅ Cluster Sampling — appropriate because the population is geographically spread and visiting all villages is impractical.
Example 5Identifying Sampling Bias
Problem: To find the average monthly income of residents in a city, a researcher surveys 200 people at a luxury shopping mall on a weekday afternoon. Identify the type of bias and explain its effect.
Bias type
Convenience sampling bias — the researcher selected whoever was easiest to reach (people at a mall on a weekday afternoon).
Who is excluded
People who work during the day, low-income residents (unlikely to shop at a luxury mall), elderly or disabled residents who cannot visit a mall, and rural residents.
Effect
The sample overrepresents higher-income, urban, working-age mall shoppers. The estimated average income will be significantly higher than the true city average.
✅ Convenience/undercoverage bias — the sample overrepresents higher-income groups, making the income estimate unrepresentatively high.

Lesson Summary
Everything you learned in Lesson 1
✅ Key Points — Sampling Techniques
  • A population is the complete group; a sample is a selected subset. A census surveys the entire population.
  • Simple random sampling: every member has an equal chance of selection. Most unbiased but requires a complete list.
  • Systematic sampling: select every kth member. Interval k = N ÷ n. Choose a random start between 1 and k.
  • Stratified sampling: divide into strata and sample each proportionally. Stratum size nᵢ = (Nᵢ/N) × n. All strata are represented.
  • Cluster sampling: randomly select whole clusters (villages, schools) and survey everyone within them. Best for geographically spread populations.
  • Sampling bias occurs when the method systematically favours some members, making the sample unrepresentative.
  • Common bias sources: convenience sampling, voluntary response, undercoverage of certain groups.
  • To check if a method is biased: ask whether every member of the population has a fair chance of being selected.
