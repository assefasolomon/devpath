⛪ Religious Reforms & Power Struggle (1906–1935)

How did emperors use religion to unify the state? And how did internal rivalries shape the succession after Menelik II's death?

📖 Reading: ~20 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎯
Introduction

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, Ethiopian emperors used religious reforms to strengthen national unity — and faced intense internal power struggles. This lesson covers two critical themes: the religious reforms of the emperors, and the political rivalries that followed Menelik II's decline.

⛪
Religious Reforms

Ethiopia has been a predominantly Christian state since the 4th century, but it also has significant Muslim and traditional religious communities. Emperors used religious policy to build national unity and assert central authority.

  • Tewodros II attempted to reform the church and reduce its political power, but faced resistance.
  • Yohannes IV promoted Orthodox Christianity as the state religion but allowed religious freedom for Muslims and others.
  • Menelik II continued the tradition of state-supported Orthodox Christianity while expanding the empire into non-Christian regions (south and east). His approach was pragmatic — respecting local customs while promoting national unity.
  • Haile Selassie I (after 1930) continued this pattern, balancing Orthodox Christianity with modernization.
📝 The role of the church

The Ethiopian Orthodox Church was a powerful institution — with vast landholdings, political influence, and control over education. Emperors who tried to limit the church's power often faced its opposition, while those who allied with it gained legitimacy.

⚔️
Power Struggle (1906–1935)

Menelik II suffered a stroke in 1906 and became increasingly incapacitated. The question of succession became a bitter political struggle between different factions — the nobility, the church, and Menelik's family.

  • 1906 — Menelik suffers a stroke; his wife Empress Taytu takes effective control.
  • 1909 — Menelik formally names his grandson Lij Iyasu as heir, but he is young and inexperienced.
  • 1913 — Menelik dies; Lij Iyasu becomes emperor.
  • 1916 — Lij Iyasu is deposed by nobles and the church for alleged conversion to Islam and for being too young. Empress Zewditu (Menelik's daughter) is crowned empress; Ras Tafari Makonnen (later Haile Selassie) becomes regent and heir.
  • 1928–1930 — Ras Tafari consolidates power; Zewditu dies in 1930, and Tafari is crowned Haile Selassie I, Emperor of Ethiopia.

This period (1906–1935) was marked by intense political rivalry, but it also saw the modernization of the Ethiopian state, the establishment of Addis Ababa as a modern capital, and the gradual emergence of Haile Selassie as a modernizing ruler.
