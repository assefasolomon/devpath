🐫 Long Distance Trade & Power Rivalry

Before Ethiopia became a unified modern state, it was a patchwork of kingdoms connected by long-distance trade routes. This lesson explores that trade, and the emperors who fought to reunify the country.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎯
Introduction

In the mid-19th century, Ethiopia was divided into several regional kingdoms — Gondar, Shewa, Tigray, Gojjam, Wollo, and others — each with its own ruler. Long-distance trade routes connected these regions to the coast and to the wider world. Then, beginning in 1855, a series of ambitious emperors — Tewodros II, Yohannes IV, and Menelik II — began to reunify the country and consolidate central power.

  • Describe the long-distance trade of 19th-century Ethiopia
  • Identify the main trade routes and commodities
  • Explain the power rivalry among regional lords
  • Summarize the rule of Tewodros II, Yohannes IV, and Menelik II
💱
Long Distance Trade

Long distance trade refers to the exchange of goods between distant regions — often across deserts, mountains, and seas — by professional merchants using caravans, ports, and established routes.

Exports (From Ethiopia)Imports (Into Ethiopia)
Coffee, ivory, gold, civet, hides, grain, cattleFirearms, textiles, salt, spices, metals, glassware
📝 Key Trade Towns

Gondar (commercial centre) · Massawa (Red Sea port) · Tadjura & Zeila (ports to Arabia) · Harar (eastern hub).

⚔️
Power Rivalry & Centralization

Between roughly 1769 and 1855, Ethiopia was fragmented into competing regional lordships. The emperor was a figurehead; real power lay with regional warlords. Historians call this period the Zemene Mesafint — the "Era of Princes."

By the mid-19th century, many Ethiopians wanted a strong central government that could end regional warfare, protect trade routes, defend against external threats (Egypt, Sudan, Europe), and modernize the state.

🔥
Emperor Tewodros II (r. 1855–1868)

Tewodros II ended the Zemene Mesafint by defeating rival lords and crowning himself emperor in 1855. He was the first emperor to attempt a comprehensive modernization of the Ethiopian state.

  • Centralized the state — abolished regional lordships
  • Reorganized the army — created a standing professional army
  • Sought modern technology — hired European missionaries and artisans

His reforms alienated the church and nobility. He imprisoned European missionaries, prompting Britain to send a military expedition. In 1868, British forces defeated his army at Maqdala, and Tewodros committed suicide rather than surrender.

⚔️
Emperor Yohannes IV (r. 1872–1889)

Yohannes IV became emperor in 1872. His reign was dominated by external threats — from Egypt, Sudan, and Italy.

  • Defeated Egypt at the Battle of Gundet (1875) and Gura (1876)
  • Defeated the Mahdist Sudan at the Battle of Metemma (1889) — but was killed in the battle

His death opened the way for Menelik II, the powerful ruler of Shewa, to become emperor.

👑
The Rise of Menelik II

Menelik II was the king of Shewa, the most powerful of the regional kingdoms. When Yohannes IV died in 1889, Menelik became emperor.

  • Founded Addis Ababa (1886)
  • Signed the Treaty of Wuchale with Italy (1889)
  • Defeated Italy at the Battle of Adwa (1896)
  • Expanded Ethiopia's territory to its modern boundaries

Summary
  • Long distance trade connected Ethiopia's interior to Red Sea and Gulf of Aden ports
  • Main exports: coffee, ivory, gold, civet, hides
  • The Zemene Mesafint (Era of Princes, c. 1769–1855) was a period of regional fragmentation
  • Tewodros II (r. 1855–1868) — first modernizer; committed suicide after British invasion at Maqdala
  • Yohannes IV (r. 1872–1889) — defended Ethiopia against Egypt and Mahdist Sudan; died at Metemma
  • Menelik II — rose from Shewa to become emperor; defeated Italy at Adwa (1896)
