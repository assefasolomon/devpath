Unit 3 · Ethiopia 1855–1941 · MoE Textbook §3.1–3.2

🐫 Long Distance Trade & Power Rivalry

Before Ethiopia became a unified modern state, it was a patchwork of kingdoms connected by long-distance trade routes. This lesson explores that trade, the Zemene Mesafint, and the emperors who fought to reunify the country.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

In the mid-19th century, Ethiopia was divided into several regional kingdoms — Gondar, Shewa, Tigray, Gojjam, Wollo, and others — each with its own ruler. Long-distance trade routes connected these regions to the coast and to the wider world. Then, beginning in 1855, a series of ambitious emperors — Tewodros II, Yohannes IV, and Menelik II — began to reunify the country and consolidate central power.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe the long-distance trade of 19th-century Ethiopia
  • Identify the main trade routes and commodities
  • Explain the power rivalry among regional lords (the Zemene Mesafint)
  • Summarize the rule of Tewodros II, Yohannes IV, and Menelik II
  • Explain how centralization was achieved
💱
Long Distance Trade
MoE Textbook §3.1
📌 Long Distance Trade

Long distance trade refers to the exchange of goods between distant regions — often across deserts, mountains, and seas — by professional merchants using caravans, ports, and established routes. In 19th-century Ethiopia, this trade connected the interior kingdoms to the Red Sea coast, the Sudan, and beyond.

Major Exports and Imports

Exports (From Ethiopia)Imports (Into Ethiopia)
Coffee, ivory, gold, civet, hides, grain, cattle Firearms, textiles, salt, spices, metals, glassware

Key Trade Towns

  • Gondar — major commercial centre in the north
  • Massawa — Red Sea port connecting to Arabia and India
  • Tadjura & Zeila — ports connecting to the Gulf of Aden
  • Harar — major trade hub in the east
  • Bonga, Jimma — centres of coffee and gold trade
⚔️
The Zemene Mesafint
Era of Princes · c. 1769–1855
📌 Zemene Mesafint (Era of Princes)

Between roughly 1769 and 1855, Ethiopia was fragmented into competing regional lordships. The emperor was a figurehead; real power lay with regional warlords (the rases). Historians call this period the Zemene Mesafint — the "Era of Princes" or "Age of Judges."

The Need for Centralization

  • End regional warfare and the constant feuding among warlords
  • Protect trade routes and expand commerce
  • Defend Ethiopia from external threats — Egypt, Sudan, European powers
  • Modernize the state with a stronger army and central administration
🔥
Emperor Tewodros II (r. 1855–1868)
The first modernizer

Tewodros II (born Kassa Hailu) ended the Zemene Mesafint by defeating rival lords and crowning himself emperor in 1855. He was the first emperor to attempt a comprehensive modernization of the Ethiopian state.

Major Reforms

  • Centralized the state — abolished regional lordships, appointed loyal governors
  • Reorganized the army — created a standing professional army
  • Attempted land reform — faced resistance from church and nobles
  • Sought modern technology — hired European missionaries and artisans
  • Promoted national unity — reduced power of regional lords and church

Tewodros's Downfall

  • His reforms alienated both the church and the nobility
  • He imprisoned European missionaries — prompting Britain to send a military expedition
  • In 1868, British forces under Robert Napier invaded, defeated his army at Maqdala, and Tewodros committed suicide rather than surrender
⚔️
Emperor Yohannes IV (r. 1872–1889)
Defender of the north

After a period of rivalry, Yohannes IV (born Kassa Mercha) became emperor in 1872. His reign was dominated by external threats — from Egypt, Sudan, and Italy — and by the challenge of holding together a still-fractured empire.

Key Achievements

  • Defeated Egypt at the Battle of Gundet (1875) and Gura (1876)
  • Defeated the Mahdist Sudan at the Battle of Metemma (1889) — but was killed in the battle
  • Consolidated the northern provinces — Tigray, Gondar, Gojjam, Wollo
  • Encouraged religious unity — promoted Orthodox Christianity
📝 A tragic end

Yohannes died at Metemma in 1889 from wounds sustained in battle against the Mahdist forces. His death opened the way for Menelik II, the powerful ruler of Shewa, to become emperor.

👑
The Rise of Menelik II
From Shewa to the throne

Menelik II was the king of Shewa, the most powerful of the regional kingdoms. He spent years consolidating Shewa and expanding its territory. When Yohannes IV died in 1889, Menelik became emperor — beginning a reign that would transform Ethiopia.

Menelik's Achievements (Preview)

  • Founded Addis Ababa (1886) as the new capital
  • Signed the Treaty of Wuchale with Italy (1889)
  • Defeated Italy at the Battle of Adwa (1896) — covered in the next lesson
  • Expanded Ethiopia's territory to its modern boundaries
  • Introduced modern infrastructure — roads, telegraph, the first banks

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 3.1
What you should remember
  • Long distance trade connected Ethiopia's interior to Red Sea and Gulf of Aden ports
  • Main exports: coffee, ivory, gold, civet, hides · Main imports: firearms, textiles, salt, spices
  • Key trade towns: Gondar, Massawa, Tadjura, Zeila, Harar
  • The Zemene Mesafint (Era of Princes, c. 1769–1855) was a period of regional fragmentation
  • Tewodros II (r. 1855–1868) — first modernizer; centralized state, modern army; committed suicide after British invasion at Maqdala (1868)
  • Yohannes IV (r. 1872–1889) — defended Ethiopia against Egypt (Gundet/Gura) and Mahdist Sudan; died at Metemma (1889)
  • Menelik II — rose from Shewa to become emperor after Yohannes's death; would go on to defeat Italy at Adwa (1896)
  • Centralization was a decades-long process that culminated in modern Ethiopia
