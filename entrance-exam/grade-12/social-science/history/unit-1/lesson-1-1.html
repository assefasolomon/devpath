⚙️ Development of Capitalism & the Industrial Revolution

The Industrial Revolution transformed the world economy between 1750 and 1914 — replacing agriculture and handicraft with machine-driven industry, reshaping societies, and laying the foundation for the modern global economy.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Between 1750 and 1914, the world witnessed the most dramatic economic transformation in human history. The Industrial Revolution began in Britain and spread across Europe and North America, replacing agriculture and handicraft with machine-driven industry. It created modern capitalism — a system that would dominate the global economy and reshape societies, politics, and empires.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define capitalism and its key features
  • Explain the origins of capitalism in Europe
  • Describe the Industrial Revolution and why it began in Britain
  • Identify the phases of industrialization
  • Analyse the economic, social, and political effects of industrialization
💰
What is Capitalism?
MoE Textbook §1.1.1
📌 Capitalism

Capitalism is an economic system in which the means of production (land, factories, machinery, capital) are privately owned and operated for profit. Production and distribution are determined by market forces — supply and demand — rather than by central planning or tradition.

Key Features of Capitalism

  • Private ownership of the means of production
  • Profit motive — production is organized around earning profit
  • Free market — prices are set by supply and demand
  • Competition among producers and sellers
  • Wage labour — workers sell their labour for wages
  • Capital accumulation — reinvesting profit to expand production
📝 Why this matters

Capitalism didn't appear overnight. It emerged gradually from the decline of feudalism in Europe — from a system where land and labour were controlled by lords and guilds, to one where individuals could own property, trade freely, and reinvest profit.

🌍
Origins of Capitalism
How Europe got there first

Capitalism emerged in Western Europe between the 15th and 18th centuries, driven by a combination of economic, political, and intellectual factors.

🏛️
Decline of Feudalism

The collapse of manorial agriculture and serfdom freed labour and land for market exchange.

🌍
Age of Exploration

Voyages from the 15th century opened global trade routes and brought immense wealth to Europe.

🏦
Banking & Finance

Italian city-states (Venice, Florence, Genoa) pioneered banking, credit, and insurance.

📜
Protestant Ethic

Weber argued Protestant values encouraged hard work, thrift, and investment.

⚖️
Enlightenment Ideas

Property rights, individual liberty, and free trade championed by Adam Smith, Locke, and others.

🚢
Colonial Trade

Colonial empires supplied raw materials, markets, and huge profits to European merchants.

📝 Adam Smith — The Wealth of Nations (1776)

Adam Smith is often called the father of capitalism. He argued that individuals pursuing their own self-interest are led, as if by an "invisible hand", to promote the general welfare of society — provided that markets are free and competitive.

⚙️
The Industrial Revolution
MoE Textbook §1.1.2
📌 The Industrial Revolution

The Industrial Revolution (c. 1750–1850) was a period of rapid technological, economic, and social change in which hand production methods were replaced by machine manufacturing, first in Britain and then across Europe and North America.

Why Britain?

Britain had a unique combination of conditions that no other European country matched at the time:

  • Coal and iron — abundant natural resources
  • Colonies — cheap raw materials and captive markets
  • Capital — profits from the slave trade, colonial trade, and banking
  • Labour — Enclosure Acts displaced rural workers, creating a huge factory workforce
  • Stable government — parliamentary system that protected property rights
  • Geography — island nation with navigable rivers and long coastline for trade
  • Agricultural revolution — new farming methods freed up food and labour for industry

📅
Phases of Industrialization
From textiles to steel and electricity
Phase 1 · 1750 – 1850
The First Industrial Revolution
Based on textiles, coal, iron, and steam power. Factories became the dominant mode of production. Britain led the world.
Phase 2 · 1850 – 1914
The Second Industrial Revolution
Based on steel, chemicals, electricity, and oil. Germany, the United States, and Japan industrialize rapidly. Mass production begins (assembly line).
Phase 3 · After 1945
The Third Industrial Revolution
Based on electronics, computers, and information technology. Later followed by automation and digital networks.

From Laissez-Faire to Monopoly Capitalism

In the second half of the 19th century, capitalism itself changed. Small competitive firms gave way to large corporations, trusts, and cartels. Banks and industry merged into finance capital. This was the era of monopoly capitalism — and of intense imperial rivalry as European powers scrambled for markets and raw materials.

📊
Effects of Industrialization
Economic, social, and political transformations

Economic Effects

  • Mass production — goods became cheaper and more abundant
  • Growth of factories and urban centres
  • New banking and credit systems
  • Railways and steamships shrunk distances — created a global market
  • Rise of joint-stock companies and later corporations
  • Imperial expansion for raw materials and markets

Social Effects

  • Urbanization — millions moved from villages to industrial cities
  • New social classes — industrial bourgeoisie (owners) vs. industrial proletariat (workers)
  • Child and female labour — widespread in early factories
  • Poor working conditions — long hours, low pay, dangerous machinery
  • Trade unions and labour movements — workers organized to demand rights
  • New ideologies — liberalism, socialism, Marxism arose in response

Political Effects

  • Rise of industrial capitalist states — Britain, France, Germany, USA
  • Imperial competition — the "Scramble for Africa" (1880s–1900s) driven by industrial needs
  • Labour parties and socialist movements emerged
  • Rise of nationalism — tied to industrial strength and imperial rivalry
  • Eventual path toward World War I (1914) as industrial powers competed
📝 A double-edged sword

Industrialization brought unprecedented material progress and unprecedented social dislocation. It enriched nations while impoverishing workers; it enabled mass education and medicine while enabling mechanized warfare; it connected the world while creating empires built on conquest.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 1.1
What you should remember
  • Capitalism = private ownership of means of production, profit motive, free markets, wage labour
  • Capitalism emerged in Western Europe from the decline of feudalism, the Age of Exploration, banking, and Enlightenment ideas
  • Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776) argued for free markets and the "invisible hand"
  • The Industrial Revolution (c. 1750–1850) replaced hand production with machine manufacturing
  • It began in Britain due to coal, iron, colonies, capital, labour, stable government, and geography
  • Key inventions: Spinning Jenny, Water Frame, Steam Engine, Power Loom, Cotton Gin, Railway
  • Phase 1 (1750–1850): textiles, coal, iron, steam
  • Phase 2 (1850–1914): steel, chemicals, electricity, oil → monopoly capitalism and imperial rivalry
  • Phase 3 (after 1945): electronics, computers, information technology
  • Effects: mass production · urbanization · bourgeoisie vs. proletariat · child labour · trade unions · imperialism
  • Industrialization was a double-edged sword — progress and dislocation, connection and conquest
