🇮🇹 Italian Occupation & Post-War Restoration (1936–1950s)

The Italian occupation of Ethiopia (1936–1941) was one of the darkest chapters in Ethiopian history — but it was also the moment that forged modern Ethiopian national identity. This lesson covers the invasion, the Five Years of Occupation, the resistance, the 1941 liberation, and Haile Selassie's post-war restoration.

📖 Reading: ~30 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

On 3 October 1935, Fascist Italy invaded Ethiopia from Eritrea. Despite heroic resistance, Italian forces — equipped with modern weapons, aircraft, and poison gas — captured Addis Ababa in May 1936. Haile Selassie fled to exile and appealed to the League of Nations. What followed was five years of brutal occupation, during which Ethiopian resistance movements (the Arbegnoch) fought on. In 1941, with British help, Ethiopia was liberated and Haile Selassie returned to his throne.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe the Italian invasion of 1935 and the reasons for Ethiopian defeat
  • Explain what happened during the Five Years of Occupation
  • Describe the Ethiopian resistance (Arbegnoch movement)
  • Explain the Liberation of 1941
  • Understand the post-war restoration and its consequences
⚔️
The Italian Invasion (1935–1936)
A new kind of war
📌 The Second Italo-Ethiopian War

The Second Italo-Ethiopian War (October 1935 – May 1936) was Italy's invasion of Ethiopia under Fascist dictator Benito Mussolini. It followed the Wal Wal Incident (December 1934) and Italy's refusal to accept League of Nations mediation. Italy used modern weapons, aircraft, and chemical weapons against a poorly-equipped Ethiopian army.

Why Italy Invaded

  • Revenge for Adwa — Mussolini wanted to erase the humiliation of 1896
  • Colonial ambition — Italy wanted a large East African empire
  • Fascist ideology — conquest as a demonstration of Italian power
  • Economic motives — access to Ethiopian resources and markets
  • Weak international response — the League of Nations was reluctant to confront Italy

Why Ethiopia Lost

✈️
Air Power

Italy used aircraft, including bombers, that Ethiopia could not match.

☠️
Chemical Weapons

Italy used mustard gas against Ethiopian troops and civilians — a war crime.

🔫
Modern Weapons

Italy had tanks, artillery, and modern rifles — Ethiopia had outdated weapons.

🤝
League of Nations

The League imposed only weak sanctions and did not stop Italy.

🌍
International Politics

Britain and France wanted to keep Italy as an ally against Germany — they did not intervene.

⏱️
Speed of Attack

Italy advanced rapidly before Ethiopia could fully mobilize.

📝 Haile Selassie at the League of Nations

On 30 June 1936, Haile Selassie personally addressed the League of Nations in Geneva, pleading for help. His speech — warning that "it is us today, it will be you tomorrow" — became one of the most famous speeches of the 20th century. The League did nothing.

⛓️
Five Years of Occupation
1936–1941

On 7 May 1936, Italian forces entered Addis Ababa. Mussolini declared the creation of Italian East Africa — combining Ethiopia with Eritrea and Italian Somaliland. The occupation lasted five years and was marked by brutal repression, but also by continuous Ethiopian resistance.

Italian Colonial Rule

  • Racial segregation — Italians lived in separate areas from Ethiopians
  • Forced labor — Ethiopians were conscripted to build roads and infrastructure
  • Land confiscation — Italian settlers took the best agricultural land
  • Destruction of Addis Ababa — parts of the capital were burned after an assassination attempt on the Italian viceroy
  • Massacres — thousands of civilians were killed in reprisals
  • Suppression of the church — Orthodox Church leaders were persecuted

The Graziani Massacre (1937)

  • After an assassination attempt on Viceroy Rodolfo Graziani, Italian forces carried out a three-day massacre in Addis Ababa
  • Thousands of civilians were killed — estimates range from 3,000 to 30,000
  • Hundreds of churches and monasteries were burned
  • This was one of the worst atrocities of the occupation
🛡️
Ethiopian Resistance
The Arbegnoch movement
📌 Arbegnoch (Patriots)

The Arbegnoch ("Patriots") were Ethiopian resistance fighters who continued to fight against the Italian occupation after the regular army was defeated. They operated from rural areas, using guerrilla tactics, and kept the spirit of Ethiopian independence alive throughout the occupation.

Key Features of the Resistance

  • Guerrilla warfare — small units attacking Italian positions, supply lines, and patrols
  • Widespread participation — men and women, from many regions
  • Famous leaders — including Ras Abebe Aregai, Dejazmach Wondoson, and many others
  • Support networks — rural communities provided food, shelter, and intelligence
  • Links to exile — some coordination with Haile Selassie and the Allies
📝 The cost of resistance

Italian forces responded to resistance with collective punishment — burning villages, executing suspected supporters, and using chemical weapons against civilian populations. The resistance was heroic but paid a terrible price.

🎉
Liberation (1941)
The end of occupation

The liberation of Ethiopia came as part of World War II. When Italy entered the war on Germany's side in 1940, Britain and its allies moved against Italian positions in East Africa. Ethiopian patriots joined the Allied campaign, and by 1941 Italian forces were in retreat.

📝 The 1941 liberation

Ethiopia was one of the first countries liberated in World War II. The victory was both a national triumph and a symbol of the Allied cause — freedom against fascism.

🏛️
Post-War Restoration
1941–1950s

After liberation, Haile Selassie returned to power and began rebuilding the country. His post-war policies aimed to restore the pre-war state and accelerate modernization. But the occupation had fundamentally changed Ethiopia — it now had a new generation of resistance leaders, a stronger sense of national identity, and a position in the post-war world order.

Key Post-War Developments

  • 1942 — Anglo-Ethiopian Agreement — restored Ethiopian sovereignty
  • 1942 — abolition of slavery — long promised, now completed
  • 1945 — Ethiopia joins the United Nations as a founding member
  • 1950 — UN Resolution 390 — Eritrea federated with Ethiopia
  • 1955 — Revised Constitution — expanded parliament but kept imperial power
  • 1950s — education expansion — the University College of Addis Ababa (1950) became Haile Selassie I University (1961)
📝 The legacy of occupation

The Italian occupation was devastating — but it also strengthened Ethiopian national identity. The shared experience of resistance, sacrifice, and liberation united the country in a way that the earlier decades of imperial consolidation could not. The Arbegnoch became national heroes, and the occupation became a defining chapter in modern Ethiopian history.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 4.2

  • Ethiopia lost due to technological gaps, weak League of Nations response, and international politics
  • 7 May 1936 — Italian forces enter Addis Ababa; Haile Selassie flees to exile
  • Haile Selassie's 1936 League of Nations speech — famous but ineffective
