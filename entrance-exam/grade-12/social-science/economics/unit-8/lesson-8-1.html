🌍 Economy and the Environment

The economy and the environment are deeply linked. This lesson explores the economics of environmental resources, the impact of rural and urban development, and the complex relationship between population growth, resource use, and environmental degradation.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

The fundamental principle of economic theory is that economic resources are scarce — finite quantities relative to the potentially limitless claims people put on them. Natural and environmental resources are a subset of these economic resources. As economic activity expands, environmental resources become increasingly scarce, and economic activities degrade the natural environment.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Explain the economic significance of environmental resources
  • Distinguish renewable from non-renewable resources
  • Describe how rural development affects the environment
  • Describe how urban development affects the environment
  • Explain the population-resource-environment linkages
🌱
Environmental Resources
MoE Textbook §8.1, pp. 183–186

Environmental resources can be classified into different categories. The prominent classification is between renewable and non-renewable resources.

TypeExamplesKey Issue
Biologically renewable Forests, fish, animal population, biomass stock Potentially exhaustible
Physical renewable stock Soil structure/fertility, ozone layer, earth's waste processing capacity Capable of regeneration, but also potentially exhaustible
Non-renewable / exhaustible Oil, minerals Finite; monetized flows
Non-monetized benefits Fresh air, rivers, lakes, oceans Not priced in markets

Key terms

  • Sustainable development — development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs
  • Ecology — deals with nature's allocation of scarce resources
  • Environmental degradation — decline in quality of air, water, and land
  • Depletion of natural capital — reduction in the stock of natural resources (oil, gas, fish stocks)
📝 The environmental Kuznets curve

At one point it was believed that as per capita incomes rose, pollution and other forms of environmental degradation would first rise and then fall in an inverted-U pattern. As incomes rise, societies are expected to have both the means and the willingness to pay for environmental protection.

Milestones in environmental awareness

  • Great Smog of London (1952)
  • Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962)
  • Limits to Growth report (1972)
  • Brundtland Commission Report "Our Common Future" (1987)
  • Rio Earth Summit (1992)
  • Kyoto Protocol (1997)
  • Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) formed (1998)
  • Millennium Development Goals (2000)
  • Rio+20 Earth Summit (2012)
  • Sustainable Development Goals (2015)
📝 The poverty-environment nexus

The poor are both agents and victims of environmental degradation. Communities may unintentionally destroy or exhaust the resources they rely on for survival due to ignorance or economic necessity. Environmental degradation then diminishes economic development by imposing high costs — reduced productivity of resources and increased health-related expenses.

🌾
Rural Development and the Environment
MoE Textbook §8.1.1, pp. 186–187

Rural development is the process of improving the quality of life and economic wellbeing of people who live in rural areas — including livelihoods (electricity, clean water, access roads, education). It has traditionally centred on exploitation of land-intensive natural resources such as agriculture and forestry.

Environmental issues in rural development

  • Use of pesticides
  • Loss of agricultural land fertility
  • Encroachment by urbanization
  • Forest depletion
  • Declining soil fertility
  • Loss of topsoil
  • Desertification
  • Unsustainable rates of water usage
  • Air pollution

The rural poverty-environment cycle

In developing countries including Ethiopia, most people live in rural areas and agriculture is their main occupation. Low-income households cut trees for firewood and cultivate marginal land. The logging and cutting of forests for cultivation leads to deforestation. Combined with overgrazing, this exposes soil to flooding and erosion — feeding back into the cycle of rural poverty and environmental destruction.

🏙️
Urban Development and the Environment
MoE Textbook §8.1.2, pp. 187–188

Urban development is the social, cultural, economic, and physical development of cities. It covers infrastructure for education, health, justice, solid waste, markets, and street pavements — with special attention to slums in large cities and informal settlements in towns and peri-urban areas.

📝 Urbanization facts

The World Bank estimates that over 50% of the population lives in urban areas globally. By 2045, the world's urban population will increase by 1.5× to 6 billion.

Environmental problems of urbanization

  • Congestion
  • Vehicular and industrial emissions
  • Poorly ventilated household stoves
  • Inaccessibility of clean water
  • Lack of sanitation
  • Growth of slums with attendant malnutrition and poor health
  • Rising industrial emissions and pollution affecting urban health

The urban poor, in particular, are likely to suffer serious consequences from environmental degradation. Poor environmental conditions reduce individual resistance to hazards.

📝 One solution

Deploy clean technologies that by design produce less pollution and waste, and use resources more efficiently.

👥
Population, Resources and the Environment
MoE Textbook §8.1.3, pp. 188–189

The world's population reached about 7.7 billion in 2019. According to the UN Population Division, this is projected to rise to about 8.1 billion in 2025 and about 9.6 billion by 2050. More than three-quarters of the world's population will live in the developing world.

The challenge of feeding a growing population

In order to meet the expanded food needs of rapidly growing populations, food production will have to increase by at least 50% in the next three decades. Because land in many areas of the developing world is being unsustainably overexploited, meeting this target will require radical changes in the distribution, use, and quantity of resources available to agriculture.

Population pressure on resources

  • Rapid population growth tends to overuse natural resources (especially land), endangering future generations
  • Agricultural landholdings become smaller and unviable to cultivate
  • Pressure on land increases due to inelastic supply
  • Disguised unemployment increases and per capita productivity falls
  • Wages fall; savings and investment decline
  • Capital formation in agriculture suffers; economy stuck at subsistence level
  • Food shortages lead to imports, worsening the balance of payments
  • Over-cultivation of semi-arid areas leads to desertification
  • Migration to urban areas → severe air, water, and noise pollution in cities
📝 What environmental policies need

For environmental policies to succeed in countries like Ethiopia, governments must address poverty, landlessness, and lack of access to institutional resources. Insecure land tenure, lack of credit and inputs, and absence of information prevent the poor from making resource-augmenting investments. Success also requires firm commitment from government and policymakers.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 8.1
  • Economic resources are scarce; environmental resources are a subset
  • Environmental resources classified into renewable (biological, physical) and non-renewable (oil, minerals)
  • Sustainable development — meets present needs without compromising future generations
  • Environmental Kuznets curve — inverted-U pattern for pollution vs income (theoretical)
  • Rural development environmental issues: pesticides, soil erosion, deforestation, desertification, water overuse
  • Urban development environmental issues: congestion, emissions, slums, poor sanitation
  • Poverty-environment nexus — the poor are both agents and victims of degradation
  • World population: 7.7B (2019) → 9.6B (2050); 50%+ live in urban areas
