🌡️ Global Warming and Climate Change

Climate change is one of the most urgent challenges facing societies and economies. This lesson examines global warming, the causes and indicators of climate change, impacts and vulnerability, and the two main responses: mitigation and adaptation.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Climate change is one of the most urgent and complex challenges for societies and economies. For developing countries like Ethiopia, climate change complicates the existing challenges of poverty eradication. For many parts of the world, climate change is an everyday reality with implications for people's livelihoods, infrastructure, and institutions.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define global warming and climate change
  • Distinguish climate from weather
  • Explain the causes of climate change
  • Identify the indicators of climate change
  • Describe the impacts and vulnerability to climate change
  • Explain the two main responses: mitigation and adaptation
🌡️
Global Warming
MoE Textbook §8.2.1, pp. 190–191
📌 Definition

Global warming refers to increasing average air and ocean temperatures. It is often used in reference to the trend that began in the mid-twentieth century and attributed largely to human activities (industrial, forestry, and agricultural) emitting greenhouse gases.

The primary greenhouse gases

  • Water vapour (H₂O)
  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂)
  • Nitrous oxide (N₂O)
  • Methane (CH₄)
  • Ozone (O₃)

Global greenhouse gas emissions grew by 70% between 1970 and 2004 (IPCC, 2007). Three gases in particular increased: CO₂, CH₄, and N₂O.

The greenhouse effect

Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere absorb and emit radiation within the thermal infrared spectrum. This traps long-wave radiation in the upper atmosphere, raises atmospheric temperatures, and produces changes in the climate system.

From around 1750 to 2011: CO₂ increased by 40%, CH₄ by 150%, and N₂O by 20% (IPCC, 2013). The increase is due to burning fossil fuels and reductions in some of the "sinks" for carbon dioxide, notably forests.

📝 The economic activity-energy-emission link

The process of global warming relates economic activity to an associated level and pattern of energy and material flows, corresponding to which are flows of greenhouse gas emissions. Carbon emissions are caused by fossil fuel use; agricultural activities and waste decomposition are important emitters of methane.

🌍
Climate Change
MoE Textbook §8.2.2, p. 192
📌 Definition

Climate change denotes a variation in the mean state of the climate persisting for an extended period (typically decades or longer) and resulting from anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions (IPCC, 2007, 2013). It includes changes such as average temperatures, precipitation, humidity, wind conditions, and all other aspects of the earth's climate.

Climate vs Weather

TermMeaning
Climate The average weather pattern over many years, expressed through variables such as temperature, precipitation, and wind
Weather Short-term atmospheric conditions — the mix of events such as temperature, rainfall, and humidity that occur each day or week
🏭
Scope of the Problem & Causes of Climate Change
MoE Textbook §8.2.3, p. 193

The IPCC predicted that, unless something is done to curb intensive fossil fuel emissions, there will be a probable rise in mean temperatures between 1.5°C and 4°C by 2099. By 2016, mean temperatures had already reached 1°C above the pre-industrial level.

Contribution of each greenhouse gas

GasShare of greenhouse effect
Carbon dioxide (CO₂)77% — largest contributor
Methane (CH₄)~14%
Nitrous oxide (N₂O)~8%

Main sources

  • Burning fossil fuels (oil, coal, natural gas) for electricity, heat, and transportation
  • Deforestation — contributes over 20% of harmful greenhouse gases in developing countries, plus loss of biodiversity and environmental services
📊
Indicators of Climate Change
MoE Textbook §8.2.4, p. 193

According to the IPCC, the warming of the climate system is unequivocal. Evidence comes from observations of increases in global average air and ocean temperatures, widespread melting of snow and ice, and rising global average sea level.

Key indicators

  • Unpredictable rain patterns
  • Too much or too little rain
  • Higher average air and ocean temperatures
  • Floods
  • Sea level rise
  • Melting of glaciers and ice caps
  • Fewer cold days, cold nights, and frosts; more common heat waves
  • Emergence of diseases such as malaria in formerly cold climates and respiratory diseases
  • Increased precipitation in some regions (Australia, Central Asia, Mediterranean, Sahel, Western US)
  • More frequent heavy rainfall, floods, storms, and tropical cyclones
  • More frequent and intense droughts in other regions
⚠️
Impacts and Vulnerability
MoE Textbook §8.2.5–8.2.6, pp. 194–195

Direct impacts of climate change

  • Damage from heat waves in some areas
  • Harm from hurricanes and tornadoes in others
  • Flooding from heightened rainfall
  • Moisture stress from reduced rainfall
  • Displacement from rising sea levels
  • Losses of grasslands, farmlands, and marine ecosystems
  • Decrease in suitable agricultural areas and growing seasons in semi-arid/arid zones
  • Degradation of coastal fisheries, mangroves, and coral reefs
  • Health impacts — diarrhoea, malaria, malnutrition; over 140,000 excess deaths/year by 2004 (WHO)
📝 Who suffers most

The worst impact will be felt by the very poor, who depend mostly on natural resources including rain-fed agriculture. Their housing in urban and rural areas is often poorly constructed and located in environmentally stressed areas. They are vulnerable to heat waves, flooding, mudslides, and diseases.

Vulnerability to climate change

Vulnerability refers to the degree to which a system is likely to experience harm from exposure to climatic hazards. It depends on the character, magnitude, and rate of climate variation, plus sensitivity and adaptive capacity.

Highly vulnerable zones (IPCC)

  • Sub-Saharan Africa — faces drying
  • Asia — faces flooding
  • Small islands — multiple sensitivities, rising sea levels
  • Arctic — melting glaciers

Bangladesh is highly prone to flooding; the Horn of Africa to droughts. Towns like Dire Dawa in Eastern Ethiopia are prone to flooding. The Ethiopian Meteorological Agency provides information and alerts to populations in Ethiopia.

🛠️
Mitigation & Adaptation
MoE Textbook §8.2.7, pp. 195–198

There are two main ways to address the climate change crisis:

  • Mitigation — deals with the causes of climate change (reducing greenhouse gas emissions)
  • Adaptation — deals with the consequences of climate change (adjusting to its impacts)

Mitigation of Climate Change

Mitigation efforts are linked to reducing greenhouse gases, especially CO₂, or stabilizing their atmospheric concentrations to prevent dangerous interference with the climate system.

  • Carbon markets
  • Taxes on carbon
  • Subsidies to encourage faster technological progress
  • REDD (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation) and REDD-plus
  • Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) — from the Kyoto Protocol of 1997
📝 Historic vs current responsibility

Historically, most greenhouse gases have been emitted by developed countries — they have a historic responsibility to reduce emissions and develop new technologies. But developing countries share current and future responsibilities. China is now the world's largest greenhouse gas emitter (though lower per capita than most rich countries).

Adaptation to Climate Change

Adaptation is the process of responding and adjusting to the actual or potential impacts of changing climate. As a policy response, it is a reactive policy that attempts to minimize the adverse impact of climate change after the fact.

Two forms of adaptation

  • Planned (policy) adaptation — undertaken by governments
  • Autonomous (private) adaptation — undertaken by households, farms, and firms in response to climate change

Four classes of adaptation strategies

  • Mobility — avoids risks across space
  • Storage — reduces risks experienced over time
  • Diversification — reduces risks across assets owned by households or collectives
  • Communal pool — joint ownership of assets and resources; sharing of wealth, labour, or incomes

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 8.2
  • Global warming = increasing average air and ocean temperatures, attributed largely to human activities
  • Primary greenhouse gases: water vapour, CO₂, N₂O, CH₄, O₃
  • GHG emissions grew 70% between 1970 and 2004; CO₂ contributes 77% to the greenhouse effect
  • Climate change = long-term variation in climate from anthropogenic emissions
  • Climate = long-term average; weather = short-term conditions
  • IPCC predicted 1.5°C–4°C rise by 2099; already 1°C above pre-industrial by 2016
  • Main sources: burning fossil fuels, deforestation (20%+ of GHGs in developing countries)
  • Indicators: rising temps, melting glaciers, sea level rise, unpredictable rainfall, extreme weather, new disease patterns
  • Vulnerability is highest for the poor; Sub-Saharan Africa, Asia, small islands, and the Arctic are IPCC-identified vulnerable zones
  • Mitigation = reduce causes (carbon markets, carbon taxes, REDD, CDM)
  • Adaptation = adjust to consequences (planned by government, autonomous by households/firms)
  • Four adaptation strategies: mobility, storage, diversification, communal pool
