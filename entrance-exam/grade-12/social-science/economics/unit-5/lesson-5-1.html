💰 Taxes: Definition, Principles, Objectives & Classification

Taxes are the price we pay for a civilized society. In this lesson, you'll learn what a tax is, why governments impose taxes, what makes a good tax system, and how taxes are classified into direct and indirect.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Taxation forms a critical element of modern government. As the saying goes, "There are two things that are certain in life: death and taxes." Without tax, there is no revenue — and without revenue, no government. This lesson introduces the fundamental concepts of taxation.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define taxation and key tax terminologies
  • List the objectives of taxation
  • Explain the principles of taxation
  • Describe the characteristics of a good tax system
  • Distinguish direct from indirect taxes
  • Identify the categories and sources of taxes
📌
Definition & Terminologies
MoE Textbook §5.1.1, p. 103
📌 Tax (OECD Definition)

Tax is defined by the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) as the "compulsory, unrequited payments to general government."

  • "Compulsory" — taxes are not voluntary purchase payments but mandatory impositions, payable in line with what is legislated
  • "Unrequited" — benefits provided by government to a taxpayer are not in proportion to the payments made by that taxpayer
  • Tax is levied without a quid pro quo — without anything in direct return
  • Taxation typically accounts for 90% or more of total governmental receipts in times of peace

Key Terminologies

TermDefinition
Tax Rate The per-unit amount of the tax, or the percentage rate at which the economic activity is taxed
Tax Base The level or quantity of an economic activity that is taxed. Higher tax rates reduce the tax base because they make the activity less attractive
Tax Incidence How the burden of a tax is distributed among economic units. Points out who is legally responsible for paying the tax
Impact of Tax The tax's first point of contact with the taxpayer — felt by those who bear the first statutory responsibility of paying it
Effect of a Tax Responses from taxpayers and the economy to the imposition and collection of taxes — influences production, growth, saving, investment, inequality
📝 Impact vs Incidence vs Effect

Impact = first contact (who legally pays)
Incidence = final resting place (who actually bears the burden)
Effect = the economy's behavioral response

Objectives of Taxation
MoE Textbook §5.1.2, p. 104

Governments impose taxes to achieve several important macroeconomic and social goals:

⚖️
Minimize Inequality

Reduce income and wealth inequalities in society.

📊
Stabilize the Economy

Control inflation, unemployment, and business cycles.

🚫
Discourage Harmful Products

Reduce consumption of goods with negative externalities (cigarettes, alcohol).

💼
Encourage Capital Formation

Provide incentives for private sector investment.

🗺️
Reduce Regional Imbalance

Balance development across regions.

📈
Enhance Standards of Living

Fund public services that improve quality of life.

🎯
Optimize Resource Use

Direct scarce resources to essential goods.

💵
Reduce Unemployment

Encourage export and job creation.

📝 The core triad

In minimalist terms, governments impose taxes for three basic purposes: (1) to cover the cost of administration, (2) to maintain law and order, and (3) to defend the public. Beyond this, taxes fund infrastructure, health, education, and social programs.

📜
Principles of Taxation
MoE Textbook §5.1.3, p. 105 — Adam Smith's Canons

In The Wealth of Nations (1776), Adam Smith proposed the principle/canons of taxation as summarized below:

  • Ability to Pay — Subjects of every state ought to contribute towards the support of the government in proportion to their respective abilities
  • Certainty — The tax everyone is bound to pay ought to be certain and not arbitrary. Time, manner, and quantity of payment must be clear and plain
  • Convenience — Every tax ought to be levied at the time or in the manner that makes it most convenient for the contributor to pay it
  • Economy — Every tax ought to be planned so it takes as little as possible out of the pockets of the people, over and above what it brings into the public treasury
  • Minimum Cost of Collection — The cost of collection should be the minimum possible (the "canon of economy")
  • Fiscal Adequacy — The tax system should yield enough revenue for the government so that it should not be forced to resort to deficit financing

Modern Principles

These classical maxims translate into modern principles. A tax system should:

  • Not distort the optimal allocation of production factors in efficient markets
  • Be fair
  • Be a flexible automatic stabilizer
  • Be clear, transparent, and definitive
  • Provide inexpensive collection

In sum, the principles touch on: equity, certainty, convenience, economy, flexibility, simplicity, diversity, and buoyancy.

✅
Characteristics of a Good Tax System
MoE Textbook §5.1.4, p. 106

Taxation is broadly characterized by:

  • Compulsory nature — imposes obligations on taxpayers; non-payment is a criminal offense
  • Collective use — revenues spent on collective use, serving common interest where both payers and non-payers benefit
  • Regular and periodic basis — with known due dates
  • Non-discrimination — levied on all people without discrimination, yet according to ability to pay

A Good Tax System Is:

  • Simple, financially adequate, and elastic
  • Broad-based — levied not only on income but also on property and commodities
  • Administratively efficient
  • Balanced and harmonious
  • Ensures the reduction of economic inequalities
  • Ensures economic stability
  • Ensures that national income / standard of living increases
  • Acts as an instrument of economic growth
  • Is socially advantageous
  • Enables optimum allocation of resources
📝 The two-word summary

A good tax system should be equitable and efficient.

Classification of Taxes
MoE Textbook §5.1.5, p. 107
📌 Direct vs Indirect Taxes

There are two major types of taxes based on impact or incidence: direct taxes and indirect taxes.

Direct taxes hit the same person who earns the income. Indirect taxes shift the burden from one person to another.

Direct Taxes — Sub-Categories

TypeDefinition
Income taxGovernments impose on income generated by individuals and businesses within their jurisdiction
Transfer taxMost common form levied on real estate. Also levied on the taxable portion of property of a deceased individual
Property taxCharged on properties such as land and buildings; used for maintaining public services
Capital gains taxCharged when an individual sells assets (stocks, real estate, business). Computed by acquisition amount and selling amount

📝 "Sin tax"

The UK also has a minor category known as "sin tax" — imposed on goods and services that commonly include tobacco, alcohol, sugar-added drinks, and gambling. Its purposes: reduce consumption of harmful goods + increase government revenue.

The US System (Example)

  • Earnings (payroll tax)
  • Individual income (income tax, capital gains)
  • Corporate income (corporate income tax)
  • Wealth (property taxes, estate taxes)
  • Consumption (sales taxes, excise tax)

From this list, items 1–4 are direct taxes and item 5 represents indirect tax. In terms of the share of tax sources, the US Federal Government generates tax revenue primarily from:

  • Individual income tax: 41.6%
  • Payroll taxes: 34.8%
  • Corporate income tax: 15.1%
  • Very small shares from consumption and other taxes

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 5.1
What you should remember
  • Tax = compulsory, unrequited payments to general government (OECD)
  • Compulsory = mandatory; unrequited = no direct benefit received in return
  • Key terms: tax rate, tax base, tax incidence, impact, effect
  • Objectives: minimize inequality, stabilize economy, discourage harmful products, incentivize capital, reduce regional imbalance, enhance living standards, optimize resources, reduce unemployment
  • Adam Smith's canons (1776): ability to pay, certainty, convenience, economy, minimum cost, fiscal adequacy
  • A good tax system is equitable and efficient
  • Direct taxes — impact and incidence on the same person (income tax, property tax, capital gains)
  • Indirect taxes — impact and incidence on different persons (VAT, excise, TOT, surtax, customs, stamp duty)
  • USA's four direct taxes: earnings, individual income, corporate income, wealth; consumption = indirect
  • "Sin tax" — imposed on tobacco, alcohol, sugar drinks, gambling
