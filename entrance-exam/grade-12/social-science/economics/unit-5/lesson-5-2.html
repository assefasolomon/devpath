⚖️ Approaches to Tax Equity

What makes a tax fair? Should the rich pay more? Should everyone pay the same rate? This lesson explores the two approaches to tax fairness — the benefits approach and the ability-to-pay approach — and the three types of tax by impact.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Tax fairness is one of the most debated topics in economics. Should the rich pay less or more? This lesson answers that question through two classic approaches: the benefits approach and the ability-to-pay approach. You'll also see how tax rates can be progressive, proportional, or regressive.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Explain the benefits approach to tax equity
  • Explain the ability-to-pay approach
  • Distinguish horizontal from vertical equity
  • Define progressive, proportional, and regressive taxes
  • Apply the concept of tax incidence
📌
Concept of Tax Equity
MoE Textbook §5.2, p. 109
📌 Tax Equity

Tax equity refers to fairness in taxation — the principle that taxes should be distributed among taxpayers in a manner that is considered just. There are two main approaches for evaluating tax equity:

🎁
Benefits Approach

Pay according to the benefits received from public services.

💰
Ability-to-Pay Approach

Pay according to your capacity to pay taxes.

🎁
The Benefits Approach
MoE Textbook §5.2.1, p. 109
📌 Benefits Approach

According to the benefits approach, an equitable tax system is one in which each taxpayer contributes in line with the benefits which he/she receives from public services. Tax obligations should be based on the benefits received from the enjoyment of public services.

The aim here is to work out how much each person gains from public spending. The benefits approach has the advantage of linking the discussion of tax equity with the expenditure side of the public budget.

The Problem with the Benefits Approach

  • While some government spending (health, education, social subsidies) can easily be attributed to beneficiary households, about half of government spending may not be
  • It is difficult, if not impossible, to determine who benefits most from spending on:
    • The army
    • Police
    • Diplomatic service
    • Judicial system
📝 The challenge

It's much easier to state the benefits principle than to apply it in reality. Public goods like national defence benefit everyone but can't be traced back to specific individuals.

💰
The Ability-to-Pay Approach
MoE Textbook §5.2.2, p. 109
📌 Ability-to-Pay Approach

According to the ability-to-pay approach, tax is levied on each taxpayer in line with their ability to pay. This approach focuses on the distributive nature of taxation — people with equal capacity pay the same, and people with greater ability pay more.

Three Key Questions

  • How to measure ability to pay?
  • How to determine a fair set of tax rates based on differing abilities to pay?
  • How to compare the economic status of various individual taxpayers?
📝 The ideal measure

Ideally, the measure for ability to pay would reflect the entire welfare a person can derive from all options available — consumption (present and future), holding of wealth, and the enjoyment of leisure. Unfortunately, such a comprehensive measure is not practicable.

⚖️
Horizontal & Vertical Equity
Two dimensions of fairness
↔️
Horizontal Equity

People in the same circumstances pay the same taxes. Example: Two people with equal incomes face equal tax obligations.

↕️
Vertical Equity

A degree of proportionality — unequals should be treated unequally. Example: Someone with higher income pays more tax than someone with lower income.

Horizontal equity: people in the same situation pay the same tax. Vertical equity: people in different situations pay different amounts.
📊
Progressive, Proportional & Regressive Taxes
Type by impact
📈
Progressive Tax

Those with higher incomes pay a higher share of taxes out of their income compared to those with lower incomes. The average tax rate rises with income.

➡️
Proportional Tax

Everyone pays the same share of taxes regardless of income level. The average tax rate is the same at all income levels.

📉
Regressive Tax

Those with high income pay a lower share of income in taxes than those with lower incomes. The average tax rate falls as income rises.

Average Tax Rate
Average Tax Rate = (Total Tax Paid ÷ Total Income) × 100%

Numeric Comparison

Income Progressive Proportional Regressive
$10,000 $1,000 (10%) $2,000 (20%) $3,000 (30%)
$50,000 $10,000 (20%) $10,000 (20%) $7,500 (15%)
$100,000 $30,000 (30%) $20,000 (20%) $10,000 (10%)
Progressive tax rates rise with income. Proportional rates stay flat. Regressive rates fall as income rises.
📝 Which type is fair?

Most modern tax systems use progressive income taxes because they address vertical equity. Sales taxes (like VAT) tend to be regressive in practice — because lower-income people spend a higher share of their income on consumption.

🔍
Tax Incidence Analysis
Who really pays?
📌 Tax Incidence

Tax incidence relates to the way the burden of a tax is distributed among economic units (consumers, producers, employees, employers). It points out who is legally responsible for paying the tax — but more importantly, who is actually made worse off.

Though taxes may be collected from firms, the ultimate burden may fall on individual consumers. The incidence of a tax is defined as its final resting place.

Impact vs Incidence vs Effect

TermMeaning
Impact The tax's first point of contact with the taxpayer. Felt by those who bear the first statutory responsibility of paying it to the government.
Incidence The final resting place of the tax burden. Who is ultimately made worse off.
Effect The responses from taxpayers and the economy — production, growth, saving, investment, inequality.
📝 Example

When a business pays VAT, the impact is on the business (they write the cheque). But the incidence may fall on consumers (through higher prices) or on producers (through lower margins), depending on demand elasticity. The effect might be reduced consumption.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 5.2
What you should remember
  • Tax equity = fairness in taxation. Two main approaches: benefits and ability-to-pay
  • Benefits approach: pay according to benefits received from public services
  • Benefits approach has a major flaw — about half of government spending can't be attributed to specific beneficiaries
  • Ability-to-pay approach: pay according to capacity. People with equal capacity pay the same; with greater capacity pay more
  • Horizontal equity: people in the same circumstances pay the same taxes
  • Vertical equity: unequals should be treated unequally
  • Progressive tax: average tax rate rises with income
  • Proportional tax: same average rate at all income levels
  • Regressive tax: average tax rate falls as income rises
  • Tax incidence = final resting place of tax burden — who is ultimately made worse off
  • Impact = first contact; incidence = final resting place; effect = behavioural response
