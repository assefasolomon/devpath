🌱 Overview of Home-grown Economic Reforms

Ethiopia's Home-grown Economic Reform (HGER) is a domestically-designed strategy to sustain rapid growth, reduce debt vulnerabilities, and create jobs — while shifting from public-led to private-led growth.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Ethiopia's rapid growth from 2010 to 2020 was largely demand-driven and financed through heavy debt and external aid. That model created inflationary pressures and failed to raise productivity or create enough jobs. The Home-grown Economic Reform (HGER) is Ethiopia's domestically-initiated response.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Explain why HGER was needed
  • State HGER's central objectives
  • Describe the three categories of HGER reforms: macroeconomic, sectoral, structural
  • Identify the key sectoral reforms (agriculture, mining, tourism)
  • Explain the structural reforms and their goals
❓
Why HGER?
The problem with the old growth model

The rapid and sustained economic growth Ethiopia registered between 2010 and 2020 was mainly driven by aggregate demand, recorded largely as a result of the expansion of government-funded large-scale infrastructure developments. These public investments were financed through heavy debt and external aid.

Problems with the old model

  • Continuous inflationary pressures from rapid public spending
  • Failure to raise productivity despite rapid growth
  • Inadequate job creation
  • Heavy debt burden made it challenging to sustain rapid growth
📝 The bottom line

Ethiopia needed a new growth financing approach — moving away from heavy reliance on public spending and debt financing, toward a private-sector-led, productivity-driven growth model.

🎯
HGER Central Objectives
Three main goals
  1. Sustain rapid growth
  2. Maintain a stable macroeconomic environment by reducing debt vulnerabilities
  3. Create adequate and sustainable job opportunities

Main aim and focus

  • Enhance productivity and competitiveness of the overall economy
  • Gradual transition from public to private sector-led growth
  • Ensure a stable macroeconomy
  • Generate adequate jobs to arrest unemployment and inflationary pressures

The HGER plan is classified into three categories: macroeconomic reforms, sectoral reforms, and structural reforms.

💰
Macroeconomic Reforms
MoE Textbook §7.2.1, pp. 169–175

To eliminate macroeconomic imbalances and create a stable macroeconomy, strict macroeconomic management has been put in place. The key focus areas:

1. Fast, sustainable, broad-based growth

  • Reform efforts across all sectors to ensure high economic growth
  • Involves private sector and development partners
  • Evaluation of past development plans used to design more inclusive future plans

2. Fiscal policy reform

  • Modernize and enhance tax collection capacity
  • Increase citizens' awareness about tax duties
  • Improve public expenditure management
  • Strict public debt management
  • Justice sector reforms targeting the informal sector and contraband
  • Strengthened budget administration and auditing

3. Financial sector reform

Ultimate objective: ensure financial stability, financial inclusion, and promote productivity and competitiveness of the private sector.

  • Market-oriented financial sector reform
  • Market-based interest rate and foreign currency exchange rate determination
  • Establishing and expanding capital markets
  • Strengthening monitoring/regulatory capacity of the National Bank of Ethiopia

4. Export and foreign currency

  • Expand inflow of foreign currencies
  • Identify structural constraints of export performance
  • Diversify export commodities by addressing supply-side problems
  • Create value additions to export commodities to improve competitiveness in regional and global value chains
🏭
Sectoral Reforms
Agriculture · Mining · Tourism

1. Agriculture

In the past, the agricultural sector received particular attention, yet is still characterized as the lowest productive. HGER's agricultural sector reform aims to:

  • Improve the role and participation of the private sector
  • Expand small- to large-scale irrigation development
  • Improve supply of inputs and finance
  • Enhance livestock productivity
  • Protect the environment and natural resources
  • Reduce post-harvest loss
  • Promote research-based food security systems
  • Promote import-substituting major agricultural crop production

2. Mining

  • Reforming mining policies and legal frameworks to create a conducive investment climate
  • Attract foreign and domestic investors into the subsector
  • Promote geological data and its dissemination for mining investment promotion
  • Government role in infrastructure development and targeted support to boost private sector confidence

3. Tourism

  • Ethiopia is endowed with abundant tourism destinations but has not fully utilized the opportunity
  • High potential for domestic and foreign revenue generation and job creation
  • Reform pays particular attention to improving and expanding tourism destinations and related infrastructure
  • Improving the role and participation of the private sector
🏗️
Structural Reforms
Government role, private sector, and system-wide changes

The role of government in the economy shall continue, but adjustments and reforms will be made based on strategic policy performances and prevailing conditions. Government intervention should create strong private initiatives — not crowd out private investors.

📌 Key principle

The government should support all development forces — including private investors — not on the basis of proximity to political elites or power, but based on their merits and performances.

Other focus areas of structural reform

  • Coordinated transport and logistics services — reducing transit time and costs of trade, improving the quality and effectiveness of transport corridors for import/export
  • Import-substituting development strategy — domestic support and policy intervention to substitute imports of strategic commodities (food items, edible oil, wheat)
  • Reforming the investment and job creation landscape — industrial parks at maximum capacity, improved business environment, Ease of Doing Business initiative
  • Increasing private sector participation — reform the bureaucracy to support and encourage (not hinder) foreign and domestic investors
  • Expediting privatization of large state-owned enterprises and liberalization of priority sectors
  • Strengthening Ethiopia's global and regional partnerships — WTO accession, African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA)
  • Promoting free movement of labour — mechanisms ensuring free mobility of skilled labour domestically and internationally
  • Promoting the development of civic societies — creating conducive environment for civic organizations to play a role in economic, social, and political activities
  • Diversifying development partnerships and sources of development finance — new bilateral arrangements and agreements alongside traditional multilateral/bilateral sources

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 7.2
  • HGER = Home-grown Economic Reform — domestically initiated strategy
  • Why: Old growth model was debt-financed, inflation-prone, low-productivity, and failed to create enough jobs
  • Central objectives: sustain rapid growth, reduce debt vulnerabilities, create jobs
  • Main aim: enhance productivity/competitiveness, transition from public-led to private-led growth
  • Three categories: macroeconomic reforms · sectoral reforms · structural reforms
  • Macroeconomic reforms: fiscal (tax, expenditure, debt), financial sector (interest rates, forex, capital markets), export diversification
  • Sectoral reforms: agriculture (irrigation, inputs, livestock, food security), mining (conducive climate, geological data), tourism (destinations, infrastructure, private sector)
  • Structural reforms: coordinated transport/logistics, import substitution, industrial parks, private sector participation, privatization of SOEs, WTO/AfCFTA, labour mobility, civic societies, diversified development partnerships
  • Key principle: support private investors based on merits and performances, not proximity to power
