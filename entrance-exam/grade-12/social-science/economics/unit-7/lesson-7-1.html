Unit 7 · Reforms · MoE Textbook §7.1

🏛️ National Development Objectives & Strategies

Ethiopia has pursued planned development for over 70 years — under three very different political systems. This lesson traces the country's development journey from the Imperial Five-Year Plans to the current Ten-Year Plan (2021–2030).

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Ethiopia is the oldest independent country in Africa. Over the past 70 years, three very different political regimes — the Imperial Monarchy, the Derg military junta, and the FDRE/EPRDF government — have each pursued different strategies to develop the economy. This lesson traces the full historical arc.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe the three Five-Year Plans of the Imperial period (1957–1973)
  • Explain the socialist development strategy of the Derg (1974–1991)
  • Identify the major development programs of the EPRDF era
  • Understand SDPRP, PASDEP, GTP I & GTP II
  • Describe the Ten-Year Development Plan (2021–2030)
👑
Imperial Period (1950–74)
MoE Textbook §7.1.1, pp. 149–152

When the Italian occupation ended in 1941, Ethiopia's economy remained largely unchanged for centuries. Emperor Haile Selassie I renewed calls for a transition from a subsistence economy to an agro-industrial economy. Between 1945 and 1957, technical missions from the US, FAO, and Yugoslavia prepared development plans — but these failed to achieve meaningful results due to scarce statistical data and minimal administrative/technical capabilities.

Three Five-Year Plans

PlanYearsMain ObjectivesResults
First 1957–1961 Infrastructure (roads, construction, communications); cadre of skilled personnel; agricultural development via commercial ventures GNP grew 3.2% (target 3.7%). Exports +3.5%/yr, imports +6.4%/yr. Coffee = 70% of forex.
Second 1962–1967 Change agricultural economy to agro-industry; diversification; modern processing methods; expand productive capacity Prioritized large commercial farms (cotton, coffee, sugar) over small subsistence farms.
Third 1968–1973 Manufacturing and agro-industrial performance; education expansion; improve peasant agriculture Integrated Rural Development project — fertilizers, seeds, rural health services.
📝 What actually happened

Per capita GDP grew only about 4% per year between 1960 and 1970. Manufacturing grew from 1.9% (1960/61) to 4.4% (1973/74); wholesale/retail/transport grew from 9.3% to 15.6%. But these changes failed to improve the lives of most Ethiopians — largely because of the feudal land tenure system that deprived rural peasants of land rights.

🔨
Socialist Period (1974–91)
MoE Textbook §7.1.2, pp. 152–153

The 1974 revolution by the Derg established a socialist state aiming at nationalization and restructuring of the economy. Strategy shifted from agriculture-led to industry-led development under a centrally planned economy. Rural land was nationalized and distributed; commercial farms put under government control; land tenancy abolished.

📝 Key features

Overvalued currency · marketing/pricing policies · Agricultural Marketing Corporation (AMC) setting quotas below market · resettlement and villagisation · collectivization.

🌱
FDRE / EPRDF (1991+)
MoE Textbook §7.1.3, pp. 153–165

The EPRDF's rise to power in 1991 marked a major shift — away from centrally planned industrial development and towards a new agricultural-led development approach. Price controls removed, state farm assets privatized, export-oriented strategy adopted, structural adjustment programmes (SAPs) implemented.

ADLI — The Foundation

📌 Agricultural Development-Led Industrialization

Launched in 1994 as the foundation for national development, with the objective of attaining food self-sufficiency by increasing agricultural productivity and output. Three main strategies:

  • Expansion of agricultural technologies
  • Investment in agricultural infrastructure (inputs)
  • Boosting rural non-agricultural sectors

ADLI also included: rural TVETs, water resources (hydro + irrigation), microfinance, agricultural marketing, smallholder co-operatives. The PADETES programme distributed fertilizer, seed, credit and information to smallholder farmers.

📋
SDPRP, PASDEP, GTP I & II
Successive development plans

SDPRP (2002/03 – 2004/05)

Launched in 2002 after wide public consultations in 2001. Built on: ADLI · food security · decentralization and empowerment · capacity building · reforms in justice and civil service.

Results: first year saw a drought causing an 11.6% fall in agricultural productivity, contributing to a 3.6% fall in GDP. Average growth over 3 years was 5.5% (year 2: 11.3%, year 3: 8.8%).

PASDEP (2005/06 – 2009/10)

Ethiopia's second poverty reduction strategy. Main objective: lay directions for accelerated, sustained, and people-oriented development and pave groundwork for the MDGs by 2015. Vision: middle-income status by 2025.

Eight strategic goals:

  1. Building all-inclusive implementation capacity
  2. A massive push to accelerate economic growth
  3. Balance between economic development and population growth
  4. Unleashing the potentials of Ethiopia's women
  5. Strengthening the infrastructural backbone
  6. Strengthening human resource development
  7. Managing risk and volatility
  8. Creating employment opportunities

Two growth scenarios: Base case = 7% average annual growth (MDG achievement); High case = 10% average annual growth (foundation for 2025 middle-income vision).

GTP I (2010/11 – 2014/15)

Four overarching objectives:

  1. Maintain at least 11% average real GDP growth per annum and attain MDGs by 2014/15
  2. Expand access and ensure quality education and health services
  3. Establish conditions for sustainable nation building through a stable democratic and developmental state
  4. Ensure sustainability of growth through macroeconomic stability

GTP I recognized the importance of urban areas and industrial development. Promoted labour-intensive micro and small-scale enterprises (MSEs) as a strategy for creating linkages with agriculture.

GTP II (2015/16 – 2019/20)

Overarching objective: sustain accelerated growth and establish a springboard for economic structural transformation — realizing the national vision of becoming a lower middle-income country by 2025. Target: 11% average real GDP growth.

📝 Major Development Challenges (GTP era)

(1) Failure to ensure quality growth — jobs, equity, structural transformation lagged
(2) External debt distress — narrow export base, heavy import dependence
(3) Savings-investment imbalance
(4) Inflation — headline 21.5% in 2019/20
(5) Rising unemployment — youth 25.7%
(6) Sluggish structural transformation, weak sectoral linkages
(7) Poor domestic resource mobilization
(8) Limited financial accessibility
(9) Deficient social services and infrastructure
(10) Limited government capacity, malpractices

🎯
Ten-Year Development Plan (2021–2030)
MoE Textbook §7.1.4, pp. 166–175
📌 National Long-Term Vision

The ten-year plan lays out a long-term vision of making Ethiopia an "African Beacon of Prosperity". Prosperity is defined in terms of happiness, standard of living, quality of life, and complete satisfaction created by overall capability built through economic gain and human/social development.

Four Pillars of Prosperity

  1. Income & Wealth — every citizen able to satisfy basic needs and aspirations
  2. Basic Services Accessible — food, water, shelter, health, education regardless of economic status
  3. Enabling & Just Environment — citizens able to utilize potentials for a decent quality life
  4. Dignity, Equality, Freedom — free participation in social, economic, political affairs

Six Major Development Objectives

  1. Build a prosperous country via market-based system with enhanced private sector role
  2. Maintain macroeconomic stability, ensure rapid/sustainable growth, create decent jobs
  3. Ensure structural economic transformation by promoting productivity and competitiveness
  4. Create enabling environment where citizens become owners/beneficiaries
  5. Ensure a competent, independent, quality civil service and good governance
  6. Build strong, inclusive institutions ensuring peace, justice, rule of law, human rights

Ten Strategic Pillars

  1. Quality economic growth and shared prosperity
  2. Economic productivity and competitiveness
  3. Technological capability and digital economy
  4. Sustainable development financing
  5. Private sector-led economic growth
  6. Resilient green economy
  7. Institutional transformation
  8. Gender and social inclusion
  9. Access to justice and efficient civil services
  10. Regional peace building and economic integration

Six Key Priority Areas

  • Multi-sectoral, diversified sources of growth and jobs
  • Sustainable, inclusive financial sector development
  • Harnessing the demographic dividend
  • Quality and efficient infrastructure
  • Sustainable urban development
  • Peace, justice, and inclusive institutions

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 7.1
  • Imperial period (1957–1973): Three Five-Year Plans — infrastructure, agro-industry, agriculture. Failed to help most people due to feudal land tenure.
  • Derg period (1974–1991): Socialist state, industry-led centrally planned economy. Land nationalized. Four phases: 0.4% → 5.7% → decline → 5%
  • EPRDF era (1991+): Shift to agricultural-led development. ADLI launched in 1994 as guiding framework.
  • SDPRP (2002–05): ADLI + food security + decentralization. Avg 5.5% growth.
  • PASDEP (2005–10): Vision of middle-income status by 2025. Eight strategic goals. Base 7% / High 10% scenarios.
  • GTP I (2010/11–2014/15): Four objectives, target 11% GDP. Industrialization + MSEs.
  • GTP II (2015/16–2019/20): Same 11% target. Springboard for structural transformation.
  • GTP performance: 9.2% avg growth (below 11% target), but strong gains — GDP/capita USD 389 → 1,080
  • GTP challenges: Quality growth, external debt, savings gap, inflation (21.5% in 2019/20), unemployment, structural transformation
  • Ten-Year Plan (2021–2030): Vision = "African Beacon of Prosperity". Six objectives, ten strategic pillars, six key priority areas.
