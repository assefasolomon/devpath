📈 Aggregate Supply

Aggregate supply is the other side of the economy — the total quantity of goods and services that firms are willing to produce. In this lesson, we explore both the short-run and long-run aggregate supply curves and what makes them shift.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Aggregate demand is one side of the economy; aggregate supply is the other. The interaction of the two determines the level of national output and the general price level. This lesson focuses on the supply side.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define aggregate supply
  • Distinguish between the Keynesian and Classical AS curves
  • Explain why SRAS slopes upward (sticky wages + worker misperceptions)
  • Explain why LRAS is vertical
  • Identify what shifts the SRAS curve
📌
Concept of Aggregate Supply
MoE Textbook §2.2.1, p. 35
📌 Aggregate Supply (MoE Textbook, p. 35)

Aggregate supply refers to the quantity supplied of all goods and services (real GDP) at various price levels, ceteris paribus. There is a direct or positive relationship between the price level and the amount of real output that firms offer for sale.

Aggregate supply includes both short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) and long-run aggregate supply (LRAS). Understanding the difference is essential for the AD-AS model.

📝 Short run vs Long run

• Short run: a time horizon during which at least one of the firm's inputs cannot be varied
• Long run: a time horizon long enough for a firm to vary all of its inputs

⚖️
Keynesian vs Classical Aggregate Supply
Two extreme views
🔑
Keynesian AS Curve

The Keynesian supply curve is horizontal. Firms will supply whatever amount of goods is demanded at the existing price level since there is unemployment. Firms can obtain as much labour as they want at the current wage.

🏛️
Classical AS Curve

The classical aggregate supply curve is vertical, indicating that the same amount of goods will be supplied whatever the price level — based on the assumption that the labour market is always in equilibrium with full employment.

📝 The modern synthesis

Modern macroeconomics combines both views: the SRAS is upward sloping (like an intermediate case), while the LRAS is vertical (Classical view applies in the long run).

📈
The Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS) Curve
MoE Textbook §2.2.2, p. 36
📌 SRAS Curve

The SRAS curve shows the quantity supplied of all goods and services (real GDP) at different price levels, ceteris paribus. The SRAS curve is upward sloping: as the price level rises, firms increase the quantity supplied; as the price level drops, firms decrease the quantity supplied.

The SRAS curve slopes upward: as price level rises (P₀ → P₁), quantity supplied rises (Q₀ → Q₁).
❓
Why Does the SRAS Slope Upward?
Two explanations
📌
1. Sticky Wages

Wages do not adjust immediately to changes in the price level. When the price level falls, real wages rise — firms cut back on labour, and output falls.

👷
2. Worker Misperceptions

Workers may misperceive changes in real wages. They see nominal wage changes but may not fully understand the change in price level — so they alter their labour supply.

Sticky Wages — An Example

  • Suppose the nominal wage is fixed at $20/hour
  • If the price level falls, the real wage rises (employers pay the same nominal wage, but each Birr now buys more)
  • A rise in real wages means higher labour costs for firms
  • Firms cut back on labour — say, from 1,000 to 800 workers
  • With fewer workers, less output is produced
  • Result: a fall in the price level causes a fall in output — the essence of the upward-sloping SRAS curve

Worker Misperceptions — An Example

  • Nominal wage falls from $30 to $25/hour
  • Price level falls from 1.50 to 1.25
  • Real wage is still $20 ($25 ÷ 1.25) — no real change
  • But workers may mistakenly believe the price level only fell to 1.39 → they think their real wage fell to $17.98
  • Believing their real wage fell, workers reduce labour supply
  • With fewer workers, firms produce less
📝 Bottom line

Both sticky wages and worker misperceptions mean that a fall in the price level leads to a fall in output — which is exactly what an upward-sloping SRAS curve represents.

📊
Shifts in the SRAS Curve
What moves the curve?

A change in the quantity supplied of real GDP is brought about by a change in the price level — that's a movement along the SRAS curve. But several factors shift the entire SRAS curve.

FactorEffect on SRAS
Cost of inputs ↑ (wages, raw materials)SRAS shifts left (costs ↑ → less profitable to produce)
Domestic resource availability ↑SRAS shifts right
Productivity ↑SRAS shifts right (more output per worker)
Technology improvesSRAS shifts right
Business taxes ↑ or subsidies ↓SRAS shifts left
Weather improves (agriculture)SRAS shifts right
Price of imported resources ↑SRAS shifts left

Key Formula

Profit per unit
Profit per unit = Price per unit − Cost per unit
  • Higher wages → higher costs → at constant prices, lower profits → fewer units produced → SRAS shifts left
  • Lower wages → lower costs → at constant prices, higher profits → more units produced → SRAS shifts right
Higher input costs shift SRAS left (SRAS₁ → SRAS₀). Lower costs or higher productivity shift it right (SRAS₁ → SRAS₂).
📏
The Long-Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS) Curve
MoE Textbook §2.2.3, p. 40
📌 LRAS Curve

In the long run, wages become flexible and misperceptions turn into accurate perceptions. The economy then produces the full-employment real GDP or the natural real GDP (QN). The LRAS curve is vertical, indicating the same output is produced regardless of the price level.

In the long run, the economy produces its natural real GDP (Q_N) regardless of the price level — hence a vertical LRAS curve.

Key Points About LRAS

  • The LRAS is vertical — the same amount of goods will be supplied at whatever the price level
  • It reflects full employment of the labour force
  • It assumes flexible wages and prices (the Classical assumption)
  • In the long run, the economy produces its natural real GDP (QN)
  • Long-run equilibrium occurs where AD intersects LRAS
📝 Keynesian vs Classical — resolved

The SRAS (upward sloping) captures the Keynesian view of sticky wages and misperceptions. The LRAS (vertical) captures the Classical view of flexible prices and full employment. Together, they give us the modern AD-AS model.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 2.2
What you should remember
  • Aggregate supply = quantity supplied of all goods and services at various price levels
  • The Keynesian AS curve is horizontal (firms supply whatever is demanded at the current price)
  • The Classical AS curve is vertical (output is at full employment regardless of price level)
  • The SRAS curve slopes upward due to sticky wages and worker misperceptions
  • The LRAS curve is vertical — the economy produces its natural real GDP (QN) in the long run
  • SRAS shifts left when input costs rise (higher wages, expensive imports)
  • SRAS shifts right when costs fall, productivity rises, technology improves, or subsidies increase
  • Profit per unit = Price per unit − Cost per unit
  • Short run = at least one input fixed; Long run = all inputs variable
