Unit 2 · AD-AS Analysis · MoE Textbook §2.1

📉 Aggregate Demand

Aggregate demand is one side of the economy — the total spending by all sectors. In this lesson, we define AD, look at its four components, and explore why the AD curve slopes downward and what makes it shift.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

The AD-AS model is the basic macroeconomic model for studying output and price level determination. Just as there are two sides to a market — a buying side (demand) and a selling side (supply) — there are two sides to an economy. This lesson focuses on the demand side.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define aggregate demand
  • List the four components of AD
  • Explain why the AD curve slopes downward
  • Distinguish between a change in quantity demanded and a change in AD
  • Describe what shifts the AD curve
📌
Concept of Aggregate Demand
MoE Textbook §2.1.1, p. 26
📌 Aggregate Demand (MoE Textbook, p. 26)

Aggregate demand (AD) refers to the total amount that different sectors in the economy willingly spend in a given period. More specifically, it is the total amount of money which all sections — households, firms, and governments — are ready to spend on the purchase of goods and services produced in an economy during a given period.

In other words, AD is the total spending by all entities in the economy. It depends on the level of prices as well as on monetary policy, fiscal policy, and other factors.

🧩
Four Components of AD
AD = C + I + G + (X − M)
Aggregate Demand Formula
AD = C + I + G + (X − M)
🏠
C — Consumption

Household consumption demand. Primarily determined by disposable income, household wealth, longer-term income trends, and the aggregate price level.

🏭
I — Investment

Private purchases of structures, equipment, and inventories. Major determinants: level of output, cost of capital, and future expectations.

🏛️
G — Government Spending

Government purchases of goods and services. Determined directly by government spending decisions.

🌐
(X − M) — Net Exports

Value of exports minus value of imports. Determined by domestic and foreign incomes, relative prices, and exchange rates.

📉
The Aggregate Demand Curve
MoE Textbook §2.1.2, p. 27
📌 The AD Curve

The AD curve is a schedule that shows the amount of real output that buyers collectively desire to purchase at each price level, ceteris paribus. The relationship between price level and real GDP demanded is inverse or negative — hence the curve slopes downward.

The AD curve slopes downward: as price level falls (P₁ → P₀), quantity demanded rises (Q₁ → Q₀).
📝 Connection to Grade 9

Remember what you learned about the relationship between the price of an individual commodity and its demand (law of demand). The AD curve follows the same inverse principle — but for the whole economy.

🎯
Why Does the AD Curve Slope Downward?
Three effects
💰
1. Real Balance Effect

A rise in the price level causes purchasing power to fall, decreasing monetary wealth. Less wealthy people demand less real GDP.

📊
2. Interest Rate Effect

Higher price level → higher demand for money → higher interest rates → less investment and consumption spending → less real output demanded.

🌐
3. International Trade Effect

If Ethiopian prices rise, Ethiopian goods become relatively more expensive than foreign goods. Both Ethiopians and foreigners buy fewer Ethiopian goods.

Interest Rate Effect — Step by Step

  • Price level rises
  • Demand for money increases (people need more cash for purchases)
  • Given fixed money supply, the "price" of money — the interest rate — rises
  • Higher interest rates curtail investment spending and interest-sensitive consumption
  • Result: real output demanded falls
📝 Example

A firm expecting a 6% return on a capital purchase will invest when the interest rate is 5% — but not when it rises to 7%. Similarly, consumers may delay buying a house or car as interest rates rise.

🔄
Movement vs Shift
A critical distinction
Change in Quantity DemandedChange in Aggregate Demand
Caused by Change in price level Change in spending determinants (C, I, G, NE)
Shown as Movement along the same AD curve Shift of the AD curve (right or left)
Direction Price ↓ → Qd ↑ (move down-right) Spending ↑ → AD shifts right

Shifts in the AD Curve

An increase in AD shifts the curve right (AD₀ → AD₁). A decrease shifts it left (AD₀ → AD₂).
📊
What Shifts the AD Curve?
Factors affecting C, I, G, NE

Factors Affecting Consumption (C)

Factors Affecting Investment (I)

FactorEffect on IEffect on AD
Interest rate ↑Cost of investment ↑ → I ↓I ↓ → AD ↓ (left)
Businesses optimisticExpect higher future sales → I ↑I ↑ → AD ↑ (right)
Business taxes ↑After-tax profits ↓ → I ↓I ↓ → AD ↓ (left)

Factors Affecting Government Spending (G)

  • G ↑ (e.g., on military equipment) → AD shifts right (if taxes and interest rates don't change)
  • G ↓ (e.g., fewer transportation projects) → AD shifts left

Factors Affecting Net Exports (X − M)

  • Foreign income ↑ → foreigners buy more Ethiopian goods → exports ↑ → net exports ↑ → AD shifts right
  • Foreign income ↓ → foreigners buy fewer Ethiopian goods → exports ↓ → net exports ↓ → AD shifts left
  • Birr depreciates → exports ↑, imports ↓ → net exports ↑ → AD shifts right
  • Birr appreciates → exports ↓, imports ↑ → net exports ↓ → AD shifts left
📝 Exchange rate example

An Ethiopian leather jacket costs 1,500 Birr. At 1 Birr = 2 Rupees, an Indian pays 3,000 Rupees. If the Rupee depreciates to 3 Rupees = 1 Birr, the same jacket now costs 4,500 Rupees — making Ethiopian exports more expensive for Indian buyers.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 2.1
What you should remember
  • Aggregate demand (AD) = total spending by all sectors in a given period
  • AD = C + I + G + (X − M)
  • The AD curve slopes downward — inverse relationship between price level and real GDP demanded
  • Three reasons for downward slope: real balance effect, interest rate effect, international trade effect
  • A change in price level → movement along the AD curve
  • A change in spending determinants → shift of the AD curve
  • Rightward shift: C ↑, I ↑, G ↑, or NE ↑
  • Leftward shift: C ↓, I ↓, G ↓, or NE ↓
  • Currency depreciation → exports ↑, imports ↓ → AD shifts right
  • Currency appreciation → exports ↓, imports ↑ → AD shifts left
