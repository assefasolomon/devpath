⚖️ Equilibrium & Shocks in the AD-AS Model

When aggregate demand meets aggregate supply, we get the equilibrium price level and real GDP. This final lesson shows how the economy reaches equilibrium — and what happens when demand or supply shocks hit.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
Bringing AD and AS together

Now we bring aggregate demand and aggregate supply together to see how the market price level and real GDP are determined. The intersection of AD and SRAS gives short-run equilibrium; the intersection of AD and LRAS gives long-run equilibrium.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define short-run equilibrium (AD ∩ SRAS)
  • Define long-run equilibrium (AD ∩ LRAS)
  • Explain the effects of aggregate demand shocks
  • Explain the effects of aggregate supply shocks
  • Analyse how shocks change price level and real GDP
📍
Short-Run Equilibrium
MoE Textbook §2.3, p. 41
📌 Short-Run Equilibrium

Short-run equilibrium occurs at the intersection of the aggregate demand curve and the short-run aggregate supply curve. At this point:

Short-Run Equilibrium Condition
Quantity of real GDP demanded = Quantity of real GDP supplied (short-run)

At the equilibrium point E: PE is the short-run equilibrium price level, and QE is the short-run equilibrium real GDP.

Short-run equilibrium (E) occurs where AD intersects SRAS. At prices above P_E, there's a surplus; at prices below, there's a shortage.

How the Market Reaches Equilibrium

  • At price P₁ (above P_E): Quantity supplied > Quantity demanded → surplus → price level drops → firms decrease output, consumers increase consumption
  • At price P₂ (below P_E): Quantity supplied < Quantity demanded → shortage → price level rises → firms increase output, consumers decrease consumption
  • In both cases, economic forces move the economy toward point E
📝 Why do consumers increase consumption when prices drop?

Three effects at work: real balance effect (money buys more), interest rate effect (lower money demand → lower interest rates → more spending), and international trade effect (exports become more competitive).

📏
Long-Run Equilibrium
MoE Textbook §2.3
📌 Long-Run Equilibrium

Long-run equilibrium identifies the level of real GDP the economy produces when wages and prices have adjusted to their final equilibrium levels and there are no misperceptions on the part of workers. It occurs where the AD curve intersects the LRAS curve.

In long-run equilibrium, the economy produces its natural real GDP (QN) — also called full-employment real GDP.

Long-run equilibrium (E_LR) occurs where AD intersects LRAS. The economy produces its natural real GDP (Q_N) at price level P*.
📝 Static equilibrium

If the AD and AS curves were to remain unchanged, the economy would continue to produce the natural rate of output and have an equilibrium price indefinitely.

📉
Shocks to Aggregate Demand
MoE Textbook §2.3.1, p. 43
📌 Demand Shock

A demand shock is an unexpected shift of the AD curve. It changes the price level and real GDP in the same direction.

Two Types

📈
Positive Demand Shock

AD shifts right. Price level ↑ and real GDP ↑. Caused by: ↑C, ↑I, ↑G, ↑NE.

📉
Negative Demand Shock

AD shifts left. Price level ↓ and real GDP ↓. Caused by: ↓C, ↓I, ↓G, ↓NE.

A positive demand shock shifts AD₀ → AD₁ (higher price & higher output). A negative demand shock shifts AD₀ → AD₂ (lower price & lower output).
📊
Shocks to Aggregate Supply
MoE Textbook §2.3.2, p. 44
📌 Supply Shock

A supply shock is an unexpected shift of the SRAS curve. Aggregate supply shocks cause the price level and real GDP to change in opposite directions.

Two Types

✅
Positive Supply Shock

SRAS shifts right. Price level ↓, real GDP ↑. Caused by: technology improvement, more resources, better weather.

⚠️
Negative Supply Shock

SRAS shifts left. Price level ↑, real GDP ↓. Caused by: oil price spike, crop-destroying storm, imported input interruption.

Negative supply shock: SRAS₁ → SRAS₀ (higher price, lower output = stagflation). Positive supply shock: SRAS₁ → SRAS₂ (lower price, higher output).
📝 Example: Oil price shock

An increase in the price of oil shifts SRAS left (from SRAS₁ to SRAS₀). Output falls and prices rise — stagflation (stagnation + inflation).

📋
Summary Table — Shock Effects
Price level and real GDP response
Shock Type Curve Moves Price Level (P) Real GDP (Q)
Positive demand shock AD → right ↑ Up ↑ Up
Negative demand shock AD → left ↓ Down ↓ Down
Positive supply shock SRAS → right ↓ Down ↑ Up
Negative supply shock SRAS → left ↑ Up ↓ Down
📝 Key insight

Demand shocks move price level and real GDP in the same direction.
Supply shocks move them in opposite directions. This is why supply shocks are harder to manage — you can't fight both inflation and unemployment at the same time.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 2.3
What you should remember
  • Short-run equilibrium = AD ∩ SRAS
  • Long-run equilibrium = AD ∩ LRAS; economy produces natural real GDP (QN)
  • At prices above PE, there's a surplus; at prices below, there's a shortage
  • Positive demand shock: AD shifts right → P ↑, Q ↑
  • Negative demand shock: AD shifts left → P ↓, Q ↓
  • Positive supply shock: SRAS shifts right → P ↓, Q ↑
  • Negative supply shock: SRAS shifts left → P ↑, Q ↓ → stagflation
  • Demand shocks move P and Q in the same direction
  • Supply shocks move P and Q in opposite directions
  • Supply shocks are harder to manage — you can't fight inflation and unemployment at the same time
