📉 Concept of Poverty and Its Measurement

Poverty is multidimensional — it is more than just low income. In this lesson, you'll learn how poverty is defined, the six dimensions poor people themselves identify, the difference between absolute and relative poverty, and how poverty is measured.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Poverty and inequality are interrelated concepts and a daily reality for millions of people around the world. This lesson opens Unit 6 by asking the most fundamental question: what is poverty, and how do we measure it? You'll learn from the voices of poor people themselves, and from the formal definitions used by institutions like the World Bank and the UNDP.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define poverty and explain why it is multidimensional
  • List the six dimensions of poverty as identified by poor people themselves
  • Distinguish absolute from relative poverty
  • Explain the steps in measuring poverty
  • Describe the Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)
  • Explain Sen's Capability Approach to poverty and wellbeing
📌
What is Poverty?
MoE Textbook §6.1.1, pp. 123–125
📌 World Bank Definition (2000)

Poverty is "the pronounced deprivation in wellbeing". Wellbeing is seen from three angles: (1) command over commodities (income/consumption), (2) specific types of consumption (food poor, health poor, house poor), and (3) the capability of the individual to function in society.

Voices of the Poor

The textbook begins by quoting how poor people describe their own circumstances:

  • "When one is poor, she has no say in public, she feels inferior. She has no food, so there is famine in her house; no clothing, and no progress in her family." — A poor woman from Uganda
  • "...low salaries and lack of jobs. And it's not having medicine, food, and clothes." — A discussion group in Brazil
  • "Life in the area is so precarious that the youth and every able person has to migrate to the towns or join the army at the war front in order to escape the hazards of hunger escalating over here." — A discussion group in rural Ethiopia

These voices show poverty is multidimensional — it is rarely about the lack of only one thing.

🔍
Six Dimensions of Poverty
As identified by poor people themselves (Nafziger, 2006)
🍞
1. Hunger — The Bottom Line

Poverty consists of many interlocked dimensions, but the bottom line is always hunger — the lack of food.

😔
2. Psychological Dimensions

Powerlessness, voicelessness, dependency, shame, and humiliation. Cultural identity and solidarity help the poor maintain their humanity.

🛣️
3. Lack of Infrastructure

No access to roads (especially rural), transportation, and clean water.

📚
4. Thirst for Education

Widespread desire for literacy, but schooling receives mixed reviews. Education offers escape — only if the economy and quality improve.

🏥
5. Poor Health & Illness

Dreaded as a source of destitution — due to health care costs AND income lost from illness.

🏠
6. Managing Assets

The poor rarely speak of income, but focus on managing physical, human, social, and environmental assets to cope with vulnerability — often with a gender dimension.

📝 Key insight

Poverty encompasses both income and non-income dimensions of deprivation — lack of income, lack of access to basic social services (education, health, safe water), lack of personal security, and lack of empowerment to participate in political decisions.

Who Are the Poor?

Empirical data from developing nations shows the poor are disproportionately:

  • Located in rural areas
  • Primarily engaged in agriculture and associated activities
  • More likely to be women and children than adult males
  • Often concentrated among minority ethnic groups and indigenous peoples
📝 Regional pattern

On average, about 80% of all target poverty groups in Africa and Asia are located in rural areas. In Latin America, the figure is about 50%.

📏
Measuring Poverty
MoE Textbook §6.1.3, pp. 126–127

Why Measure Poverty? Four Reasons

  • To keep poor people on the agenda
  • To identify poor people and target appropriate interventions
  • To monitor and evaluate projects and policy interventions geared to poor people
  • To evaluate the effectiveness of institutions whose goal is to help poor people

Three Steps to Measure Poverty

1️⃣
Define Welfare Indicator

Choose how to measure wellbeing (income, consumption, etc.).

2️⃣
Establish the Poverty Line

Set a minimum acceptable standard to separate the poor from the non-poor.

3️⃣
Generate a Summary Statistic

Aggregate information from the distribution of the welfare indicator relative to the poverty line.

Common Poverty Measures

MeasureDefinition
Poverty Headcount Index (H/N) Fraction of the population whose income or consumption falls below the absolute poverty line (Yp). H = headcount; N = total population.
Total Poverty Gap (TPG) Total amount of income needed to raise everyone below the poverty line up to that line. Sum of the differences between the poverty line and actual income for all poor people.
Average Poverty Gap (APG) TPG divided by the total population.
Foster-Greer-Thorbecke (FGT) Index A class of measures of absolute poverty. Helps understand the degree of income inequality among the poor.
🌐
Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)
Beyond income alone
📌 Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)

The MPI is the most prominent application of multidimensional poverty measurement. Developed by UNDP in 2010, it incorporates three dimensions at the household level: health, education, and wealth (standard of living). It recognizes that people suffer from multiple deprivations simultaneously.

The Three Dimensions and Their Indicators

DimensionWeightIndicators
Health Each indicator: 1/6 of MPI a) Whether any child has died in the family
b) Whether any adult or child is malnourished
Education Each indicator: 1/6 of MPI a) Whether at least one household member has completed 5 years of schooling
b) Whether any school-age child is out of school (grades 1–8)
Standard of Living Each indicator: 1/18 of MPI (6 indicators = 1/3 total) a) Lack of electricity
b) Insufficiently safe drinking water
c) Inadequate sanitation
d) Inadequate flooring
e) Unimproved cooking fuel
f) Lack of more than one of 5 assets (telephone, radio, TV, bicycle, motorbike/vehicle)
📝 The "dual cut-off" method

Sabina Alkire and James Foster extended the FGT index to multiple dimensions. A person is identified as multidimensionally poor through two cut-offs:
(1) Within each dimension (e.g., is the person deprived in health?)
(2) Number of dimensions in which a person must be deprived to count as poor.

🧠
Sen's Capability Approach
MoE Textbook §6.1.4, p. 128
📌 Sen's Capability Approach (1998 Nobel Laureate)

Amartya Sen argued that income and wealth are not ends in themselves but instruments for other purposes. What really matters for one's status as poor or non-poor is the capability to function. Poverty is not low wellbeing but the inability to pursue wellbeing because of the lack of economic means.

Key Concepts

  • Functionings — what a person does or can do with the commodities of given characteristics
  • Capabilities — the real freedoms that people have to achieve their potential doings and beings
  • Conversion factors — personal, socio-political, and environmental conditions that determine whether someone can convert resources into functionings
📝 The book vs. illiteracy example

Sen's famous example: "A book is of little value to an illiterate person, except perhaps as a cooking fuel." This illustrates that what matters is not what a person has, but what a person is, or can be, and does, or can do.

What is Human Wellbeing?

According to Sen, being well means:

  • Being healthy, well nourished, well clothed
  • Being literate and long-lived
  • Being able to take part in the life of the community
  • Being mobile and having freedom of choice in what one can become and can do
📝 Why this matters

The lack of these capabilities indicates deficiency in wellbeing — and therefore poverty. This is a much broader view than just measuring income or headcounts.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 6.1
What you should remember
  • Poverty = pronounced deprivation in wellbeing (World Bank, 2000)
  • Poverty is multidimensional — income plus non-income deprivations
  • Six dimensions identified by poor people: hunger, psychological dimensions, lack of infrastructure, thirst for education, poor health, and managing assets
  • Absolute poverty: inability to meet subsistence essentials; relative poverty: comparison with others' standing
  • Four reasons to measure poverty: keep poor on agenda, identify the poor, monitor/evaluate, evaluate institutions
  • Three steps: define welfare indicator → establish poverty line → generate summary statistic
  • Common measures: headcount index, total poverty gap, average poverty gap, FGT index
  • MPI (UNDP 2010): three dimensions — health, education, standard of living — with "dual cut-off" method
  • Sen's Capability Approach: what matters is not what a person has, but what they can be and do — capabilities and functionings
  • Poor people disproportionately live in rural areas, work in agriculture, are women and children, and belong to minority groups
