📊 Concept of Inequality and Its Measurements

Inequality is closely linked to poverty — but it's a different concept. This lesson covers how income is distributed (functional vs personal), how to measure inequality with quintiles, the Lorenz Curve, and the Gini Coefficient.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

In Lesson 6.1 we explored poverty and its measurement. Now we look at inequality — the unequal distribution of income, wealth, and opportunity across a society. Inequality is closely related to poverty, but it asks a different question: not just "how many are poor?" but "how far apart are the rich and the poor?"

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define economic inequality
  • Distinguish functional from personal distribution of income
  • Explain how to measure inequality using quintiles and deciles
  • Interpret the Lorenz Curve
  • Calculate and interpret the Gini Coefficient
📌
What is Inequality?
MoE Textbook §6.2, p. 129
📌 Inequality

Inequality refers to the situation of being unequal or uneven, or a social disparity of distribution or opportunity. Inequalities take different dimensions: economic, social, political, etc. — including inequalities of power, prestige, status, gender, job satisfaction, conditions of work, degree of participation, and freedom of choice.

📌 Economic Inequality (focus of this lesson)

Economic inequality is the disproportionate distribution of total national income among households. This income distribution is divided into two types:

  • Functional / factor share distribution of income
  • Personal / size distribution of income
🏭
Functional Distribution of Income
How factors of production share total income
📌 Functional / Factor Share Distribution

Depicts the share of total national income that each of the factors of production (land, labour, and capital) receives. The theory of functional income distribution asks:

  • What percentage does labour receive as a whole?
  • How does this compare with the percentages distributed as rent (land), interest (financial capital), and profit (physical capital)?
📝 Core idea

The functional distribution attempts to explain the incomes of factors of production by the contribution they make to production (Todaro and Smith, 2015).

👥
Personal Distribution of Income
How income is distributed across households
📌 Personal / Size Distribution

The most commonly used indicator of income distribution. It deals with individual persons or households and the total incomes they receive, regardless of how they received that income.

How to Build a Size Distribution

1️⃣
Arrange Individuals

Order all individuals by ascending personal income.

2️⃣
Group into Quintiles/Deciles

Divide the population into fifths (quintiles) or tenths (deciles) according to ascending income.

3️⃣
Determine Income Shares

Find what proportion of total national income each group receives.

🔢

📌 Kuznets Ratio

The ratio of the incomes received by the top 20% and the bottom 40% of the population — used as a measure of the degree of inequality between high-income and low-income groups in a country. Named after economist Simon Kuznets.

Example calculation: bottom 40% receives 14%; top 20% receives 51%. Kuznets ratio = 14 / 51 ≈ 0.28

📝 What this tells us

A lower Kuznets ratio means more equal distribution. A higher ratio means more inequality. In this example, the single richest person (15% of income) receives more than the entire bottom 40% (14%) combined — a stark illustration of income concentration.

📈
The Lorenz Curve
MoE Textbook §6.2, pp. 131–133
📌 Lorenz Curve

The Lorenz Curve shows the cumulative shares of income received by individuals or groups. It was developed by economist Max Lorenz in 1905 as a way to analyze personal income statistics.

How to Construct It

  • Horizontal axis: cumulative percentage of income recipients (from 0% to 100%, poorest to richest)
  • Vertical axis: cumulative percentage of income received (from 0% to 100%)
  • 45-degree diagonal line: the "line of perfect equality" — each % of population receives the same % of income
  • The Lorenz Curve: plots the actual cumulative income shares — always bowed below the diagonal
The Lorenz Curve shows income inequality. The further the curve bows from the diagonal, the more unequal the distribution.

Reading the Curve

  • If income were perfectly equal, the Lorenz Curve would coincide with the 45-degree diagonal
  • In practice, Lorenz curves are located below the 45-degree line — between it and the horizontal axis
  • The more bowed the curve, the less equal the distribution
  • A point halfway along the diagonal means 50% of the population gets 50% of income
🎯
The Gini Coefficient
MoE Textbook §6.2, p. 133
📌 Gini Coefficient

The Gini Coefficient is a measure of inequality expressed by the ratio of the area between the Lorenz Curve and the 45° line (area A) to the total area under the 45° line (area A + B).

Gini = A / (A + B)

Named after Italian statistician Corrado Gini, who formulated it in 1912.

Interpreting the Gini Value

Gini ValueMeaning
0Perfect equality — Lorenz Curve coincides with the diagonal; income shared equally
1Perfect inequality — one person owns all income
Between 0 and 1The higher the value, the more unequal the distribution
📝 Worked example

Country A has Gini = 0.28; Country B has Gini = 0.60.
→ Country B has more unequal income distribution than Country A.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 6.2
What you should remember
  • Inequality = situation of being unequal or uneven; a social disparity of distribution or opportunity
  • Economic inequality = disproportionate distribution of total national income among households
  • Functional distribution: income shares received by each factor of production (land, labour, capital)
  • Personal distribution: income distribution across individual persons or households — the most commonly used measure
  • Build a size distribution by arranging individuals by income, grouping into quintiles or deciles, and measuring income shares
  • Kuznets ratio = top 20% income share ÷ bottom 40% income share
  • Lorenz Curve: plots cumulative income shares against cumulative population; the more bowed, the more unequal
  • The 45° diagonal = line of perfect equality
  • Gini Coefficient = A / (A + B), where A = area between Lorenz Curve and diagonal; B = area under the Lorenz Curve
  • Gini = 0 → perfect equality; Gini = 1 → perfect inequality
  • Higher Gini → more unequal distribution (e.g., Gini 0.60 more unequal than Gini 0.28)
