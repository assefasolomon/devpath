📖 Definition & Focus Areas of Macroeconomics

Macroeconomics is the study of the economy as a whole — but how did it emerge, what does it focus on, and how do we measure its performance? This lesson answers all three questions.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Economics is divided into two big branches: microeconomics and macroeconomics. In Grade 9 you first met the idea of macroeconomics. Now, in Grade 12, we revisit its definition and focus areas in depth — because understanding how the whole economy works is essential for solving national problems like poverty, unemployment, and inflation.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Distinguish between microeconomics and macroeconomics
  • Describe the focus areas of macroeconomics
  • List the central macroeconomic questions
  • Define GDP and GNP and explain their relationship
  • Explain the three approaches to measuring GDP
  • Distinguish between nominal and real GDP
🔍
Microeconomics vs Macroeconomics
MoE Textbook §1.1.1, p. 2
📌 Macroeconomics (MoE Textbook, p. 2)

Macroeconomics studies the overall or aggregate behaviour of the economy — such as the overall level of output, prices, and employment. In other words, macroeconomics studies what happens to the whole economy or economic system.

The word "macro" comes from the Greek word "makro", meaning large. Macroeconomics is a relatively new branch of economics that emerged after the publication of John Maynard Keynes' book The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money in 1936.

📝 Complementary — not competing

Microeconomics and macroeconomics are complementary perspectives on the same subject. Together they give a full picture of how economies work.

Focus Areas of Macroeconomics
MoE Textbook §1.1.2, p. 3

The focus areas of macroeconomics are the aggregate behaviour of the economy, such as economic growth, employment, inflation, distribution of income, macroeconomic policies, and international trade.

📝 Measuring performance

Macroeconomics is primarily concerned with forecasting GDP by analysing the major economic factors that show predictable trends and patterns.

Central Macroeconomic Questions
What macroeconomists try to answer
📈
Output & Growth

What factors determine the flow of total output over time? How can a nation increase its rate of economic growth?

💼
Employment

Why do outputs and employment sometimes fail? How can full employment be maintained?

💸
Inflation

What are the causes of inflation and how can it be controlled?

🏛️
Government Policy

How do government policies affect output, unemployment, inflation, and growth?

🌐
International Trade

How does the domestic economy interact with the rest of the world?

🔄
Business Cycles

How can business cycle downturns be managed?

Macroeconomic Goals

  • Achieving economic growth
  • Ensuring full employment
  • Maintaining price stability
  • Reducing budget or balance of payment deficit
  • Ensuring fair distribution of income in society

GDP & GNP
Two ways to measure the economy
📌 Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

GDP is the measure of the market value of all final goods and services produced in a country during a year, regardless of who produces them.

GDP Formula
GDP = Σ Pᵢ Qᵢ
  • Pᵢ = series of prices of outputs produced in different sectors
  • Qᵢ = quantity of various final goods and services produced
📌 Gross National Product (GNP)

GNP is the total value of final goods and services produced by domestically owned factors of production in a given period (usually one year), irrespective of their geographical location.

Relationship between GDP & GNP
GNP = GDP + NFI
  • NFI = Net Factor Income from abroad
  • NFI = (factor income received from abroad by citizens) − (factor income paid to foreigners)
GDP uses the geographic principle; GNP uses the nationality principle. Adding Net Factor Income (NFI) to GDP gives GNP.
📝 Closed economy case

If a country has no interaction with the rest of the world, then GNP = GDP.

📐
Three Approaches to Measure GDP/GNP
MoE Textbook §1.1.2
🏭
1. Product / Value-Added

Add the market value of goods and services currently produced by each sector. Only final goods are counted, to avoid double counting.

💳
2. Expenditure

Add all expenditures on final goods and services: C + I + G + NE — household consumption, private investment, government purchases, and net exports.

💵
3. Income

Add all incomes earned by factors of production — wages, rent, interest, and profits — plus other claims, less subsidies and transfer payments.

Expenditure Approach Formula
GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
📝 Why three approaches?

All three approaches should give the same GDP figure, because what is produced (product), what is spent (expenditure), and what is earned (income) are just three views of the same economic activity.

🔢
Nominal vs Real GDP
Adjusting for price changes
TypeDefinitionPurpose
Nominal GDP GDP measured in actual (current) market prices Shows value in today's money
Real GDP GDP calculated in constant prices using a base year Removes the effect of inflation — shows true growth
📝 Which one matters most?

Real GDP is the most widely used measure of the level and growth of output. An upward or downward movement in real GDP tells us how the economy is actually performing — without distortion from rising prices.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 1.1
What you should remember
  • Macroeconomics studies the aggregate behaviour of the economy; microeconomics studies individual units
  • Macroeconomics emerged as a distinct field after Keynes' General Theory in 1936
  • Focus areas include: national income, employment, money, growth, price level, international trade, government budget
  • Central questions: output, growth, employment, inflation, policy, business cycles, international interaction
  • Macroeconomic goals: economic growth, full employment, price stability, fair income distribution, reduced deficit
  • GDP = market value of all final goods and services produced inside a country in a year
  • GNP = value produced by a country's citizens anywhere in the world
  • GNP = GDP + NFI (Net Factor Income from abroad)
  • Three approaches to measure GDP: product, expenditure, and income
  • Nominal GDP uses current prices; real GDP uses constant (base-year) prices to remove inflation
