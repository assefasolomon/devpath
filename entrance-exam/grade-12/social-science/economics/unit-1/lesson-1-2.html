Unit 1 · Macroeconomics · MoE Textbook §1.2

📈 Key Challenges in Macroeconomics

Once we measure the economy's performance, we face its biggest challenges: keeping growth strong, prices stable, workers employed, and trade balanced. This lesson covers the five key challenges every macroeconomy must confront.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
The five key challenges

Once the key macroeconomic variables and their measurement are determined, subsequent themes centre around the key challenges of the macroeconomy — economic growth, inflation, unemployment, business cycles, and trade balance.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define economic growth and explain its limitations
  • Define inflation and calculate the inflation rate using CPI
  • Identify the types of inflation by speed and cause
  • Define unemployment and distinguish its types
  • Describe the phases of the business cycle
  • Explain balance of trade — surplus, deficit, and balanced trade
📊
Economic Growth
MoE Textbook §1.2.1, p. 6
📌 Economic Growth

Economic growth represents an increase in the capacity of an economy to produce goods and services. It is a necessary ingredient for both high incomes and higher living standards.

The economic growth rate is the rate at which real GDP increases over a period of time. Higher GDP per capita translates to better diet, health, life expectancy, and greater educational opportunity.

Why Developing Countries Understate Real Income

  • Many are subsistence farmers — output for own consumption isn't correctly reported
  • Underreporting due to fear of tax
  • No allowance for non-monetary sectors
  • Higher price distortions in developing countries
  • Pollution and environmental degradation costs are not deducted
📝 Two limitations of the simple growth measure

1. It is in value terms — so inflation distorts it.
2. It does not consider population growth — some countries' populations grow faster than national income.

The Solution: Real Per Capita Income

Economists suggested measuring growth in terms of a rise in real per capita income = total GNP ÷ total population. But as an average, it conceals more than it reveals.

📌 Human Development Index (HDI)

Deployed by the UNDP and sometimes called the "quality of life index," HDI is a non-monetary composite index combining indicators like education, health, and income to measure development.

💸
Inflation
MoE Textbook §1.2.2, p. 8
📌 Inflation

Inflation represents a regular and continuous rise in the general price level. The general price level refers to the average price of goods and services. Inflation is cumulative — a small rise in the beginning may become very large in the future.

Key Effects of Inflation

  • Decreases the purchasing power of money
  • Increases the cost of living
  • When wages don't rise with prices, real wage rate falls
  • Governments may index contracts, wages, and interest rates to protect citizens
📌 Deflation

Deflation is a fall in the general price level — the opposite of inflation.

🧮
Consumer Price Index & Inflation Rate
How we measure price changes
📌 Consumer Price Index (CPI)

The CPI is a price index whose movement reflects changes in the prices of goods and services typically purchased by consumers.

Consumer Price Index
CPI = (Current cost of basket ÷ Base-year cost of basket) × 100
Inflation Rate
Inflation rate = [(CPIₜ − CPIₜ₋₁) ÷ CPIₜ₋₁] × 100%

Worked Example — Wheat & Oil

A typical consumer buys 50 kg wheat and 10 litres oil per year.

YearWheat (Birr/kg)Oil (Birr/L)Cost of BasketCPI
2000 EC210(2×50)+(10×10) = 200 Birr100
2001 EC313(3×50)+(13×10) = 280 Birr140
2002 EC214(2×50)+(14×10) = 240 Birr120
2003 EC420(4×50)+(20×10) = 400 Birr200

Base year: 2000 EC (CPI = 100).

  • 2001 EC inflation = (140 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = +40%
  • 2002 EC inflation = (120 − 140) ÷ 140 × 100 = −14.3% → deflation
  • 2003 EC inflation = (200 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = +66.6%
📝 Interpreting the numbers

A negative inflation rate = deflation. In 2002 EC, prices actually fell. In 2001 EC and 2003 EC, prices rose.

🔥
Types of Inflation
By speed and by cause

By Speed of Occurrence

TypeAnnual RateDescription
Creeping≤ 3%Moderate — prices rise slowly over time
Walking3% to < 10%Single-digit inflation
Running10% to 20%Prices rise rapidly
GallopingDouble/triple digitsQuick rate for a short period
Hyperinflation> 50% per monthPrices rise uncontrollably

By Cause

📈
Demand-Pull

Aggregate demand grows faster than supply — "too much money chasing too few goods."

📉
Cost-Push

Rising production costs (wages, raw materials) push prices up.

💵
Currency / Seigniorage

Governments print money to finance expenditure — an "inflation tax" on money holders.

📝 Historic hyperinflations

Hungary, July 1946: 41.9 quadrillion % per month — prices doubled every 15.3 hours.
Zimbabwe, 2007–2009: Prices doubled daily — the government printed a $100 trillion bill before the currency became worthless.

👥
Unemployment
MoE Textbook §1.2.3, p. 12
📌 Unemployment

A person is unemployed if he/she is not employed and has been looking for a job, or is on temporary layoff. People not in either category (e.g., full-time students, retirees) are not in the labour force.

Key Definitions

  • Labour force = Number of employed + Number of unemployed
  • Unemployment rate = (Number of unemployed ÷ Labour force) × 100
  • Labour-force participation rate = (Labour force ÷ Adult population) × 100
  • In Ethiopia, the working-age population is 14 to 60; formal employment requires 18+

Types of Unemployment

🔄
Frictional

Workers moving between jobs. Example: new graduates searching for their first job. Short-term and unavoidable.

📉
Cyclical

Caused by deficiency of aggregate demand — recessions. Example: workers laid off in a recession, or falling export demand.

🏗️
Structural

Mismatch between demand and supply of labour — skills don't match available jobs. Education is the key remedy.

⚠️
Underemployment

A person takes a job below their skill level or works full-time but earns below the poverty level.

📝 Opportunity cost of unemployment

When willing workers cannot find jobs, economic resources go unused. The opportunity cost of unemployment is the output that unemployed workers could have produced.

🔄
The Business Cycle
MoE Textbook §1.2.4, p. 15
📌 Business Cycle

The business cycle (also called trade cycle, economic cycle, or boom-bust cycle) refers to economy-wide fluctuations in production, trade, and general economic activities. It represents upward and downward movements in GDP around a long-term growth trend.

The business cycle has four distinct phases: expansion, peak, contraction, and trough. The dashed line shows the long-term growth trend.

Four Phases

PhaseCharacteristics
Expansion (Recovery)Increasing employment, economic growth, upward pressure on prices
Peak (Boom)Highest point — economy producing at maximum output, full employment, inflationary pressures
Contraction (Recession)Growth slows, employment declines, unemployment increases, pricing pressures subside
Trough (Depression)Lowest point — economy hits bottom, next expansion emerges from here
📝 Key point

Economic fluctuations are not regular and predictable. Recessions are common but irregular. Modern society tries to control the business cycle with appropriate economic policies.

🌐
Balance of Trade
MoE Textbook §1.2.5, p. 16
📌 Balance of Trade

The balance of trade is the difference between exports and imports of goods and services of a country for a given period. It is an important component of the balance of payment.

Three Possible Outcomes

OutcomeConditionMeaning
Trade DeficitImports > ExportsCountry is buying more than it sells
Trade SurplusExports > ImportsCountry is selling more than it buys
Balanced TradeExports = ImportsTrade is in equilibrium

Real-World Comparison (2020)

  • Ethiopia: trade deficit of about US$10.7 billion
  • China: commodity trade surplus of about US$535 billion
📝 When trade is out of balance

Some degree of trade deficit can be tolerable — especially when it reflects importing capital equipment that boosts productivity. But large, persistent deficits can trigger financial crises. Foreign investors lose confidence, move their money abroad, and the economy drops into deep recession (e.g., Mexico 1995, Asian financial crisis 1997-98, Russia & Argentina early 2000s).

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 1.2
What you should remember
  • Economic growth = increase in capacity to produce goods and services; measured by the growth rate of real GDP
  • Developing countries understate real income due to subsistence farming, tax evasion, no non-monetary sectors, and unaccounted pollution
  • Inflation = continuous rise in the general price level; deflation = fall in the price level
  • CPI = (Current cost of basket ÷ Base-year cost of basket) × 100
  • Inflation rate = [(CPIₜ − CPIₜ₋₁) ÷ CPIₜ₋₁] × 100%
  • Types by speed: creeping (≤3%), walking (3–10%), running (10–20%), galloping, hyperinflation (>50%/month)
  • Types by cause: demand-pull, cost-push, currency/seigniorage
  • Three types of unemployment: frictional, cyclical, structural — plus underemployment
  • Business cycle phases: expansion → peak → contraction → trough
  • Balance of trade: deficit (imports > exports), surplus (exports > imports), or balanced
