🏛️ Schools of Thought in Macroeconomic Analysis

Macroeconomics didn't emerge as a single unified theory. Different schools of thought — Classical, Keynesian, Monetarist, New Classical, and New Keynesian — have each shaped how we understand the economy. This lesson compares them all.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
Five schools, one big question

From the inception of macroeconomics, different schools of thought have engaged in macroeconomic analysis. Each asks the same big question — how does the macroeconomy work, and should the government intervene? — and each answers it differently.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Describe the Classical and Neoclassical view
  • Explain the Keynesian response to the Great Depression
  • Describe the Monetarist critique of Keynesianism
  • Explain the New Classical and New Keynesian syntheses
  • Compare all five schools across key policy questions
🏛️
Classical & Neoclassical
MoE Textbook §1.3.2, p. 18
🏛️
Classical School
Adam Smith · David Ricardo · Say · Mill · Fisher · pre-1930s

The classical view was the predominant economic philosophy until the Great Depression. It held that flexible prices and wages would quickly adjust to restore full employment. The economy is self-correcting — government intervention is unnecessary.

If an economy slips into recession, prices fall, surpluses disappear, and full employment returns automatically. Any active fiscal or monetary policy would only cause inflation, not increase GDP.

"Hands-off" policy Flexible prices Say's Law Full employment
📌 Say's Law

"Supply creates its own demand" — the classical belief that producing goods automatically generates enough income to buy them. The Great Depression contradicted this.

Supply-Siders

Linked to classical preference for supply-side policies are economists Arthur Laffer and Robert Mundell. Supply-siders despise government intervention and emphasize the distorting effects of taxation. Their policy advice: cut tax rates to stimulate the economy.

📌 The Laffer Curve

Formalized by Arthur Laffer, this curve shows the relationship between tax rates and tax revenue. It illustrates that cutting tax rates can sometimes actually increase total tax revenue.

Neoclassical Economics

Developed at the end of the 19th and beginning of the 20th century. Main contributors: Leon Walras, Alfred Marshall, Vilfredo Pareto. They closely follow classical findings but emphasize the limitations of laissez-faire, use marginal utility, and accept mathematical economics.

  • In the long run, the economy fluctuates around its potential GDP and its natural rate of unemployment
  • Potential GDP determines the economy's size
  • Wages and prices adjust flexibly
  • Government should focus on long-term growth and controlling inflation, not recessions
  • Neoclassical is more useful for long-run analysis; Keynesian is more useful for the short run
🔑
Keynesian Economics
MoE Textbook §1.3.3, p. 19
🔑
Keynesian School
John Maynard Keynes (1883–1946) · 1936

The Great Depression of the early 1930s changed classical thinking. The self-correcting feature of the market did not work. Real GDP was less than potential GDP. Keynesian economics focused on explaining recessions and offering policy prescriptions for minimizing them.

Keynes prescribed active fiscal policy to alleviate weak aggregate demand. Firms produce only if they expect to sell — so aggregate demand drives real GDP.

Active fiscal policy Sticky wages/prices Aggregate demand Short-run focus

Two Building Blocks of the Keynesian View

1️⃣
Aggregate Demand May Be Low

Aggregate demand is not always automatically high enough to provide firms with an incentive to hire enough workers to reach full employment.

2️⃣
Sticky Wages & Prices

The macroeconomy may adjust only slowly to shifts in aggregate demand because wages and prices are sticky — they don't respond quickly to changes in demand.

📝 Keynes on monetary vs fiscal policy

Monetary policy is of little help — additional money is absorbed by investors with no noticeable effect on the interest rate. Fiscal policy works well — additional government spending stimulates aggregate demand and hence employment and output.

💵
Monetarism
MoE Textbook §1.3.4, p. 20
💵
Monetarist School
Milton Friedman (1912–2006) and colleagues

Monetarists argued that it is monetary policy — not fiscal policy — that addresses macroeconomic problems. Since they assumed interest sensitivity of investment to be very high, fiscal policy leads only to strong crowding out of private investment.

Friedman suggested the central bank should follow a constant growth rule for some monetary aggregate and not tinker with monetary policy to influence aggregate demand and employment.

Monetary policy Constant money growth rule Anti-discretion
📐
New Classical Economics
MoE Textbook §1.3.5, p. 21
📐
New Classical School
Lucas · Sargent · Barro · Prescott · 1970s–1980s

The new classicals are the natural successors of classical economists — modern-day classicals. They stress mathematical techniques and support classical ideas like flexible prices and wages, rational expectations, market efficiency, and full employment.

All observable fluctuations are due to rational agents responding to incentives, not nominal rigidities. They introduced the "policy ineffectiveness proposition" — the policy maker either cannot or should not use countercyclical policy.

Rational expectations Policy ineffectiveness Micro foundations
📌 Policy Ineffectiveness Proposition (PIP)

The New Classical claim that systematic countercyclical policy has no real effect because rational agents anticipate it and adjust their behaviour accordingly.

🔑
New Keynesian Economics
MoE Textbook §1.3.6, p. 21
🔑
New Keynesian School
Akerlof · Phelps · Taylor · Fischer · Blanchard · Mankiw · 1970s–1980s

Just as the New Classicals succeeded the classicals, the New Keynesians succeeded Keynes. They accepted the rational expectations hypothesis (REH) but stressed the existence of nominal rigidities — arising from multi-period nominal wage contracts.

Such rigidities invalidate the policy ineffectiveness proposition. Hence, they argue for government to stabilize the economy, even under REH. The most recent wave is more micro-based — stressing imperfect competition, coordination failures, and credit restrictions.

Accept REH Nominal rigidities Pro-stabilization Micro-based

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 1.3
What you should remember
  • Classical: prices/wages are flexible, markets self-correct, no government intervention needed (Adam Smith, Say's Law)
  • Keynesian: aggregate demand drives output; sticky wages/prices; active fiscal policy works (Keynes, 1936)
  • Monetarist: monetary policy — not fiscal — is the key tool; constant money growth rule (Friedman)
  • New Classical: rational expectations; policy ineffectiveness proposition (Lucas, Sargent, Barro)
  • New Keynesian: accept REH but stress nominal rigidities; still argue for stabilization (Mankiw, Taylor, Blanchard)
  • Each school emerged in response to the limits of the previous one
  • The policy question — should governments intervene? — divides classicals from Keynesians
