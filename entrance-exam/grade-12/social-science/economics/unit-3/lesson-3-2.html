🏛️ Public Goods

Public goods are goods whose benefits are shared. Unlike private goods, they can't easily be sold in markets — because they're non-rival and non-excludable. In this lesson, we explore what makes a good "public" and why markets underprovide them.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Have you ever appreciated the satisfaction you got from using street lights? Or clean air? These are examples of public goods — goods whose benefits are shared and which markets often fail to provide efficiently.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define public goods and their two key features
  • Explain non-rivalry and non-excludability
  • Classify goods using the 2×2 table
  • Explain the free rider problem
  • Describe mechanisms for providing public goods
📌
Definition & Key Features
MoE Textbook §3.2, p. 51
📌 Public Goods (MoE Textbook, p. 51)

Public goods are goods whose benefits are shared. They generally have two major features:

1️⃣
Non-Rivalry in Consumption

Once the good is provided or supplied, consumption by one person does not reduce the quantity available for consumption by another. The marginal cost of allowing another person to consume the good is zero.

2️⃣
Non-Excludability

It is difficult and expensive to prevent or exclude someone (non-payers) from consuming the good.

📝 Pure public goods

Goods that fulfil both these properties are known as pure public goods. Examples: national defence, clean air, biodiversity, wilderness.

🔢
Classifying Goods: The 2×2 Table
Rivalry × Excludability

Goods can be classified based on two criteria: whether they are rivalrous (one person's use reduces availability to others) and whether they are excludable (sellers can prevent non-payers from consuming).

📝 Classification isn't absolute

Whether a good is public depends on market conditions and technology. Technology now allows radio or TV broadcasts to be encrypted, so those without a special decoder can be excluded — turning a public good into a club good. Similarly, toll booths on Ethiopian express roads (Addis Ababa–Adama, Addis Ababa–Hawassa) enforce payment for what would otherwise be non-excludable.

📝 Public ≠ government-provided

Public provision doesn't necessarily mean public production. A government may collect garbage using its own trucks (public provision + public production) or hire a private firm (public provision + private production). Similarly, many private goods (electricity, telecom) are supplied by governments, and many public goods (protection, guarding) are supplied by the private sector.

Unusual Public Goods

A number of things that are not conventionally considered commodities have public good characteristics:

  • Honesty — benefits everyone when widespread
  • Fair income distribution — public good-like
  • Certain information — a poem, mathematical theorem
  • Polio vaccination — herd immunity protects everyone
  • HIV/AIDS blood tests — public health benefit
🎭
The Free Rider Problem
Why markets underprovide public goods
📌 Free Rider (Social Loafer)

A free rider is a person who seeks to enjoy the benefit of public goods without contributing anything to the cost of financing the amount made.

This problem was first observed in trade unions — where non-members benefit from the successful bargaining of union members. As a result, non-members were not willing to become members and make a contribution.

Why Free Riding Arises

  • Public goods are non-excludable — it's difficult to exclude non-payers from using/benefiting
  • Therefore, there's an incentive not to pay
  • If individual A chooses to be a free rider, and B is equally smart, B also has an incentive to let others pay
  • Result: there is no automatic force that makes the good provided
  • Market fails to supply it — the private market may provide no output as no one is willing to purchase it
Carla gets the same benefit from street lighting as Alice and Bob — but she doesn't pay. This is the free rider problem.
📝 The tragedy of the commons

Free ridership is closely related to the tragedy of the commons — where common pool resources get overused because no one has an incentive to conserve them.

🛠️
Providing Public Goods Efficiently
When the market fails, collective action steps in

In cases where the private market fails to provide the efficient level of public goods, provision requires collective action. People need to realize that a public goods situation exists and either raise contributions from private individuals or let the government provide.

Four Mechanisms

🎬
Private Provision of Excludable Public Goods

Movies, music concerts — private firms provide when they can exclude non-payers (ticket required).

🏞️
Public Provision with Entrance Fees

National Parks charge entrance fees — the government provides but excludes non-payers via fee.

💰
Public Provision via Tax Revenues

National defence funded by taxes earmarked for it — the classic solution to non-excludable goods.

🙏
Religious Beliefs

Church/mosque services are public goods; during the ceremony a basket is passed around. Religion can prevent free riding by convincing people that God is watching.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 3.2
What you should remember
  • Public goods = goods whose benefits are shared
  • Two key features: non-rivalry and non-excludability
  • Non-rivalry = one person's consumption doesn't reduce availability to others
  • Non-excludability = difficult to prevent non-payers from consuming
  • Four types of goods: private (rival, excludable), common pool (rival, non-excludable), club (non-rival, excludable), public (non-rival, non-excludable)
  • Pure public goods: national defence, clean air, biodiversity, wilderness, free-to-air TV
  • The free rider problem = people enjoy public goods without contributing
  • Free riding arises because public goods are non-excludable
  • Mechanisms to provide public goods: private provision (movies), public with fees (National Parks), public via taxes (defence), religious beliefs (church collection)
  • Classification depends on technology and market conditions — encryption can turn a public good into a club good
