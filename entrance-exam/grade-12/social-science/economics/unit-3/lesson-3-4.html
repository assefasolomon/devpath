🔍 Asymmetric Information

When one party in a transaction knows more than the other — the seller knows more than the buyer, or the insured knows more than the insurer — the market can break down. This lesson explores adverse selection, moral hazard, and how signalling and screening solve the problem.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Have you ever bought a used car and discovered later that it had hidden problems? Or wondered why insurance companies ask so many questions? These situations arise from asymmetric information — when one party in a transaction has more information than the other.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define asymmetric information
  • Explain adverse selection (hidden information)
  • Explain moral hazard (hidden action)
  • Describe the solutions: signalling and screening
  • Distinguish adverse selection from moral hazard
📌
What is Asymmetric Information?
MoE Textbook §3.4, p. 60
📌 Asymmetric Information (MoE Textbook, p. 60)

Asymmetric information is a situation in which different agents have different amounts of information about a good. In other words, it occurs when one party in a transaction is in possession of more information than the other.

In certain transactions, sellers can take advantage of buyers because asymmetric information exists — the seller has more knowledge of the good being sold than the buyer.

Information asymmetry deals with the study of decisions in transactions where one party has more information than another. This creates an imbalance in power which can sometimes cause the transaction to go away.

Asymmetric information: the seller knows the true quality; the buyer doesn't. This imbalance can prevent efficient transactions.
📝 The two problems that arise

Two types of problems are associated with asymmetric information:
1. Adverse selection (hidden information)
2. Moral hazard (hidden action)

🎭
Adverse Selection Problem
Hidden information
📌 Adverse Selection

Adverse selection refers to situations where one side of the market cannot observe the "type" or quality of the goods on the other side of the market. It is sometimes known as the "hidden information problem" or "anti-selection problem".

This is a term used in economics, insurance, statistics, and risk management. When bad results occur due to information asymmetries between buyers and sellers, the bad products or customers are more likely to be selected.

Classic Examples

🚗
Used Cars (Lemons)

Sellers of good used cars can't prove quality; buyers discount all cars. Sellers of bad cars (lemons) are more willing to sell — driving good cars out of the market.

🏥
Health Insurance

Health insurance is more likely to be purchased by people who are more likely to get sick. The insurer can't tell who is high-risk.

Adverse selection in insurance: high-risk customers buy more, driving premiums up and healthy customers out — a downward spiral.
🎬
Moral Hazard Problem
Hidden action
📌 Moral Hazard

Moral hazard refers to a situation where one side of the market cannot observe the actions of the others. It is sometimes known as the "hidden action problem" — the inability to observe and/or verify an agent's action.

A moral hazard problem arises when an individual or situation does not bear the full consequences of its actions, and therefore has a tendency to act less carefully than it otherwise would — leaving another party to bear some responsibility for the consequences.

Classic Examples

🚙
Car Theft Insurance

An individual with insurance against automobile theft may be less vigilant about locking their car — because the insurance company bears part of the consequences.

🏥
Ex-Post Moral Hazard

Insured parties don't behave more riskily, but they ask the insurer to pay for more negative consequences as coverage increases — e.g., seeking unnecessary medical treatment.

📝 The medical insurance example

Without medical insurance, some forgo medical treatment due to its costs, or simply deal with substandard health. But after medical insurance becomes available, some may ask the insurance provider to pay for the cost of medical treatment that they would not otherwise have undergone.

Moral hazard: after getting insurance, people may act less carefully because they no longer bear the full cost of their actions.
🛠️
Solutions: Signalling & Screening
How markets cope with information asymmetry
📌 Signalling

Signalling is the idea that one party (the agent) conveys some meaningful information about itself to another party (the principal). It's a way for the more informed party to reveal their quality.

Signalling Examples

  • Education — Employees signal their skill levels to employers by acquiring a certain degree of education
  • Warranties — In the market for used goods, owners of good used cars can offer a warranty — signalling that they have good cars, since owners of bad cars cannot afford this
📝 Limitations of signalling

• How much time, energy, or money should the sender spend on sending the signal?
• How can the receiver trust the signal to be an honest declaration of information?
• Under what circumstances will the signalling equilibrium break down?

📌 Screening

Screening is a strategy of combating adverse selection. The screener (the party with less information) attempts to rectify this asymmetry by learning as much as possible about the other party.

Screening Examples

  • Banks screening borrowers — Banks screen people interested in borrowing money to weed out those who will not be able to repay. They ask for financial history, job security, reason for borrowing, education, experience.
  • Group health insurance — Offering health insurance as a package of fringe benefits. The insurance company can base rates on the averages over the set of employees, and is assured all employees will participate — eliminating adverse selection.
Signalling: the informed party reveals quality. Screening: the uninformed party investigates.
📋
Adverse Selection vs Moral Hazard
Key differences
Aspect Adverse Selection Moral Hazard
Timing Before the transaction After the transaction
Problem Hidden information (type/quality) Hidden action (behaviour)
What's unknown? The other party's characteristics The other party's behaviour
Classic example High-risk people buy more health insurance Insured people are less careful about theft
Solution Signalling and screening Monitoring, deductibles, co-payments
📝 Memory trick

Adverse selection = "Who is buying?" (selection of the wrong type).
Moral hazard = "How do they behave?" (change in behaviour after the deal).

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 3.4
What you should remember
  • Asymmetric information = different agents have different amounts of information about a good
  • It creates an imbalance in power in transactions — sometimes causing the transaction to fail
  • Two key problems: adverse selection and moral hazard
  • Adverse selection = hidden information problem — one side can't observe the type/quality of the other side
  • Adverse selection examples: used cars (lemons), health insurance (high-risk buyers)
  • Moral hazard = hidden action problem — one side can't observe the actions of the other
  • Moral hazard examples: car theft insurance, ex-post medical insurance claims
  • Signalling = informed party conveys meaningful info (education, warranties)
  • Screening = uninformed party learns about the other (bank credit checks, group insurance)
  • Key difference: adverse selection = before the transaction (who is buying); moral hazard = after (how do they behave)
