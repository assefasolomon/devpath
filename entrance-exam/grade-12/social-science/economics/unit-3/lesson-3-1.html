📉 Market Failure

In a free market, the equilibrium of a market is determined by the market forces of demand and supply. However, the equilibrium price and quantity may not be optimal — this is when market failure occurs. This lesson defines it and explores common types and solutions.

📖 Reading: ~20 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Sometimes the free market produces outcomes that are not optimal — tobacco and alcohol are over-consumed, while education and healthcare are under-consumed. These outcomes happen in the absence of government intervention. This is what economists call market failure.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define market failure
  • Explain allocative efficiency
  • Identify the common types of market failure
  • Describe solutions to market failures
  • Explain why market failure can exist in government activity too
📌
What is Market Failure?
MoE Textbook §3.1, p. 49
📌 Market Failure (MoE Textbook, p. 49)

Market failure is the economic situation defined by an inefficient distribution of goods and services in the free market. It occurs when the price mechanism fails to account for all of the costs and benefits that are necessary to provide and consume a good.

In other words, each individual makes the correct decision for himself/herself, but these may prove to be the wrong decisions for the group. In traditional microeconomics, this is shown as a steady-state disequilibrium in which the quantity supplied is not equal to the quantity demanded.

📝 What causes market failure?

The structure of market systems contributes to market failure. In the real world, markets cannot be perfect due to inefficient producers, externalities, environmental concerns, and lack of public goods.

⚖️
Allocative Efficiency
The benchmark of a perfect market
📌 Allocative Efficiency

Allocative efficiency is achieved when it is impossible to change the allocation of resources in the economy in a way that will increase the welfare of society. This occurs when marginal social benefit (MSB) = marginal social cost (MSC).

📈
Marginal Social Benefit

MSB = Marginal Private Benefit (MPB) + Marginal External Benefit (MEB)

📉
Marginal Social Cost

MSC = Marginal Private Cost (MPC) + Marginal External Cost (MEC)

📝 What are externalities?

External costs and benefits — or externalities — are costs and benefits of consumption or production which are experienced by society other than the producers or the consumers. They cause the difference between private and social costs/benefits, leading to market failure.

🧩
Common Types of Market Failure
MoE Textbook §3.1.1, p. 50

Commonly cited market failures include:

⚡
Externalities

Costs or benefits imposed on third parties not involved in a transaction. Can be positive (education) or negative (pollution).

👑
Monopoly

A single seller controls the market and can set prices above competitive levels, reducing consumer welfare.

🔍
Information Asymmetries

One party knows more than the other. Leads to adverse selection and moral hazard.

🔒
Factor Immobility

Resources (labour, capital) cannot move freely to where they are most needed.

🏛️
Public Goods Problem

Goods whose benefits are shared — producers can't exclude non-payers, so markets underprovide them.

📝 The classic example: National defence

Every citizen receives similar defence benefits regardless of how much they pay. It's very difficult to privately produce the optimal amount. Since governments cannot use a competitive price system to determine the correct level, they also face major difficulty producing the optimal amount. This is an example of market failure with no pure solution.

Five common types of market failure all lead to the same outcome: the free market fails to reach the social optimum.
🛠️
Solutions to Market Failures
MoE Textbook §3.1.2, p. 50

There are many potential solutions for market failures. They can take three forms:

🤝
Private Market Solutions

Tort lawsuits, private bargaining, and voluntary agreements between affected parties.

🏛️
Government-Imposed Solutions

Legislation, direct provision, taxation, subsidies, tradable permits, extension of property rights.

👥
Voluntary Collective Action

Consumer and producer cooperatives providing services that the pure market underprovides.

📝 Real-world examples

• Tech companies that receive positive externalities from tech-educated graduates can subsidize computer education through scholarships.
• Taxing tobacco increases the cost of consumption — making it more expensive for people to smoke.
• Co-ops — like utility co-ops for electric service to rural homes, or a shared refrigerated storage facility for dairy farmers.

⚠️
Government Failure — A Key Insight
MoE Textbook §3.1, p. 49

Contrary to what the name implies, market failure does not describe inherent imperfections in the market economy alone — there can be market failures in government activity too.

📌 Example: Rent-Seeking

Special interest groups can gain a large benefit by lobbying for small costs on everyone else, such as through a tariff. When each small group imposes its costs, the whole group is worse off than if no lobbying had taken place.

Important Caveats

  • Not every bad outcome from market activity counts as a market failure
  • A market failure does not imply that private market actors cannot solve the problem
  • Not all market failures have a potential solution — even with prudent regulation or extra public awareness
  • Both private and public institutions can fail — the goal is to compare alternatives

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 3.1
What you should remember
  • Market failure = inefficient distribution of goods and services in the free market
  • It occurs when the price mechanism fails to account for all costs and benefits
  • Allocative efficiency occurs when MSB = MSC
  • MSB = MPB + MEB; MSC = MPC + MEC
  • Common market failures: externalities, monopoly, information asymmetries, factor immobility, public goods
  • Solutions: private (bargaining, lawsuits), government (taxes, subsidies, legislation, public provision), voluntary collective action
  • Government failure exists too — e.g., rent-seeking by special interest groups
  • Not every bad outcome is market failure; not every market failure has a solution
