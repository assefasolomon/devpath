⚡ Externalities

When your actions affect others — but that effect isn't reflected in the price — you've created an externality. This lesson explores positive and negative externalities, why they cause market failure, and how taxes, subsidies, and private bargaining can fix them.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

When someone smokes in a room, non-smokers suffer. When a factory pollutes a river, downstream farmers lose. When you plant flowers, your neighbours enjoy the beauty. These are all externalities — effects on third parties that aren't reflected in market prices.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define externality
  • Distinguish positive from negative externalities
  • Explain why externalities cause inefficiency
  • Describe Pigouvian tax and Coase theorem solutions
  • Explain the concept of internalizing externalities
📌
What is an Externality?
MoE Textbook §3.3, p. 55
📌 Externality (MoE Textbook, p. 55)

An externality occurs when the consumption or production of goods has positive or negative effects on other people's utility where these effects are not reflected in the price.

Two Types

✅
Positive Externality

One person's consumption (or production) increases other people's utility — without them paying for it.

❌
Negative Externality

One person's consumption (or production) decreases other people's utility — without them receiving compensation.

📝 Examples

Negative: Smoking contaminates clean air. Non-smokers suffer with no part in its consumption. Downstream farmers suffer from sewage discharge — with no compensation.
Positive: Flower farming, education, street lighting, beautiful neighbourhoods, a well-maintained garden.

🔍
Characteristics of Externalities
Three key features
1️⃣
Positive or Negative

Some externalities are beneficial, others harmful. The producer/seller is the first party, the buyer is the second party, and the affected person is the third party.

2️⃣
Consumers or Producers

Both consumers (smoking) and producers (manufacturing) generate externalities. They are non-rival and non-excludable — a special kind of public good or bad.

3️⃣
Reciprocal in Nature

A firm imposes odour on residents; residents demand less odour — imposing cost on the firm. Externalities result from combined action of two or more parties, not one person alone.

📝 The reciprocity insight

This concept is related to the question of whether the injured or injurer is responsible in a car accident. Externalities are not the result of one person's action alone — they are the result of interaction.

❌
Negative Externality & Efficiency
Why markets overproduce

In the presence of a negative externality, MSC > MPC because there's an external cost of pollution. This creates a difference between MPC and P, and hence inefficiency.

Marginal Social Cost
MSC = MPC + MEC
  • MSC = Marginal Social Cost
  • MPC = Marginal Private Cost
  • MEC = Marginal External Cost
Negative externality: MSC > MPC. The market produces at e (Q), but the socially optimal level is e* (Q*). Overproduction of Q−Q* creates deadweight loss.
📝 Key insight

Profit maximization requires MPC = P (point e). The social optimum is at e* where MSC = MSB. Overproduction = Q*Q. The welfare loss (deadweight loss) equals the triangle area between e and e*.

✅
Positive Externality & Efficiency
Why markets underproduce

In the presence of a positive externality, MSB > MPB because there is external benefit. This creates a difference between MPB and MC, and hence inefficiency.

Marginal Social Benefit
MSB = MPB + MEB
  • MSB = Marginal Social Benefit
  • MPB = Marginal Private Benefit
  • MEB = Marginal External Benefit
Positive externality: MSB > MPB. The market produces at e (Q), but the socially optimal level is e* (Q*). Underproduction of Q−Q* creates deadweight loss.
📝 Key insight

Profit maximization requires MPB = MC (point e). The social optimum is at e* where MSB = MSC. Underproduction = QQ*. The welfare loss is the triangle between e and e*.

🛠️
Solutions to Externalities
MoE Textbook §3.3, p. 68

Solutions for Negative Externalities

💰
1. Pigouvian Tax (Pigou, 1920)

A per-unit tax equal to the difference between MSC and MPC at the socially optimal output. This raises the polluter's private cost to match the social cost.

🤝
2. Coase Theorem (Coase, 1960)

Private bargaining between affected parties. Given zero transaction costs, small number of parties, and well-defined property rights, the optimum outcome depends on bargaining power.

📜
3. Property Rights

Defining and enforcing property rights — the sale of pollution rights. Producers incur additional costs, so they produce less and pollute less.

📝 Pigouvian tax assumptions

• Externality = difference between MSC and MPC
• There is competitive industry
• Pollution per unit of output is constant
• External cost is borne by others

Solutions for Positive Externalities

🎁
1. Per-Unit Subsidy

A subsidy equal to the difference between SMB and PMB at the socially optimal output. If private firms are subsidized, they will supply the optimal output.

🏛️
2. Government Provision

The government itself can supply the product at a lower price (Ps) and bear the loss — as with public education or public healthcare.

📝 Internalizing externalities

All solutions to externality problems recognize the need for internalizing externalities. This means the external cost (benefit) should be included in the private cost (benefit) of individuals, so that people take external effects into account in their decisions.

Negative and positive externalities both create inefficiency — but the solutions differ.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 3.3
What you should remember
  • Externality = when production or consumption affects other people's utility without being reflected in the price
  • Positive externality = increases others' utility without payment (education, flowers)
  • Negative externality = decreases others' utility without compensation (pollution, smoking)
  • Externalities can come from consumers or producers
  • Externalities are reciprocal in nature — they result from interaction between parties
  • Negative externality: MSC > MPC → overproduction of Q−Q*
  • Positive externality: MSB > MPB → underproduction of Q−Q*
  • Pigouvian tax (Pigou, 1920) = per-unit tax equal to the external cost
  • Coase theorem (Coase, 1960) = private bargaining under zero transaction costs, small numbers, well-defined property rights
  • Subsidies fix positive externalities; taxes fix negative ones
  • Internalizing externalities = including external costs/benefits in private costs/benefits
