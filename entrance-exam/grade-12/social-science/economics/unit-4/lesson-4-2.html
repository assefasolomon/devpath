🏛️ Fiscal Policy

When the economy is in recession, the government can spend more and tax less to boost demand. When inflation is high, it can spend less and tax more to cool the economy. This lesson explores the two faces of fiscal policy — expansionary and contractionary.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Fiscal policy is the government's most direct tool for managing the economy. When the private sector cuts back, the government can step in. When spending is too high, the government can pull back. This lesson shows you exactly how.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define fiscal policy
  • List the two key tools of fiscal policy
  • Explain the difference between expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy
  • Analyse the AD-AS effects of each type
  • Give real-world examples of both
📌
What is Fiscal Policy?
MoE Textbook §4.2, p. 73
📌 Fiscal Policy (MoE Textbook, p. 73)

Fiscal policy is the expenditure and revenue (tax) policy of the government to achieve desired objectives. It can be expansionary or contractionary depending on prevailing economic conditions.

📝 Two tools, two directions

Fiscal policy works through government spending (G) and taxation (T). By adjusting both, the government can either boost demand (expansionary) or reduce it (contractionary).

🛠️
Two Key Tools of Fiscal Policy
MoE Textbook §4.2.1, p. 74
💰
1. Taxation

Funds in the form of direct and indirect taxes, capital gains, etc. Taxes affect the consumer's income — and changes in consumption lead to changes in real GDP.

💳
2. Government Spending

Welfare programmes, government salaries, subsidies, infrastructure, etc. Government spending has the power to raise or lower real GDP.

📝 Key formula

Recall AD = C + I + G + (X − M). Changes in G directly shift AD. Changes in taxes affect C (disposable income), which also shifts AD.

🧩
Components of Government Spending & Taxes
The detailed breakdown

Four Components of Government Spending

#ComponentDescription
1Government Spending (G)Sum of government expenditures on final goods and services: salaries of public servants, weapons for the military, investment expenditure
2Transfer PaymentsDirect payments to individuals — unemployment insurance, social security, Medicare, welfare. No goods/services in return
3Grants in AidFederal assistance to state and local governments
4Net Interest PaymentsInterest payments made to holders of government debt, less interest received

Four Major Components of Tax Revenue

#ComponentDescription
1Personal TaxesIncome taxes and property taxes — major sources of total government revenue
2Social Insurance ContributionsPrimarily social security taxes — assessed as a fixed percentage of a worker's wages
3Taxes on Production & ImportsPrimarily sales taxes; also include tariffs on imported goods
4Corporate TaxesPrimary taxes on the profits of businesses

I. Expansionary Fiscal Policy
MoE Textbook §4.2.2, p. 75

When the economy faces deficient demand — aggregate demand falling short of output at full employment — there is a depression marked by overproduction, rising unemployment, and falling prices and incomes. To boost demand, the government adopts expansionary fiscal policy.

Two Major Instruments

🏗️
Increase Expenditure

The government should make large investments in public works — roads, bridges, buildings, railway lines, canals. Also provide free education and medical facilities. The aim: pump more money into the system to boost demand.

📉
Reduce Tax Rate

Taxes on personal incomes and expenditures should be reduced. If possible, taxes on lower income groups should be abolished to increase their disposable income. Expand subsidies, pensions, unemployment allowances, and interest-free loans.

Expansionary fiscal policy: AD₀ → AD₁ shifts the economy from a recession (Y₀) toward potential GDP (Y₁).
📝 Key insight

At the initial equilibrium (E₀), the economy is producing below its potential (Y₀ < Ypotential). Expansionary fiscal policy — through tax cuts or increased government spending — shifts AD to the right. The new equilibrium (E₁) is closer to full employment.

📉
II. Contractionary Fiscal Policy
MoE Textbook §4.2.2, p. 76

When the economy's aggregate demand is more than full employment, demand is said to be an excess demand. This gap results in an inflationary situation. To control excess demand and reduce the pressure of high inflation, the government adopts contractionary fiscal policy.

Two Major Instruments

✂️
Reduce Expenditure

Curtail expenditures on public works — roads, buildings, rural electrification, irrigation. This reduces people's money income and thus their demand for goods and services. It also reduces the budget deficit.

📈
Increase Taxes

Raise rates of all taxes, especially taxes on rich people. Taxation withdraws purchasing power from taxpayers, reducing effective demand. Care must be taken that measures are disinflationary without harming production or savings.

Contractionary fiscal policy: AD₀ → AD₁ shifts the economy from above-potential output back toward potential GDP, easing inflation.
📝 Key insight

A very large budget deficit pushes aggregate demand beyond potential GDP, causing inflation. In this situation, contractionary fiscal policy — spending cuts or tax increases — reduces the upward pressure on the price level.

📋
Expansionary vs Contractionary
Side-by-side comparison
Aspect Expansionary Fiscal Policy Contractionary Fiscal Policy
Applied when Recession / deficient demand Inflation / excess demand
Government spending Increase (↑G) Reduce (↓G)
Taxes Reduce (↓T) Increase (↑T)
AD shift Right → Left ←
Effect on real GDP Increase Decrease
Effect on price level Increase Decrease
Budget impact Deficit expands Deficit shrinks
Choose the right tool for the problem. Recession → expansionary. Inflation → contractionary.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 4.2
What you should remember
  • Fiscal policy = government's expenditure and revenue (tax) policy
  • Two tools: taxation and government spending
  • Four components of government spending: G, transfer payments, grants in aid, net interest payments
  • Four tax revenue components: personal taxes, social insurance, production/import taxes, corporate taxes
  • Expansionary fiscal policy: ↑G + ↓T → AD shifts right → boosts output during recession
  • Contractionary fiscal policy: ↓G + ↑T → AD shifts left → controls inflation
  • Expansionary fiscal policy is used during recession / deficient demand
  • Contractionary fiscal policy is used during inflation / excess demand
  • Budget impact: expansionary widens the deficit; contractionary shrinks it
