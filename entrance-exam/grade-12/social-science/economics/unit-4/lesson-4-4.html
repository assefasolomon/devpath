💼 Income Policy & Wages

Income policy uses wage and price controls to fight inflation — from voluntary guidelines to mandatory freezes. This lesson covers minimum wages, price ceilings, price floors, and how they affect market outcomes.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

When inflation is high, the government can adopt an income policy — using wage and price controls to stabilize the price level. This lesson shows how markets determine wages in the absence of controls, and how price ceilings and price floors interfere with that process.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define income policy
  • Explain how wages are determined in a free market
  • Describe minimum wage as a price floor
  • Explain what a price ceiling is and why it causes shortages
  • Explain what a price floor is and why it causes surpluses
📌
What is Income Policy?
MoE Textbook §4.4, p. 84
📌 Income Policy (MoE Textbook, p. 84)

Income policies are economy-wide wage and price controls, most commonly instituted as a response to inflation, and usually seeking to establish wages and prices below free market level. They vary from "voluntary" wage and price guidelines to mandatory controls such as price/wage freezes.

Income policy is the suitable complement for expansionary monetary and fiscal policies, in particular under conditions that reduce the space for further macroeconomic expansion.

📝 Why income policy matters

In an inflationary environment, it's possible to stabilize the price level through the instruments of income policy. Governments take this step if restrictive fiscal and monetary policies fail to reduce the increasing price level.

Two Main Tools

💰
Minimum Wages

A legally mandated price floor on hourly wages — the minimum amount an employer must pay.

📊
Price Controls

Price ceilings (maximum) and price floors (minimum) — set by law to keep prices from rising or falling too far.

⚖️
Wage Determination in a Free Market
MoE Textbook §4.4, p. 84

Just as in any market, the price of labour — the wage rate — is determined by the intersection of supply and demand. When the supply of labour increases, the equilibrium price falls; when the demand for labour increases, the equilibrium price rises.

Characteristics of a Perfectly Competitive Labour Market

  • A large number of firms competing to hire a specific type of labour to fill identical jobs
  • Numerous qualified people with identical skills who independently supply their labour services
  • "Wage taking" behaviour — neither workers nor firms exert control over the market wage
  • Perfect, costless information and labour mobility

Market labour demand is a downward-sloping curve (price-adjusted), while market labour supply generally slopes upward to the right — indicating that collectively workers will offer more labour hours at higher relative wage rates.

In a free labour market, the equilibrium wage (W₀) and employment level (Q₀) are determined where labour supply meets labour demand.
💰
Minimum Wages
A legally mandated price floor
📌 Minimum Wage

A minimum wage is the lowest wage per hour that a worker may be paid as mandated by federal law. In simple words, it is a legally mandated price floor on hourly wages, below which workers may not be offered or accept a job.

Minimum wages have been defined as the minimum amount of remuneration that an employer is required to pay to wage earners for work performed during a given period, which cannot be reduced by collective agreement or individual contract.

Purpose of Minimum Wages

  • Protect workers from unduly low pay
  • Ensure a just and equitable share of the fruits of progress to all
  • Guarantee a minimum living wage to all who need protection
  • Reduce poverty and inequality — including between men and women — by promoting the right to equal remuneration for work of equal value
📝 Minimum wage = price floor

A minimum wage works like a price floor in the labour market. When set above the free-market equilibrium wage, it can create a surplus of labour (i.e., unemployment). We'll see the exact diagram in the "Price Floor" section below.

🔻
Price Ceiling
Maximum legal price → causes shortage
📌 Price Ceiling

A price ceiling is the mandated maximum amount that a seller is allowed to charge for a product or service. Usually set by law, price ceilings are typically applied to staples such as food and energy products when such goods become unaffordable to regular consumers. It is a maximum legal price below the equilibrium price.

A price ceiling provides perverse incentives (unintended consequences), causing a shortage.

Price ceiling ($20) below equilibrium ($40) creates a shortage: quantity demanded (125) exceeds quantity supplied (75).
🔺
Price Floor
Minimum legal price → causes surplus
📌 Price Floor

A price floor is a government- or group-imposed price control or limit on how low a price can be charged for a product, good, commodity, or service. A price floor must be higher than the equilibrium price in order to be effective.

Examples

  • Minimum wage laws — government sets the minimum hourly rate that can be paid for labour
  • Agricultural price floors — many agricultural goods have price floors imposed by the government
  • Resale price maintenance — a manufacturer and its distributors agree that the distributors will sell the manufacturer's product at or above a price floor

Effects of a Price Floor Above Equilibrium

  • Consumers pay a higher price for the same product
  • They reduce purchases, switch to substitutes (e.g., from butter to margarine), or drop out of the market entirely
  • Suppliers get a guaranteed higher price — but with fewer willing buyers
  • Result: excess supply (surplus)
  • Transfers some consumer surplus to producer surplus
  • Creates a deadweight loss
  • May lead to market failure if resources are not allocated efficiently
Price floor ($4) above equilibrium ($3) creates a surplus: quantity supplied (120) exceeds quantity demanded (80).
📋
Price Ceiling vs Price Floor
Key differences
Aspect Price Ceiling Price Floor
Definition Maximum legal price Minimum legal price
Set where? Below equilibrium price Above equilibrium price
Result Shortage (Qd > Qs) Surplus (Qs > Qd)
Purpose Protect consumers from high prices Protect producers/workers from low prices
Example Rent control, food price caps Minimum wage, agricultural price supports
Effect Perverse incentives — may cause black markets Transfers consumer surplus to producers; creates deadweight loss
Price ceilings protect consumers (but cause shortages). Price floors protect producers (but cause surpluses).

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 4.4
What you should remember
  • Income policy = economy-wide wage and price controls, usually to fight inflation
  • Income policy varies from voluntary guidelines to mandatory wage/price freezes
  • In a free labour market, the wage rate is determined where labour supply meets labour demand
  • Perfectly competitive labour market: many firms, many workers, wage-taking, perfect info
  • Minimum wage = legally mandated price floor on hourly wages
  • Purpose of minimum wage: protect workers from unduly low pay
  • Price ceiling = maximum legal price, set below equilibrium → causes shortage
  • Price floor = minimum legal price, set above equilibrium → causes surplus
  • Price floor effects: higher consumer prices, fewer willing buyers, excess supply, deadweight loss
  • Examples: rent control (ceiling), minimum wage (floor), agricultural price supports (floor)
