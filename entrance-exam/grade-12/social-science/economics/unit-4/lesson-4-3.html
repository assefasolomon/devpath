💵 Monetary Policy

Fiscal policy works through government spending and taxes. Monetary policy works through the money supply and interest rates — controlled by the National Bank of Ethiopia. This lesson shows you the three tools and how they fight recessions and inflation.

📖 Reading: ~25 min
🎯 Quiz: 5 questions
🎥 Videos: 4 resources
🎯
Introduction
What you will learn

Monetary policy is the central bank's tool for managing the economy. Unlike fiscal policy (which is controlled by the government), monetary policy is controlled by the National Bank — an independent agency that oversees the banking system.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define monetary policy
  • Describe the role of the National Bank
  • List the three tools of monetary policy
  • Explain expansionary vs contractionary monetary policy
  • Trace the policy pathways from money supply to real GDP
📌
What is Monetary Policy?
MoE Textbook §4.3, p. 78
📌 Monetary Policy (MoE Textbook, p. 78)

Monetary policy is the process of drafting, announcing, and implementing the plan of actions taken by the National Bank (or other monetary authority) of a country that controls the quantity of money in an economy and the channels by which new money is supplied.

Monetary policy consists of the management of money supply and interest rates, aimed at meeting macroeconomic objectives such as controlling inflation, consumption, growth, and liquidity.

📝 What it manages

• Interest rates — by modifying them
• Money supply — by buying/selling government bonds
• Foreign exchange rates — by regulating them
• Bank reserves — by changing required reserve amounts

🏦
The National Bank
Who conducts monetary policy?
📌 National Bank

The National Bank is a government agency that oversees the banking system and is responsible for the conduct of monetary policy. This bank takes different names in different countries:

CountryCentral Bank
EthiopiaNational Bank of Ethiopia (NBE)
United StatesFederal Reserve System (the Fed)
United KingdomBank of England
European UnionEuropean Central Bank (ECB)
📝 How NBE formulates policy

The monetary authority looks at macroeconomic numbers such as GDP and inflation, industry-specific growth rates, geopolitical developments in international markets (oil embargos, trade tariffs), and inputs from the government and other credible sources.

🛠️
Three Tools of Monetary Policy
MoE Textbook §4.3.1, p. 79

The National Bank influences the money supply indirectly by changing the monetary base or the reserve-deposit ratio. To do this, it has three instruments at its disposal:

📊
1. Open Market Operations (OMO)

The purchase and sale of government bonds by the National Bank. Buying bonds → money supply ↑. Selling bonds → money supply ↓.

🏦
2. Discount Rate (DR)

The interest rate the National Bank charges banks to borrow funds from it. Set by the NBE's board of governors. In Ethiopia, the discount window facility started in March 2001.

📋
3. Required Reserve Ratio (RRR)

Funds banks must retain as a proportion of customer deposits. Lowering RRR → more loans possible. Raising RRR → fewer loans.

📝 NBE example

The National Bank of Ethiopia cut the minimum deposit reserve from 15% to 10% in January 2012, and cut the liquid assets-to-deposits ratio to 20% from 25%. The move aimed to encourage banks to lend money, particularly to the export sector.

📈
Expansionary Monetary Policy
MoE Textbook §4.3.2, p. 80

If a country is facing high unemployment during a slowdown or recession, the monetary authority can opt for an expansionary policy — which aims at increasing economic growth and expanding economic activity.

Major Instruments

⬇️
Reduce Discount Rate

Enables commercial banks to take more loans from NBE — and in turn give more loans to producers at lower interest rates.

💰
Buy Securities (OMO)

The central bank buys government bonds and securities from commercial banks — paying in cash to increase their cash stock and lending capacity.

⬇️
Reduce RRR

Lowering the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) increases commercial banks' capacity to give credit.

📝 Example from the textbook

The economy is in recession with output below potential GDP. The original equilibrium (E₀) occurs at an output level of 600. An expansionary monetary policy will reduce interest rates and stimulate investment and consumption spending, shifting AD₀ to AD₁ — so the new equilibrium (E₁) occurs at the potential GDP level of 700.

📉
Contractionary Monetary Policy
MoE Textbook §4.3.2, p. 81

Increased money supply can lead to higher inflation, raising the cost of living and cost of doing business. Contractionary monetary policy — increasing interest rates and slowing the growth of the money supply — aims to bring inflation down. This can slow economic growth and increase unemployment, but it is often necessary to cool down an overheated economy.

Major Instruments

⬆️
Increase Discount Rate

Raises the cost of borrowing — discourages commercial banks from borrowing from the central bank. Forces them to raise lending rates, making credit costlier.

💵
Sell Securities (OMO)

The central bank sells government securities to commercial banks — absorbing liquidity from the system. Falls in investment and aggregate demand follow.

⬆️
Increase RRR

Raising the required reserve ratio curtails the lending capacity of commercial banks.

📝 Example from the textbook

The economy is producing at a quantity above its potential GDP. The original equilibrium (E₀) occurs at an output of 750. A contractionary monetary policy will raise the interest rate, discouraging borrowing for investment and consumption, and shift AD₀ left to AD₁ — so the new equilibrium (E₁) occurs at the potential GDP level of 700.

🔗
The Pathways of Monetary Policy
Chain of effects

The chain of effects that connects loose and tight monetary policy to changes in output and the price level.

The chain: change in money supply (M) → change in interest rate (r) → change in investment (I) and consumption (C) → change in price level (P) and real GDP (Y).

Summary Pathways

Policy Pathway Final Effect
Expansionary ↑M → ↓r → ↑I, ↑C → AD shifts right Higher P, higher real GDP (short run)
Contractionary ↓M → ↑r → ↓I, ↓C → AD shifts left Lower P, lower real GDP (short run)
📝 Countercyclical policy

Monetary policy should be countercyclical: loosen when a recession has caused unemployment, tighten when inflation threatens. But overreaction is a danger — going too far in either direction can cause the opposite problem.

Summary
Key takeaways from Lesson 4.3
What you should remember
  • Monetary policy = management of money supply and interest rates by the National Bank
  • In Ethiopia, the National Bank of Ethiopia (NBE) conducts monetary policy
  • Three tools: Open Market Operations, Discount Rate, Required Reserve Ratio
  • OMO buying → money supply ↑; OMO selling → money supply ↓
  • Discount rate ↓ → borrowing cheaper; Discount rate ↑ → borrowing costlier
  • RRR ↓ → more lending possible; RRR ↑ → less lending
  • Expansionary monetary policy: ↑M → ↓r → ↑I, ↑C → AD shifts right → ↑P, ↑Y
  • Contractionary monetary policy: ↓M → ↑r → ↓I, ↓C → AD shifts left → ↓P, ↓Y
  • Used when: expansionary for recession, contractionary for inflation
  • Should be countercyclical — but avoid overreaction
